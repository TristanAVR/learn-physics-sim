Coupling Two Solvers

Two simulations that each work perfectly, and the one line of glue that makes them explode together.

Sooner or later you will want two different solvers in one scene. A stiff rod, which converges only with a direct solver or hundreds of iterations, presses into a soft mattress that a handful of cheap Gauss-Seidel sweeps handles well. A cable lies on a trampoline. A rigid tool squeezes a jelly. Each part has its own best method, and often its own code, written by someone else. The only thing they share is the place where they touch.

Gluing them together looks trivial: let the rod push on the mattress, let the mattress push back, exchange the numbers once per step. Try it, and with the wrong pair of stiffnesses the contact starts to chatter, then to bang, then the scene leaves the screen. Nothing is wrong with either solver. The glue is a numerical method of its own, with its own stability limit, and this chapter derives it.

By the end you will be able to predict whether a coupling scheme converges from one ratio of stiffnesses, fix it by choosing which side receives which quantity, by relaxing, or (best) by telling the stiff solver how much the soft side gives, and you will know why that last trick needs the row sums of a matrix rather than its diagonal once several contacts share the soft side.

Monolithic or partitioned

There are two ways to simulate two bodies that touch. The monolithic way puts every equation of both bodies and the contact into one big system and solves it with one solver. That is what the constraint chapters did: a Gauss-Seidel loop does not care whether a constraint belongs to the rod, the mattress or the contact between them. It is the most robust choice, and the reference answer for everything in this chapter.

The partitioned way keeps a separate solver per body and lets them talk only through interface values: the contact force, and the positions of the touching points. You choose it when the subsystems want different treatment:

  • Different stiffness. A near-rigid rod needs many iterations, or a direct solve (the solvers chapter showed why Gauss-Seidel crawls on stiff chains). A soft membrane converges in a few sweeps. In one monolithic loop, every soft constraint pays for the rod's iteration count.
  • Different structure. A rod is a chain: its matrix is banded and a tridiagonal direct solve costs almost nothing. A membrane is a sheet: its matrix is sparse but wide, and an iterative method suits it.
  • Different code. The cloth solver, the fluid solver and the rigid-body engine are often separate libraries with their own time steps. The interface is all you can touch.

Partitioning buys flexibility and costs a new question: the two solvers must agree on the interface, and the agreement is reached by an iteration that may or may not converge. Let's look at the smallest example where it fails.

The toy: two springs in series

A hand pushes the top of a "rod" spring of stiffness $k_1$ down by a distance $d$. The rod's tip sits on a "membrane" spring of stiffness $k_2$ standing on the ground. Everything is static, and the contact is glued, so the algebra stays linear. The interface force $f$ compresses both springs. The rod is squeezed by $d - y$, where $y$ is how far the membrane surface has sunk, and the membrane is squeezed by $y$:

$$f = k_1 (d - y), \qquad f = k_2\, y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad f^* = \frac{k_1 k_2}{k_1 + k_2}\, d.$$

That is the monolithic answer: two springs in series. Now split it. Solver 1 owns the rod and solver 2 owns the membrane. The most natural division of labour is the one a contact suggests: the rod is pushed down onto the membrane, so the rod solver treats the membrane surface as a wall at its current position $y$ and computes how hard it pushes, and the membrane solver receives that force and computes how far it sinks.

// Solver 1, the rod: given where the membrane surface is, how hard do I push?
function rodGivenPosition(d, y) { return k1 * (d - y); }
// Solver 2, the membrane: given a force, how far do I sink?
function membraneGivenForce(f) { return f / k2; }

Run the toy below. The hand moves slowly in and out, and the plot shows the interface force against the exact $f^*$. With the default stiffnesses ($k_1 = 50$ N/m, $k_2 = 100$ N/m) the red curve sits on the grey one. Now drag $k_1$ above $k_2$.

Explicit lag, and why it diverges

The scheme in the demo is the simplest partitioned scheme there is. Each step, the rod solver uses the membrane position from the end of the previous step, then the membrane solver uses the force the rod just computed:

$$f_{n+1} = k_1 (d - y_n), \qquad y_{n+1} = \frac{f_{n+1}}{k_2}.$$

This is called explicit coupling or explicit lag, because one of the two solvers always works with a value that is one step out of date. (Running both solvers in parallel, each with the other's old value, is the same idea and no better.) To see whether it settles on $f^*$, follow the error $e_n = f_n - f^*$. Substitute $y_n = f_n / k_2$ into the first equation and subtract $f^* = k_1(d - f^*/k_2)$:

$$e_{n+1} = -\frac{k_1}{k_2}\, e_n.$$

Every exchange multiplies the error by $-k_1/k_2$. If the rod is softer than the membrane, the error shrinks geometrically, flipping sign each step. If the rod is stiffer, it grows geometrically: with $k_1 = 150$ N/m and $k_2 = 100$ N/m, every step multiplies the error by $-1.5$, and after one second at 60 steps per second by $1.5^{60} \approx 3.7 \times 10^{10}$. The demo prints the predicted and the measured factor per pass, and they agree to the last digit, because the toy is exactly linear.

The physical picture is worth keeping. The rod solver believes the membrane is a rigid wall at its old position. A stiff rod pressed into a rigid wall produces a huge force. The membrane receives that huge force and sinks far too much. Next step the rod sees the membrane far below, barely touches it, and produces almost no force, so the membrane springs back up past where it should be. That is chatter, and when $k_1 > k_2$ each swing is bigger than the last.

Swapping the roles

Now give each solver the other job: the rod receives the force and works out where its tip ends up, and the membrane receives that position and works out the force needed to hold its surface there:

$$x_{n+1} = d - \frac{f_n}{k_1}, \qquad f_{n+1} = k_2\, x_{n+1} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e_{n+1} = -\frac{k_2}{k_1}\, e_n.$$

The factor is inverted. With a rod ten times stiffer than the membrane, the error now shrinks tenfold per exchange instead of growing tenfold. Choose swapped roles in the toy and push $k_1$ to its maximum to see it.

The rule of thumb: the factor per exchange is the compliance of the side that receives the force divided by the compliance of the side that receives the position. So give the force to the stiff side (it barely moves under any force) and the position to the soft side (it barely pushes back against any position). The natural contact formulation, where the stiff body treats the soft one as a wall, is exactly the wrong way round.

Swapping is not always possible. In a contact, the body that detects the penetration usually resolves it, and the soft side may be a solver you cannot change. And when inertia enters, the dynamic compliances replace the static ones, which can flip the ratio again. That case has a famous name in fluid-structure interaction: the added-mass instability, where a light structure coupled explicitly to a heavy fluid diverges no matter how small the time step, because the fluid's effective mass, not its stiffness, sets the ratio. You will meet a version of it in the main demo.

Fixed-point iteration between solvers

If one exchange per step is not enough, do several. Within one step, alternate the two solvers with the same $d$ until the force stops changing:

for (let k = 0; k < passes; k++) {
  f = rodGivenPosition(d, y);     // solver 1 with the latest position
  y = membraneGivenForce(f);      // solver 2 with the latest force
}

This is a fixed-point iteration, also called block Gauss-Seidel: each solver is one "block", and each block uses the newest values of the other. It is the coupling equivalent of the Gauss-Seidel iteration from the solvers chapter, with the same kind of convergence factor. Here the error after each pass is multiplied by the same $-k_1/k_2$ as before, so more passes help only when the scheme already converges. When it diverges, more passes diverge faster. Fixed-point iteration does not fix a bad ratio; it only removes the time lag.

It does give you one measurable quantity: the change in the interface force from one pass to the next. If that change does not shrink, the coupling has not converged, whatever the picture looks like. Every partitioned code should print it.

Under-relaxation

The classic fix is to not trust the new force completely. Blend it with the old one, using a relaxation factor $\theta$ between 0 and 1:

$$f^{k+1} = (1 - \theta)\, f^k + \theta\, k_1 (d - y^k).$$

Write $r = k_1/k_2$. The error now evolves as $e^{k+1} = \big(1 - \theta(1 + r)\big)\, e^k$, so the iteration converges when

$$\big|1 - \theta (1 + r)\big| < 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 0 < \theta < \frac{2}{1 + r}.$$

Any ratio can be tamed by a small enough $\theta$. With $r = 10$ you need $\theta < 2/11 \approx 0.18$, and the best choice, $\theta = 1/(1 + r) \approx 0.091$, makes the factor exactly zero: the iteration lands on $f^*$ in one pass. But look at what that best choice needs: the stiffness ratio. If you knew how soft the other side is, you could do better than blending. That is the next idea. (Practical codes estimate $\theta$ on the fly from the last two corrections, a method called Aitken relaxation; it is a secant method on the interface.)

The compliance of the soft side

The rod solver failed because it treated the membrane as a rigid wall. The fix is to tell it the truth: the wall gives. For a linear soft body, how much it gives is captured by one matrix. Push node $j$ with a unit force, hold everything else free, and measure how far every node $i$ moves. That displacement is $W_{ij}$, the compliance of the soft body (also called its Green's function or influence matrix), in metres per newton. A force pattern $f$ moves the nodes by

$$u = W f, \qquad W = K^{-1},$$

where $K$ is the soft body's stiffness matrix. You never invert $K$. You need $W$ only at the contact nodes, and each column is one solve with a unit force on the right-hand side, which a tridiagonal or sparse solver does in microseconds. $W$ is symmetric (the displacement at $i$ from a push at $j$ equals the displacement at $j$ from a push at $i$, a fact engineers call Maxwell-Betti reciprocity) and positive definite.

Inside a time step, the right $K$ is the one the soft solver actually solves. For implicit Euler (or converged XPBD) with node mass $m$ that is $K + M/h^2$: inertia makes a body stiffer over a short step, because it cannot get out of the way in time. In the main demo below, each membrane node has mass 0.01 kg, a mattress spring of 20 N/m to its rest position and a cover spring of 1000 N/m to each neighbour, and $h = 1/120$ s, so the diagonal of the step matrix is $144 + 20 + 2\times 1000 = 2164$ N/m. Solving for the middle column gives a one-step compliance of 1.21 mm/N under the foot, against a static 3.50 mm/N.

// One column of W: push contact node k with 1 N, solve, read the displacement at every contact node.
for (let k = 0; k < nC; k++) {
  const rhs = new Float64Array(N - 2);
  rhs[contacts[k] - 1] = 1;
  const u = solveTridiag(memDiag, kc, rhs);       // the membrane's own step matrix
  for (let j = 0; j < nC; j++) W[j][k] = u[contacts[j] - 1];
}

Compliance-augmented contact

Give the rod solver a model of the membrane: if the membrane currently feels a force $f^k$ and sits at $y^k$, then under a different force $f$ it will sit at about

$$y(f) \approx y^k + \hat W\,(f - f^k),$$

where $\hat W$ is the rod solver's estimate of the membrane's compliance. The rod solver now solves its contact against this moving wall instead of a fixed one. In the toy, $f = k_1\big(d - y^k - \hat W (f - f^k)\big)$ has the solution $f^{k+1} = k_1 (d - y^k + \hat W f^k) / (1 + k_1 \hat W)$, and the error factor becomes

$$\frac{e^{k+1}}{e^k} = \frac{k_1\,(\hat W - W)}{1 + k_1 \hat W}, \qquad W = \frac{1}{k_2}.$$

Three facts follow. With $\hat W = W$ the factor is zero: one pass gives the exact answer, whatever the stiffness ratio. With $\hat W = 0$ it is $-k_1/k_2$, the explicit lag we started from. And overestimating is safe: for any $\hat W \ge W$ the factor lies between 0 and 1, so the iteration converges monotonically, only more slowly. Underestimating is not: the factor reaches $-1$ when $\hat W = (W - 1/k_1)/2$. With $k_1 = 1000$ N/m and $k_2 = 100$ N/m that is 45% of the true compliance; at 40% the toy diverges with a factor of $-1.2$, at 50% it converges with $-0.83$. Try the slider compliance estimate / true.

In a position-based rod solver, "solve the contact against a wall that gives by $D$ metres per newton" is one line you already know: an XPBD constraint with compliance $\alpha = D$. The contact constraint $C = y_\text{foot} - \text{level}$ uses as its level the membrane position with the current force removed, $y^k + D f^k$, and with compliance $\alpha$ XPBD converges to $C = -\alpha f$, which is exactly the foot sitting on the predicted membrane surface:

// Inside the rod's Gauss-Seidel loop, for each contact j (foot above the membrane node c):
const level = memY[c] + D[j] * fNow[j];      // where the membrane would sit with no contact force
const at = D[j] / (h * h);                    // XPBD time-scaled compliance, alpha = D[j]
const C = yFoot - level;
let dl = (-C - at * lam[j]) / (wFoot + at);
if (lam[j] + dl < 0) dl = -lam[j];           // contacts only push
lam[j] += dl;
yFoot += wFoot * dl;
// after the loop: f[j] = lam[j] / (h * h), which the membrane solver then receives

The compliance changes the path, not the destination. When the iteration converges, the force stops changing, so $f = f^k$ and the correction $D(f - f^k)$ vanishes. The final contact condition is then exactly "the foot sits on the membrane", whatever value of $D$ you used. A wrong $D$ costs iterations or stability, never accuracy. This is what makes the trick so useful: you can use a crude, cheap, conservative estimate of the soft side's compliance.

Several contacts: the off-diagonal terms

A flat foot touches the membrane at several nodes, and the membrane does not respond to each contact separately. Push under one node and its neighbours sink too: $W$ has large off-diagonal entries. If each contact uses only its own diagonal entry, $D_{jj} = W_{jj}$, it ignores the sinking caused by its neighbours' forces, which is exactly the mistake of the rigid wall, made a little smaller.

Make it precise. Take the rod as rigid (the foot is held at a fixed depth) and let $d$ be the vector of penetrations of the foot into the unloaded membrane. The exact contact forces solve $W f = d$. One pass of compliance-augmented coupling computes, for each contact,

$$f^{k+1} = f^k + D^{-1}\,(d - W f^k) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad e^{k+1} = \big(I - D^{-1} W\big)\, e^k.$$

With $D$ the diagonal of $W$, this is Jacobi iteration on $W$, and it converges if and only if the spectral radius $\rho(I - D^{-1}W)$, the largest eigenvalue magnitude, is below 1. Here are the numbers for the demo's membrane, at three neighbouring nodes under the foot (one-step compliances, mm/N):

$$W = \begin{pmatrix} 1.21 & 0.81 & 0.54 \\ 0.81 & 1.21 & 0.81 \\ 0.54 & 0.81 & 1.21 \end{pmatrix}.$$

Two contacts (the top-left 2×2 block). With the diagonal, $I - D^{-1}W$ has eigenvalues $\pm 0.81/1.21$, so $\rho = 0.669$: it converges, with the error flipping sign every pass. For any two contacts the diagonal is safe, because $\rho = |W_{12}|/\sqrt{W_{11}W_{22}} < 1$ follows from $W$ being positive definite. It approaches 1 as the two contacts approach the same node.

Three contacts. The eigenvalues of $W$ are 2.66, 0.67 and 0.30 mm/N. The largest belongs to the mode where all three contacts push down together, and the membrane gives much more under that combined push than any single diagonal entry admits: $1 - 2.66/1.21 = -1.20$. So $\rho = 1.20$ and the diagonal version diverges, even though every contact on its own is modelled correctly.

The repair is to put the row sum on the diagonal instead, the Gershgorin bound:

$$D_{jj} = \sum_k |W_{jk}|.$$

For the three contacts that gives $D = \operatorname{diag}(2.56, 2.83, 2.56)$ mm/N and $\rho = 0.889$. It converges. And it always will, for any number of contacts and any positive definite $W$. The matrix $D - W$ has non-negative diagonal entries $\sum_{k \ne j}|W_{jk}|$ that are at least the sum of the magnitudes of its off-diagonal entries in that row, so by the Gershgorin circle theorem all its eigenvalues are non-negative: $D \succeq W$. If $e$ is an eigenvector of $D^{-1}W$ with eigenvalue $\mu$, then $We = \mu De$ and $\mu = e^\top W e / e^\top D e$, which lies in $(0, 1]$ because $0 < e^\top W e \le e^\top D e$. So the eigenvalues of $I - D^{-1}W$ lie in $[0, 1)$. No negative eigenvalues means no flip-flop, and none at or above 1 means no divergence. If the rod is not rigid, its own compliance $S$ joins $D$ in the denominator, and the factor only gets smaller.

The diagonal is not just slow, it is wrong-way-round in disguise. The general condition for this kind of splitting to converge is that $2D - W$ be positive definite. For the three-contact example, $2D - W$ with the diagonal has an eigenvalue of $-0.24$; with the row sums its smallest is $+2.64$. With two contacts the diagonal satisfies the condition automatically, which is why the bug often hides until a third contact appears. The price of safety is speed: for two contacts the row sum gives $\rho = 2 \times 0.81 / (1.21 + 0.81) = 0.802$, slower than the diagonal's 0.669.

The main demo

Now the real thing. A stiff rod (nine particles, links of $10^5$ N/m, solved by XPBD with 300 Gauss-Seidel iterations per pass, standing in for a direct solver) is lowered by a hand onto a soft membrane (41 particles, solved by its own 30 Gauss-Seidel sweeps of its implicit-Euler system). Both bodies move only vertically, so the physics is the toy's, with a real membrane. The two solvers exchange only the contact forces and the membrane's positions. The faint dashed line and the grey plot curve are a monolithic copy of the same scene running alongside, one solver for everything. After the foot settles, the monolithic force matches the static prediction $F = d / (S + W_\text{foot})$ for a push of $d = 4$ cm, where $S$ is the rod's static compliance and $W_\text{foot} = 1/(\mathbf 1^\top W^{-1} \mathbf 1)$ the static compliance the membrane presents to a rigid flat foot: 12.72 N for three contacts.

Things to try:

  1. Explicit lag and fixed-point. With one contact the readout predicts a factor of about 18 per pass: the membrane's one-step compliance under the foot (1.21 mm/N) over the rod foot's (0.068 mm/N). Fixed-point iteration flips between "huge force" and "no force" every pass. With an odd number of passes each step ends on a huge kick and the scene explodes; with an even number it ends on "no force" and the foot sinks silently through the membrane. Both are the same divergence.
  2. Soften the rod to 100 N/m with explicit lag and one contact. The factor drops to about 0.98 and never much lower: a 50 g foot cannot move more than $h^2/m = 1.39$ mm per newton in one step, however soft the rod. That is the added-mass effect: the inertia of the light side, not its stiffness, keeps the ratio near 1.
  3. Relaxed fixed-point with one contact. With $\theta = 0.05$ the force settles within 0.3% of the reference (the remaining gap is the rod's own unconverged iterations). At $\theta = 0.1$ it still settles; at 0.12 it chatters, as $\theta < 2/(1 + r) = 0.106$ predicts.
  4. Swapped roles. The membrane solver handles the contact (the foot is a rigid ceiling for it) and the rod receives the force. It converges on the reference, as the rule of thumb says.
  5. Compliance: diagonal with 1 or 2 contacts converges; with 3 or 5 it does not. The readout includes the rod's own small give, so it predicts 1.02 for three contacts rather than the rigid 1.20, and 1.53 for five. Watch the interface change readout: it never decays. With three contacts and five passes the total force jumps between about 9 and 18 N. Set an even number of passes and it settles at 11.46 N instead of 12.72 N, with the middle contact carrying nothing: a wrong answer that looks calm. Only the interface change gives it away.
  6. Compliance: Gershgorin converges on the monolithic answer for every foot width. Now drop the membrane sweeps from 30 to 8 and try the diagonal again: it converges. Too few sweeps leave the membrane under-converged, so it responds to each new force only partly, which acts as hidden under-relaxation. Improve the membrane solver and the coupling breaks: a real bug pattern.

Hint: which force goes into the level?

fNow[j] is the force the membrane is currently feeling, the one that put it at memY. Adding D[j] * fNow[j] undoes its effect, giving the level the membrane would have with no contact force. XPBD then pulls the foot down by $\alpha f$ below that level, which is where the membrane will be under the new force.

Solution

Compare with solution.js:

function contactCompliance(W, modeName) {
  const nC = W.length, D = new Float64Array(nC);
  if (modeName === 'compliance: diagonal') {
    for (let j = 0; j < nC; j++) D[j] = W[j][j];
  } else if (modeName === 'compliance: Gershgorin') {
    for (let j = 0; j < nC; j++) {
      let s = 0;
      for (let k = 0; k < nC; k++) s += Math.abs(W[j][k]);
      D[j] = s;
    }
  }
  return D;
}

Using the three-contact matrix $W$ from this chapter, compute by hand (or with a few lines of code) the spectral radius of $I - D^{-1}W$ for the two-contact block and for all three contacts, once with $D$ the diagonal and once with the row sums. Hint: eigenvalues without a library

For the symmetric 3×3 with equal diagonal $a$, the vector $(1, 0, -1)$ is an eigenvector of $W$ with eigenvalue $a - W_{13}$. The other two lie in the span of $(1, 0, 1)$ and $(0, 1, 0)$, which reduces the problem to a 2×2. With the row sums, $D^{-1}W$ is not symmetric, but $D^{-1/2}WD^{-1/2}$ is and has the same eigenvalues.

Solution

Two contacts: diagonal $\rho = 0.669$, Gershgorin $\rho = 0.802$. Three contacts: diagonal $\rho = 1.196$ (it diverges), Gershgorin $\rho = 0.889$. With a nearly rigid rod the demo predicts 1.196 and 0.889 too. At the default stiffness the rod foot gives by 0.068 mm/N per step, and that compliance $S$ joins $D$ in $(S + D)^{-1}(D - W)$: it damps the mode where all contacts push together, which is the diverging one, and brings the diagonal scheme down to 1.02. It still diverges, just more slowly. The rod's own give helps the Gershgorin scheme only slightly (its all-together mode already converges in one pass).

Summary

  • Monolithic coupling solves everything in one system: robust, and the reference. Partitioned coupling keeps one solver per body and exchanges interface forces and positions: flexible, and a numerical method in its own right.
  • With explicit lag or fixed-point iteration, the interface error is multiplied each exchange by the compliance of the force-receiving side over the compliance of the position-receiving side: $-k_1/k_2$ in the toy. Give the force to the stiff side.
  • Under-relaxation by $\theta$ converges for $\theta < 2/(1 + r)$; the best $\theta$ needs the stiffness ratio.
  • The soft side's compliance $W$ (its Green's function at the contacts, including inertia over a step) can be put into the stiff side's contact as an XPBD compliance $\alpha = D$. Overestimating is safe, and the converged answer does not depend on $D$.
  • With several contacts on the same soft body, use the row sums $D_{jj} = \sum_k|W_{jk}|$: then $D \succeq W$ and convergence is guaranteed. The diagonal alone can diverge from three contacts on.
