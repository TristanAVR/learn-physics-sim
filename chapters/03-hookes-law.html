Springs and Hooke's Law

The one-line law behind ropes, cloth, jelly and every integrator test on this site.

So far our particles feel gravity and drag, but nothing holds them together. To build anything with a shape, a rope or a cushion or a block of jelly, particles must push and pull on each other, and the simplest way to do that is a spring. This chapter is all physics and no numerical methods: what a spring does, what follows from it, and why almost every material behaves like a spring when you look closely enough.

It also prepares the next chapter. Time Integration tests every method on a mass bouncing on a spring, and it is much easier to follow when the spring itself is already familiar. By the end of this one you will be able to measure a spring's stiffness, predict how far it stretches and how fast it bounces, work out the energy it stores, and combine springs.

A guess before you start. You hang a 1 kg bag on a spring and it stretches 2 cm. How far does it stretch with a 3 kg bag? Write your answer down.

The notation

SymbolRead it asUnits
$x$the stretch: how far the spring is from its rest length. Positive when it is longer, negative when it is squashed.m
$F$the force the spring puts on whatever is attached to its endN (newtons)
$k$the stiffness (or spring constant): newtons of force per metre of stretchN/m

The rest length is the length at which the spring pulls on nothing. The stretch $x$ is always measured from there, never from zero length.

Hooke's law

Robert Hooke stated it in 1678 as a Latin anagram, ut tensio, sic vis: "as the extension, so the force". Pull a spring twice as far and it pulls back twice as hard:

$$F = -\,k\,x.$$

Read it in two parts:

  • The size, $k\,|x|$: proportional to the stretch, with $k$ setting how much force each metre costs. A soft spring (small $k$) gives a lot of stretch for a little force; a stiff one barely moves.
  • The sign, the minus: the force always points back toward the rest length. Stretch it to the right ($x > 0$) and the force points left ($F < 0$). Squash it ($x < 0$) and it pushes right. This "always back toward the middle" is what makes springs oscillate.

With numbers: measuring a spring

The easiest way to find $k$ is to hang a known weight on the spring and measure the stretch. When the weight hangs still, the spring's pull up exactly balances the weight down:

$$k\,x = m\,g \quad\Longrightarrow\quad k = \frac{m\,g}{x}, \qquad x = \frac{m\,g}{k}.$$

Hang 1 kg (weight $1 \times 9.81 = 9.81$ N) on a spring and it stretches 2 cm $= 0.02$ m. Then $k = 9.81 / 0.02 = 490.5$ N/m. Now a 3 kg bag: its weight is $29.43$ N, so it stretches $x = 29.43 / 490.5 = 0.06$ m, exactly 6 cm. Three times the load, three times the stretch, because the law is a straight line. That answers the guess. (If you said something else, the idea to fix is exactly this proportionality: double the cause, double the effect.)

units

A spring has $k = 250$ N/m. You pull its end with 5 N. How far does it stretch, in centimetres?

Answer

$x = F/k = 5/250 = 0.02$ m $= 2$ cm. Always convert to metres before using $k$ in N/m.

The picture: a straight line

Plot the force against the stretch and Hooke's law is a straight line through the origin, sloping down. Its slope is $-k$: a steeper line is a stiffer spring.

Energy: why there is a half

Stretching a spring takes work, and the spring stores that work as energy you can get back. Work is force times distance, but the force is not constant while you stretch: it starts at zero and grows to $k\,x$ by the end. So the average force over the whole stretch is half the final force, and

$$E = \underbrace{\tfrac12\,k\,x}_{\text{average force}} \times \underbrace{x}_{\text{distance}} = \tfrac12\,k\,x^2 .$$

That is the area of the orange triangle: half of base $x$ times height $kx$. Two things follow:

  • The energy depends on $x^2$: stretch twice as far and you store four times the energy. The 1 kg bag above stores $\tfrac12 \times 490.5 \times 0.02^2 = 0.098$ J; the 3 kg bag stores nine times that, $0.88$ J.
  • The energy is the same whether you stretch or squash by the same amount ($x^2$ does not care about the sign).

For the curious, the force is the downhill slope of the energy, $F = -\,dE/dx = -kx$. Every material model later on this site, up to the hyperelastic materials of the MPM chapters, is written this way round: energy first, force as its slope.

Combining springs

Sometimes it is easier to think about how much a spring gives per newton than how much it resists per metre. That is the compliance, $1/k$, in metres per newton, so that $x = (1/k)\,F$. A perfectly rigid connection has compliance 0, which is why the XPBD chapter is written entirely in compliances. The two views make combining springs easy:

End to end (series)Side by side (parallel)
pictureone spring hanging from anothertwo springs sharing one load
force in each springthe whole load, $F$shared: part each
stretch of each springeach stretches; the stretches addthe same, $x$, for both
rulecompliances add: $\dfrac{1}{k} = \dfrac{1}{k_1} + \dfrac{1}{k_2}$stiffnesses add: $k = k_1 + k_2$
two 500 N/m springs$1/k = 1/500 + 1/500$, so $k = 250$ N/m: softer$k = 1000$ N/m: stiffer

Why do compliances add in series? Each spring carries the full force $F$, so the first stretches $F/k_1$ and the second $F/k_2$, and the total stretch is $F\,(1/k_1 + 1/k_2)$. In parallel, both stretch the same $x$, so their forces $k_1 x$ and $k_2 x$ add to $(k_1 + k_2)\,x$.

A mass on a spring: the bounce

Hang a mass on the spring, pull it down a little, and let go. It bounces up and down around the point where it would hang still. How fast? Newton's second law, $a = F/m$, with Hooke's law for $F$ (measuring $x$ from the hanging point, so gravity is already balanced there):

$$a = -\frac{k}{m}\,x = -\,\omega^2\,x, \qquad \text{with } \omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} .$$

$\omega$ (omega) is the angular frequency in radians per second. It packs the two things that decide the bounce into one number: a stiffer spring or a lighter mass means a larger $\omega$ and a faster bounce. One full bounce, the period, takes

$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}} .$$

For the 1 kg bag on the 490.5 N/m spring: $\omega = \sqrt{490.5/1} = 22.1$ rad/s, so $T = 2\pi/22.1 = 0.28$ s, about three and a half bounces a second. The motion itself is a cosine. Released from rest a distance $A$ below the hanging point:

$$x(t) = A\cos(\omega t).$$
Why a cosine? (two lines of calculus)

We need a function whose acceleration, its second derivative, is $-\omega^2$ times itself. Differentiate $x = A\cos(\omega t)$ once: $v = -A\omega\sin(\omega t)$. Again: $a = -A\omega^2\cos(\omega t) = -\omega^2 x$. It works, and it starts at $x = A$ with $v = 0$, as released. Without calculus: a cosine is what you see when you watch a point going round a circle at a steady speed from the side, and the spring's motion is exactly that circle seen edge on, which is the picture the next chapter draws in "phase space".

Two things are worth noticing. First, the period does not depend on how far you pull it: a big bounce and a small bounce take the same time, because a bigger stretch also means a bigger force. Second, energy sloshes back and forth: at the turning points the mass is still and all the energy is in the spring ($\tfrac12 kA^2$); in the middle the spring is unstretched and all of it is motion ($\tfrac12 m v^2$). The total never changes:

$$E = \tfrac12\,m\,v^2 + \tfrac12\,k\,x^2 = \tfrac12\,k\,A^2 .$$

This is the exact answer the next chapter compares every integrator against.

Try the demo: change the mass and the stiffness and watch the equilibrium line and the period readout. Quadruple the mass and the period doubles ($T \propto \sqrt{m}$). Change the pull and the period stays put.

Why almost everything is a spring

Hooke's law is not really a law of nature. Real springs, rubber bands and steel beams follow it only for small stretches. But it is the right approximation for almost anything that has a resting position, and that is why it is everywhere in simulation.

Think of any force that pushes an object back toward some resting place: a pendulum, a ball in a bowl, two atoms in a crystal, a cell of jelly being squeezed. Plot that force against the displacement. It is some curve that passes through zero at the resting place. Zoom in close enough to that point, and every smooth curve looks like a straight line, with some slope we can call $-k$. So for small displacements, every such system is a spring.

The pendulum is the classic example. A bob of mass $m$ on a string of length $L$, pushed sideways by a small distance $s$, feels a restoring force of about $m g\,s/L$ (gravity's pull, tilted by the string). That is Hooke's law with $k = mg/L$, so

$$\omega = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{g}{L}}, \qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} .$$

The mass cancels, which is why a grandfather clock keeps time whatever its bob weighs, and a 1 m pendulum swings with a period of $2\pi\sqrt{1/9.81} = 2.0$ s.

Where Hooke's law breaks. Stretch far enough and real materials leave the straight line. Rubber gets stiffer as its long molecules straighten. Metal yields: it bends and stays bent (that is plasticity, met again in Plasticity: Snow and Sand). A coil spring squeezed until its coils touch becomes nearly rigid. A rope or a bungee cord pushes back on nothing when slack: it is a spring only when stretched.

In two and three dimensions

In a simulation a spring joins two particles, $a$ and $b$, and can point in any direction. It is the same law, applied along the line between them:

  1. Current length. $\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{x}_b - \mathbf{x}_a$, and its length $\ell = |\mathbf{d}|$.
  2. Stretch. $\ell - L$, where $L$ is the rest length.
  3. Direction. $\mathbf{n} = \mathbf{d}/\ell$, the unit vector from $a$ to $b$.
  4. Force. $\mathbf{F}_a = k\,(\ell - L)\,\mathbf{n}$ on $a$ (toward $b$ when stretched), and the exact opposite, $\mathbf{F}_b = -\mathbf{F}_a$, on $b$.

Because the two forces are equal and opposite, a spring can only change the distance between its particles, never push the pair as a whole. Springs, Damping and Energy builds soft bodies from many of these.

Hint

In the code, the stretch points down (the spring hangs), so a positive force pulls the mass down and the weight is $+mg$. Hooke's law is still $F = -kx$. The stretch at time $t$ is the equilibrium stretch plus pullDown * Math.cos(omega() * t).

Solution

Compare with solution.js:

function springForce(x) { return -stiffness.value * x; }
function equilibriumStretch() { return mass.value * g / stiffness.value; }
function omega() { return Math.sqrt(stiffness.value / mass.value); }
function stretchAt(t, pullDown) { return equilibriumStretch() + pullDown * Math.cos(omega() * t); }

With the defaults: $x = 9.81/50 = 19.6$ cm, $\omega = \sqrt{50} = 7.07$ rad/s, $T = 0.89$ s.

break the period

The period does not depend on how far you pull. Check it in the demo by changing the pull. Then think: the grey ghost is a real simulation of a spring that obeys Hooke's law for every stretch. If the spring stopped obeying it for large stretches, getting stiffer, say, would a big bounce be faster or slower than a small one?

Answer

Faster. A spring that stiffens at large stretch pulls harder on the big bounces, so it brings the mass back sooner. Only the straight-line law makes the period independent of the amplitude.

Summary

  • Hooke's law, $F = -kx$: the force is proportional to the stretch from the rest length and points back toward it. $k$ is the stiffness in N/m.
  • Hanging still: $kx = mg$. That is how you measure $k$, and how you predict a stretch.
  • Stored energy: $E = \tfrac12 kx^2$, the triangle under the force line. Twice the stretch, four times the energy.
  • Series springs add compliances ($1/k$); parallel springs add stiffnesses.
  • A mass on a spring bounces at $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, period $T = 2\pi/\omega$, whatever the amplitude, as $x = A\cos\omega t$, with its energy constant.
  • Near any resting position, almost every force is a spring; that is why springs are everywhere in simulation.
