Contact and Friction

Pushing only when touching, and the force that keeps a box on a slope and a rope on a post.

The last chapter found contacts: a normal and a penetration depth for each touching pair. It then pushed things apart and left it at that. The result looked like a liquid: particles slid off every slope and the heap spread flat. Real objects stay put. A book lies on a tilted desk, a ladder leans against a wall, a sailor holds a ship with a few turns of rope around a post. All of that is friction, and none of it exists in our simulation yet.

This chapter treats contact properly, as a constraint that can only push, adds bounce to a method that naturally has none, and then builds Coulomb friction in a position-based form that costs a handful of lines. You will watch a block start to slide at exactly the angle the textbook predicts, see a stick-slip oscillation squeak along, and then see something surprising: a rope made of nothing but particles, draped over a cylinder, reproduces the capstan equation, the exponential law that lets one person hold a ship.

Contact is a one-sided constraint

A distance constraint holds two particles at a fixed distance whether they want to come closer or move apart. A contact is different. A particle resting on the floor may lift off freely; it is only forbidden from going into the floor. With the gap function from the last chapter,

$$C(\mathbf{x}) = \phi(\mathbf{x}) - r \;\ge\; 0,$$

the rule is an inequality: project only when $C < 0$, and leave the particle alone otherwise. In position-based dynamics (the PBD chapter) that is the one-line test you already wrote:

const C = sceneSdf(x, y) - radius;
if (C < 0) { x -= C * nx; y -= C * ny; }   // push out along the normal, only when penetrating

The same idea in the language of forces is called complementarity. The normal force $N$ can only push ($N \ge 0$), the gap can only be open or closed ($C \ge 0$), and at least one of them is zero ($N\,C = 0$): either the bodies are apart and there is no force, or they touch and there may be. A solver that keeps a running Lagrange multiplier per contact, as XPBD does (the XPBD chapter), enforces $N \ge 0$ by clamping the accumulated multiplier at zero after each iteration, so that a contact pushed too hard in an early iteration can let go again later.

The normal force itself is something we can read off. In XPBD the force that a constraint applied over a step of length $h$ is $\lambda / h^2$. For a plain PBD push-out by a depth $d$ on a particle of mass $m$, the same reasoning gives $N = m\,d / h^2$: the force that, acting for one step on a particle at rest, would have moved it by $d$. Keep that conversion in mind. It is the key to friction.

Restitution: putting the bounce back

Drop a ball onto the floor in a PBD simulation and it stops dead. The projection puts it on the surface, and the velocity update $\mathbf{v} = (\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_{\text{prev}})/h$ then gives it exactly the velocity that reaches the surface and no more: its normal velocity after the step is zero. Position-based contact is perfectly inelastic. For sand, cloth and ropes that is what you want. For a rubber ball it is not.

The coefficient of restitution $e$ is the ratio of the normal speed after an impact to the speed before. A ball dropped from height $H$ hits at $\sqrt{2gH}$ and leaves at $e\sqrt{2gH}$, so it rises to $e^2 H$. A tennis ball has $e \approx 0.75$; a ball of wet clay has $e \approx 0$.

Müller and colleagues, in Detailed Rigid Body Simulation with Extended Position Based Dynamics (2020), add it in a second, velocity-level pass after the usual velocity update. For each contact that was active in the substep, with normal $\mathbf{n}$:

  1. Let $\bar v_n$ be the normal velocity before the substep (negative when approaching) and $v_n = \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v}$ the normal velocity after the PBD update (about zero).
  2. The target normal velocity is $\max(-e\,\bar v_n, 0)$: the incoming speed reflected and scaled by $e$.
  3. Change the velocity by $\Delta\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{n}\,\big(\max(-e\,\bar v_n, 0) - v_n\big)$.

One refinement matters in practice. A particle resting on the floor gains $g h$ of downward velocity every substep and has it removed by the contact. If that tiny speed were reflected, the particle would hop forever. So the paper sets $e = 0$ whenever $|\bar v_n| \le 2 g h$: resting contacts do not bounce.

function applyRestitution(i, h) {
  const vn = vx[i] * cnx[i] + vy[i] * cny[i];
  const e = Math.abs(vnBefore[i]) > 2 * G * h ? restitution.value : 0;
  const target = Math.max(-e * vnBefore[i], 0);
  vx[i] += (target - vn) * cnx[i];
  vy[i] += (target - vn) * cny[i];
}

This is the challenge exercise at the end, and it checks against a closed form: in the demo's slope scene, the "drop the block" button lifts the block 0.3 m above the slope. With $e = 0.5$ it should bounce to $e^2 \times 0.3 = 0.075$ m. The reference solution reaches 0.0745 m; the half-millimetre shortfall comes from resolving the impact one substep at a time.

Coulomb friction

Push a heavy box across a floor. At first nothing happens: the floor pushes back with exactly as much sideways force as you apply. Push harder and at some point the box breaks free and slides; once moving, it takes a little less force to keep it going. Charles-Augustin de Coulomb summarised this in the 1780s in a law that is still the default in nearly every simulator:

  • Static friction: while the contact is not sliding, the tangential force $F_t$ can take any value up to a limit, $|F_t| \le \mu_s N$, whatever is needed to prevent sliding.
  • Kinetic friction: once sliding, the tangential force has magnitude $\mu_k N$ and opposes the sliding velocity.

$\mu_s$ and $\mu_k$ are the static and kinetic friction coefficients, with $\mu_k \le \mu_s$. Some values: rubber on dry concrete is around 1, wood on wood 0.25 to 0.5, steel on steel 0.6 dry and 0.1 lubricated, ice on ice about 0.03. The law is approximate (real friction depends a little on speed, temperature and contact area), but it captures the two essential facts: friction is proportional to how hard the surfaces are pressed together, and there is a threshold between sticking and slipping.

The friction cone

Put the normal force and the friction force together into one contact force $\mathbf{F} = N\mathbf{n} + \mathbf{F}_t$. Static friction allows any $\mathbf{F}$ whose tangential part is at most $\mu_s$ times its normal part: any force within an angle $\arctan\mu_s$ of the normal. In 3D that set is a cone around the normal, the friction cone; in 2D it is a wedge. A contact sticks if the force needed to keep it still lies inside the cone, and slips otherwise. This is the picture behind every friction model in the literature.

The block on a slope

The classic test is a block of mass $m$ on a slope tilted by $\theta$. Split gravity into a part along the slope, $m g \sin\theta$, and a part into it, $m g \cos\theta$. The normal force balances the second, $N = m g\cos\theta$. Static friction must supply the first. That is possible while

$$m g \sin\theta \le \mu_s\, m g \cos\theta \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \tan\theta \le \mu_s.$$

The mass and gravity cancel: the block slips at the angle $\theta_s = \arctan\mu_s$, which for $\mu_s = 0.5$ is 26.57°. Once sliding, kinetic friction leaves a net acceleration along the slope of

$$a = g\,(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta).$$

This is a sharp test: a simulation either reproduces both numbers or it does not.

Friction in position-based form

A position-based solver does not work with forces, so we translate. Over one step, a force $F$ acting on a mass $m$ that starts at rest moves it by $F h^2/m$. Run that backwards:

  • The normal push-out $d_n$ made this step stands for a normal force $N = m\, d_n/h^2$.
  • The tangential motion $\Delta\mathbf{x}_t$ of the particle over the step, relative to the surface, stands for the tangential force $\mathbf{F}_t = m\,\Delta\mathbf{x}_t/h^2$ that static friction would need to cancel.

Put both into Coulomb's static condition $|\mathbf{F}_t| \le \mu_s N$, and $m$ and $h^2$ cancel:

$$|\Delta\mathbf{x}_t| < \mu_s\, d_n \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \text{static: undo all of } \Delta\mathbf{x}_t.$$

Otherwise the contact slides, and kinetic friction removes the displacement that a force $\mu_k N$ would produce in one step, $\mu_k d_n$, but never more than the motion itself (friction can stop a slide; it cannot reverse it):

$$\text{kinetic:} \quad \Delta\mathbf{x}_t \leftarrow \Delta\mathbf{x}_t\,\Big(1 - \min\big(\mu_k d_n / |\Delta\mathbf{x}_t|,\, 1\big)\Big).$$

This is the rule from Macklin, Müller, Chentanez and Kim, Unified Particle Physics for Real-Time Applications (2014), in their section on friction, where both particles of a pair may move and the correction is shared by inverse mass. Against a static surface the particle takes all of it:

function solveContact(i, nx, ny, depth) {
  x[i] += depth * nx;  y[i] += depth * ny;          // normal: push out by d_n = depth
  const dx = x[i] - px[i], dy = y[i] - py[i];        // motion over this substep
  const dn = dx * nx + dy * ny;
  const tx = dx - dn * nx, ty = dy - dn * ny;        // its tangential part
  const lt = Math.hypot(tx, ty);
  if (lt < muS * depth) { x[i] -= tx; y[i] -= ty; }           // static: stick
  else if (lt > 0) {
    const k = Math.min(muK * depth / lt, 1);                    // kinetic: slide, slowed
    x[i] -= tx * k;  y[i] -= ty * k;
  }
}

Check it against the slope by hand. A block at rest on the slope, in one substep, is predicted to move by $g h^2$ straight down. The part into the slope, $d_n = g\cos\theta\,h^2$, is pushed back out; the part along it is $|\Delta\mathbf{x}_t| = g\sin\theta\,h^2$. The static test $g\sin\theta\,h^2 < \mu_s\,g\cos\theta\,h^2$ is exactly $\tan\theta < \mu_s$. Once sliding, each substep removes $\mu_k g\cos\theta\,h^2$ of the slide, which is a deceleration of exactly $\mu_k g\cos\theta$. The position-based rule is not an approximation of Coulomb's law for this problem: it is Coulomb's law.

In the "tilting slope" scene, drag the angle slider up slowly, or press "tilt slowly from 0 until it slips". The block turns red when it slides. With the default $\mu_s = 0.5$ it lets go at 26.6°, where the readout shows $\tan\theta = 0.500$. Set the angle to 35° and the block slides with $a = 9.81(\sin 35^\circ - 0.4\cos 35^\circ) = 2.41$ m/s2, which the readout reproduces to three digits. The arrows show gravity (black) and its two parts along (orange) and into (teal) the slope.

Measure the tangential motion over the whole step, not over one iteration. If your solver runs several iterations, $\Delta\mathbf{x}_t$ must be the motion since the start of the substep, and $d_n$ the total normal correction. Comparing one iteration's tiny tangential nudge with that iteration's tiny normal nudge gives the wrong answer whenever the iterations do not converge evenly. And if the surface itself moves (a conveyor belt, a moving platform), $\Delta\mathbf{x}_t$ is the motion relative to the surface point.

Stick-slip

Static friction above kinetic friction creates a characteristic instability. Switch to "stick-slip": a 1 kg block on the floor is pulled through a spring of stiffness $k = 100$ N/m, whose far end moves to the right at a steady 2 cm/s. The plot shows the spring force divided by $mg$.

At first the block sticks and the spring stretches; the force climbs at $k \times 0.02 = 2$ N/s. When it reaches $\mu_s m g = 4.905$ N the block breaks free. Now only $\mu_k m g$ resists, the spring is stretched more than that needs, and the block lurches forward, overshoots, and stops. Then it sticks again and the cycle repeats: a sawtooth.

The bottom of the sawtooth has a neat closed form. During the slip the block is a mass on a spring with a constant force $\mu_k m g$ opposing it, so it oscillates about the stretch at which the spring force equals $\mu_k mg$. It starts at a spring force of $\mu_s mg$, a distance $(\mu_s - \mu_k)mg/k$ above that centre. Measured in the frame of the moving end, it also starts with velocity equal to the pull speed, and it sticks again exactly when that relative velocity returns, which by the symmetry of the oscillation is at the same distance below the centre. So the spring force when it sticks again is

$$F_{\text{stick}} = (2\mu_k - \mu_s)\,m g,$$

independent of the pull speed and of $k$. With $\mu_s = 0.5$ and $\mu_k = 0.4$ the force runs between $0.5\,mg$ and $0.3\,mg$; the readouts show 0.499 and 0.300. The stretch between the two is $2(\mu_s - \mu_k)mg/k = 1.96$ cm, which the moving end takes 0.98 s to make up, so the block lurches about every 1.3 s. A squeaking door hinge, a bowed violin string and the creep and jump of a geological fault are the same oscillation at different scales. Set $\mu_k = \mu_s$ and the sawtooth disappears: the block slides smoothly.

The capstan: friction that multiplies

Wrap a rope around a fixed post. Pull on one end with tension $T_1$, and ask how much tension $T_2$ the other end can carry before the rope slips. The answer is the capstan equation, also called the Euler-Eytelwein formula:

$$T_2 \le T_1\, e^{\mu\theta},$$

where $\theta$ is the total angle of contact in radians. It is exponential in the angle, which is why it matters so much. With $\mu = 0.3$, a half turn ($\theta = \pi$) holds a ratio of 2.57, a turn and a half ($3\pi$) holds 16.9, two full turns 43.4, three full turns 286. A deckhand pulling with the force of 10 kg on a rope wrapped three times around a bollard can hold a load of nearly 3 tonnes. If you worked through the cable-driven bending chapter, you met this equation as the reason a tendon loses tension along a curved sheath.

Where the exponential comes from

Take a short piece of rope in contact over a small angle $d\theta$, with tension $T$ at one end and $T + dT$ at the other. The two tensions pull at slightly different angles, and their components towards the post add up to a normal force $dN = 2T\sin(d\theta/2) \approx T\,d\theta$. Friction can hold a tension difference of at most $\mu\,dN$:

$$dT \le \mu\,T\,d\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dT}{T} \le \mu\,d\theta \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \ln\frac{T_2}{T_1} \le \mu\,\theta.$$

The more tension the rope carries, the harder it presses on the post, and the more friction it gets. Tension breeds friction breeds tension: that feedback is the exponential.

The capstan in the simulation

Switch the demo to "rope over cylinder". The rope is the particle rope of the PBD and XPBD chapters, 74 particles 2 cm apart with zero-compliance distance constraints, with a weight on each end. Each rope particle collides with the cylinder through the same solveContact as the block. There is no capstan code anywhere. The equation has to emerge, if it emerges at all, from dozens of independent particle contacts each applying the positional Coulomb rule.

Set both friction coefficients to 0.3 and press "raise the right weight until it slips". The right weight grows by 10 % per second until the rope starts to move on the light side, and the readout records the ratio of the tensions where the rope leaves the cylinder. The prediction is $e^{0.3\pi} = 2.566$; the simulation slips at about 2.60. Switch to one and a half turns, set the left weight to 0.15 kg and the right one to 2 kg (to shorten the wait), and run it again: the prediction is $e^{0.9\pi} = 16.90$, and the rope lets go at about 17.3.

Both measurements come out 1 to 2.5 % high, and the reason is physical, not numerical. Our rope weighs 0.05 kg per metre, and the part lying on the cylinder presses down on it under its own weight, adding normal force and therefore friction. Integrating the rope element balance with that extra load included predicts 2.61 for the half turn and 17.5 for the turn and a half, bracketing what the simulation does. With a weightless rope both would collapse to $e^{\mu \theta}$.

Why the rope needs $\mu_k = \mu_s$ here. A real rope stretches, so as the load grows, the section near the heavy side creeps over the post a little before the whole rope slips; belts on pulleys do the same, and engineers call it belt creep. Creeping sections slide, so they feel kinetic, not static, friction. In the simulation the stretch comes from the constraint solver, and with $\mu_k < \mu_s$ the rope lets go somewhere between $e^{\mu_k\theta}$ and $e^{\mu_s\theta}$. Try it: with $\mu_s = 0.3$ and $\mu_k = 0.2$ the half turn slips at a tension ratio of about 2.15, between $e^{0.2\pi} = 1.87$ and $e^{0.3\pi} = 2.57$.

The rope needs some care to behave. Its particles weigh 1 g each, while the weights weigh hundreds of grams, and such mass ratios are hard for Gauss-Seidel projection. The demo uses 20 substeps per frame and 16 passes over the rope's constraints per substep. With fewer, the rope stretches visibly under load, the stretching reads as tangential motion at the contacts, and the rope creeps and slips early.

Friction on rolling and spinning bodies

Particles have no orientation, so in this chapter friction only ever resisted sliding. For a rigid body (the rotations chapter) the friction force acts at the contact point, a distance $r$ from the centre of mass, so it also exerts a torque of magnitude $\mu_k N r$ while the contact slides. Two consequences are worth knowing.

A ball launched sliding without spin, like a bowling ball, is slowed by friction at the contact point while that same force spins it up. It stops sliding when the contact point comes to rest, $v = \omega r$, and from then on it rolls with no friction loss at all. For a solid sphere this happens at $v = \tfrac57 v_0$, after a time $2v_0 / (7\mu_k g)$: for a ball released at 5 m/s with $\mu_k = 0.2$, 3.57 m/s after 0.73 s. A body standing on a flat face and spinning, like a coin spun on its edge or a turntable braking on its felt, has its friction spread over the contact patch. For a flat disc of radius $R$ with even pressure, adding up $\mu_k\,dN$ times each element's distance from the centre gives a braking torque of $\tfrac23\mu_k N R$.

You get both behaviours for free in a position-based rigid body solver. The positional friction correction is applied at the contact point, and the solver's generalized inverse mass, $w = 1/m + (\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{n})^2/I$ for a correction along $\mathbf{n}$ at offset $\mathbf{r}$, splits it into linear and angular parts exactly as a force at that point would. Rolling resistance, the small torque that eventually stops a rolling ball, is a separate, empirical effect of deforming surfaces and is usually added as a small damping of the angular velocity.

Hint: which motion to cancel

The variables tx, ty already hold the tangential part of the particle's motion over the substep, measured from px[i], py[i]. Static friction subtracts all of it; kinetic friction subtracts the fraction Math.min(mu_k * depth / lt, 1) of it.

Solution

  x[i] -= tx;                       // static: cancel all tangential motion
  y[i] -= ty;

Solution 
function applyRestitution(i, h) {
  const nx = cnx[i], ny = cny[i];
  const vn = vx[i] * nx + vy[i] * ny;
  const before = vnBefore[i];
  const e = Math.abs(before) > 2 * G * h ? restitution.value : 0;
  const target = Math.max(-e * before, 0);
  vx[i] += (target - vn) * nx;
  vy[i] += (target - vn) * ny;
}

The bounce reaches about 0.0745 m against 0.075 m predicted. Without the threshold, the $g h$ that gravity adds every substep is reflected as a bounce of its own, so the resting block buzzes on the surface instead of settling.

friction at grid nodes in MPM

MPM handles collisions with obstacles on the grid, at the velocity level, after the grid velocities have been updated. The MPM course notes (in their treatment of collision objects, following the snow paper of Stomakhin et al., 2013) do exactly what this chapter does with displacements, but with velocities: if a grid node's velocity relative to the obstacle has a normal component $v_n < 0$ (moving in), remove it; then shrink the tangential part $\mathbf{v}_t$ by $\mu |v_n|$, or set it to zero if it is smaller than that. Read $|v_n|$ as our $d_n/h$ and $\mathbf{v}_t$ as our $\Delta\mathbf{x}_t/h$ and it is the same rule: the normal correction sets the budget, and friction spends at most $\mu$ times that budget on the tangential motion. The papers use a single $\mu$ for both regimes.

Summary

  • A contact is an inequality constraint $C \ge 0$: project only when $C < 0$. The normal force can only push; XPBD clamps the accumulated multiplier at zero.
  • Position-based contact is perfectly inelastic. Restitution is a velocity pass: set the normal velocity to $\max(-e\,\bar v_n, 0)$, with $e = 0$ for resting contacts ($|\bar v_n| \le 2gh$).
  • Coulomb friction: stick while $|F_t| \le \mu_s N$, otherwise slide against $\mu_k N$. A block on a slope slips at $\tan\theta = \mu_s$ and then accelerates at $g(\sin\theta - \mu_k\cos\theta)$.
  • Positional friction compares the tangential motion over the step with the normal correction $d_n$: cancel it if it is below $\mu_s d_n$, otherwise remove $\mu_k d_n$ of it. For a particle on a plane this is Coulomb's law exactly.
  • $\mu_s > \mu_k$ produces stick-slip, a sawtooth between $\mu_s mg$ and $(2\mu_k - \mu_s)mg$.
  • A rope over a post holds a tension ratio of up to $e^{\mu\theta}$ (the capstan equation), and a particle rope with per-particle positional friction reproduces it.
  • On rigid bodies, friction at the contact point also produces a torque, $\mu_k N r$ while sliding.
