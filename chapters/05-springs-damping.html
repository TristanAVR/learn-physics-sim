Springs, Damping and Energy

Tie particles together, make them wobble, and find out where the wobble goes.

So far our particles have ignored each other. To build anything with a shape, a rope or a cushion or a block of jelly, particles must push and pull on their neighbours. The oldest and still the most useful way to do that is to join them with springs. A spring is easy to write, easy to reason about, and it is the local behaviour of nearly every elastic material, so what you learn here carries straight into cloth, rods and continuum mechanics.

Springs alone ring forever, so we add dampers. And springs bring back the stability limit from Time Integration in a new form: a network of springs is only as stable as its fastest wobble. By the end of this chapter you will be able to build a soft body from springs, choose a damping coefficient from a target damping ratio, account for every joule of energy, and predict the stiffness at which your soft body will explode before it does.

A spring between two particles

In the previous chapter the spring sat on a line and its force was $-k x$. In two (or three) dimensions a spring connects two particles $a$ and $b$ at positions $\mathbf{x}_a$ and $\mathbf{x}_b$. It has a rest length $L$, the length at which it pulls on nothing, and a stiffness $k$ in newtons per metre. Let

$$\mathbf{d} = \mathbf{x}_b - \mathbf{x}_a, \qquad \ell = |\mathbf{d}|, \qquad \mathbf{n} = \frac{\mathbf{d}}{\ell}.$$

$\ell$ is the current length and $\mathbf{n}$ the unit vector pointing from $a$ to $b$. Hooke's law says the spring's tension is proportional to its stretch $\ell - L$, and the force acts along the spring:

$$\mathbf{F}_a = k\,(\ell - L)\,\mathbf{n}, \qquad \mathbf{F}_b = -\mathbf{F}_a.$$

Check the signs with a picture. If the spring is longer than its rest length, $\ell - L > 0$, so particle $a$ is pulled toward $b$ (along $+\mathbf{n}$) and $b$ toward $a$. If it is compressed, both signs flip and the particles are pushed apart. The two forces are equal and opposite, which is Newton's third law, and it means a spring can never change the total momentum of the system: a soft body cannot push itself across the floor.

The force also comes from an energy. A spring stores $E_\text{spring} = \tfrac12 k (\ell - L)^2$, and the force on each particle is minus the gradient of that energy with respect to the particle's position. Since $\partial \ell / \partial \mathbf{x}_a = -\mathbf{n}$, we get $\mathbf{F}_a = -\partial E / \partial \mathbf{x}_a = k(\ell - L)\,\mathbf{n}$, the same formula. Keep this route in mind; every elastic model later in the course, up to the hyperelastic materials of MPM, defines an energy first and differentiates it to get forces.

A damper along the spring

A real spring loses energy as it flexes. We model that with a damper (a dashpot, like the piston in a door closer), which resists the rate at which the spring changes length. The rate of stretching is the relative velocity of the two ends, projected on the spring's axis:

$$\dot\ell = (\mathbf{v}_b - \mathbf{v}_a)\cdot\mathbf{n}.$$

The damper adds a tension $c\,\dot\ell$, with a damping coefficient $c$ in N·s/m. The complete spring-damper force on $a$ is

$$\mathbf{F}_a = \bigl[\,k\,(\ell - L) + c\,(\mathbf{v}_b - \mathbf{v}_a)\cdot\mathbf{n}\,\bigr]\,\mathbf{n}, \qquad \mathbf{F}_b = -\mathbf{F}_a.$$

Why project onto the axis instead of damping the whole relative velocity? Because a pair of particles spinning around each other at a fixed distance is not deforming. A damper on the full relative velocity would brake that spin, and a tumbling soft body would slow its rotation through the air as if it were moving through syrup. The projection damps only stretching, which is what a dashpot does. In code:

function springForce(s) {
  const pa = particles[s.a], pb = particles[s.b];
  const d = V.sub(pb.x, pa.x);
  const len = V.len(d);
  if (len < 1e-9) return V.v(0, 0);                 // coincident particles: no direction
  const n = V.scale(d, 1 / len);                      // unit vector from a to b
  const stretch = len - s.rest;                       // > 0 when longer than the rest length
  const stretchRate = V.dot(V.sub(pb.v, pa.v), n);    // > 0 when getting longer
  const tension = k * stretch + c * stretchRate;
  return V.scale(n, tension);                         // force on a; b gets the opposite
}

Each step, we zero every particle's force, add gravity, loop over the springs adding $\mathbf{F}$ to one end and subtracting it from the other, and then integrate. For the integrator we use symplectic Euler from chapter 04: velocity first, then position with the new velocity.

for (const p of particles) {
  if (p.w === 0) continue;                          // inverse mass 0: pinned in place
  p.v = V.addScaled(p.v, p.f, h * p.w);             // v += h F / m
  p.x = V.addScaled(p.x, p.v, h);                   // x += h v (the new v)
}

Each particle stores its inverse mass $w = 1/m$. A pinned particle, such as the top of a hanging chain, gets $w = 0$: forces then change nothing, which is exactly "infinitely heavy". You will meet $w$ everywhere from now on.

Drop the square and grab a corner. It squashes on landing, rings for a moment and settles. Switch off the diagonal springs and it folds flat: a grid of squares can shear for free, because shearing a square does not change the length of its sides. The diagonals are what resist shear. The readouts show the energy stored in each form, and three quantities, $h$, $\omega_\text{max}$ and $h\,\omega_\text{max}$, that we will come back to.

One mass, one spring, one damper

To understand damping properly, strip the system down to a single mass $m$ on a spring and damper, displaced by $u$ from equilibrium. Newton's law gives the most studied equation in physics:

$$m\,\ddot u + c\,\dot u + k\,u = 0.$$

(If the mass hangs under gravity, $u$ is measured from the equilibrium point, where the spring is stretched by $mg/k$; gravity then drops out of the equation.) Two numbers describe the solutions completely. The natural frequency $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$ is how fast the undamped system would swing. The damping ratio

$$\zeta = \frac{c}{2\sqrt{k\,m}}$$

is how strong the damper is compared with the spring and mass. It has no units, and its value alone decides the character of the motion:

Regime$\zeta$Released from rest at $u_0$
Undamped$0$oscillates forever, $u = u_0 \cos\omega t$
Underdamped$0 < \zeta < 1$oscillates at $\omega_d = \omega\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}$ inside a decaying envelope $u_0 e^{-\zeta\omega t}$
Critically damped$1$$u = u_0 (1 + \omega t)\,e^{-\omega t}$: the fastest return with no overshoot
Overdamped$> 1$creeps back without crossing zero, more slowly the larger $\zeta$

For the underdamped case the full solution is $u = u_0\,e^{-\zeta\omega t}\bigl(\cos\omega_d t + \tfrac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\sin\omega_d t\bigr)$, and the first swing past zero overshoots by the fraction $e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}$ of the starting displacement. Setting $\zeta = 1$ gives the critical damping coefficient

$$c_\text{crit} = 2\sqrt{k\,m}.$$

The demo below is a 1 kg block on a 100 N/m spring, so $\omega = 10$ rad/s and the undamped period is $2\pi/\omega = 0.628$ s. It draws the simulated displacement over the exact solution and plots the energy.

At $\zeta = 0.1$ ($c = 2$ N·s/m) the block overshoots by 72.9 % on its first swing and keeps 28 % of its energy per cycle. At $\zeta = 0.5$ the overshoot is 16.3 %. At $\zeta = 1$ ($c = 20$ N·s/m) it returns without crossing zero and stays within 1 % of rest after 0.66 s. At $\zeta = 2$ it takes 1.75 s: more damping is not faster. Curiously, $\zeta \approx 0.83$ settles within 1 % sooner still, in 0.42 s, by overshooting slightly less than 1 %; that is why many control systems are tuned a little below critical. In every case the simulated curve sits on the dashed one: with $h = 1$ ms the largest difference over three seconds is 1.5 mm out of 300.

Energy, and where damping takes it

A system of particles and springs in gravity has three kinds of energy:

$$E = \underbrace{\sum_i \tfrac12 m_i |\mathbf{v}_i|^2}_{\text{kinetic}} + \underbrace{\sum_\text{springs} \tfrac12 k (\ell - L)^2}_{\text{spring}} + \underbrace{\sum_i m_i\, g\, y_i}_{\text{gravity}}.$$

Without dampers or collisions, $E$ is constant: energy moves between the three forms but never leaves. With dampers, differentiate $E$ along the motion. The spring and gravity terms cancel against the work done by their own forces, and what remains is the work done by the damper forces, which for each damper is

$$\frac{dE}{dt} = -c\,\dot\ell^{\,2} \;\le\; 0.$$

So a damper can only remove energy, and it removes it fastest when the spring is changing length fastest. That energy has become heat in the real world; in the simulation it simply disappears. In the soft-square demo, watch the total-energy curve: it drops in steps at each impact (the ground here is perfectly inelastic, so contacting particles lose their downward velocity) and drains smoothly in between as the dampers work.

The energy plot is also your best debugging tool. Energy that increases in a system with no motors and no mouse is always a bug or an instability. With symplectic Euler and no damping, the total wobbles slightly but does not drift; that wobble is the integrator, and it shrinks with $h$. A steady climb means your time step is too large for the fastest thing in the system. Which brings us to networks.

Spring networks and the fastest mode

Choose "hanging chain" in the soft-body demo: twenty particles of 25 g joined by 10 cm springs, released horizontally from a pinned end. Then choose "soft square" again. Both are just particles and springs, and the code that runs them is identical. What differs is how many ways they can wobble.

A single mass on a spring has one frequency, $\sqrt{k/m}$. A network of $N$ particles has $2N$ (in 2D) normal modes, each a pattern of motion in which every particle oscillates at the same frequency. Slow modes are large and gentle: the chain swinging as a whole, the square rocking. Fast modes are small and jittery: neighbouring particles moving against each other. The stability limit from chapter 04, $h\,\omega \le 2$ for symplectic Euler, applies to every mode, so the one that matters is the fastest, $\omega_\text{max}$.

For a chain of equal masses the fastest mode is easy to guess: alternate particles move in opposite directions along the chain. Each particle then sees both of its springs stretched or squashed by $2u$, so the restoring force is $4ku$ and

$$\omega_\text{max} \approx \sqrt{\frac{4k}{m}} = 2\sqrt{\frac{k}{m}}.$$

That is twice the frequency of one mass on one spring to a wall. The chain becomes unstable at a quarter of the stiffness a single spring could take with the same mass and time step. For the soft square the fastest mode is a checkerboard jiggle, every particle moving against its neighbours, strongest in the middle; its frequency comes from the eigenvalues of the network's stiffness matrix. For this 8 × 8 grid with diagonals, the largest eigenvalue is $7.57\,k$ (in units where the masses are 1), so

$$\omega_\text{max} = \sqrt{\frac{7.57\,k}{m}}.$$

The demo computes this for you (by power iteration on the current shape, a few iterations per frame) and prints $\omega_\text{max}$ and $h\,\omega_\text{max}$. With $k = 300$ N/m and $m = 1/64$ kg, $\omega_\text{max} = 381$ rad/s. Ten substeps per 1/60 s frame give $h = 1.67$ ms and $h\,\omega_\text{max} = 0.64$, comfortably stable. Setting $h\,\omega_\text{max} = 2$ and solving for $k$:

$$k_\text{crit} = \frac{4\,m}{7.57\,h^2} \approx 2970\ \text{N/m}.$$

A single 15.6 g particle on a single spring to a wall would survive up to $4m/h^2 = 22{,}500$ N/m. The network gives up at an eighth of that, because many springs pull on each particle at once.

Stiff networks need small steps, and lighter particles make it worse. $k_\text{crit}$ scales as $m/h^2$. Double the resolution of the square (four times the particles, each a quarter of the mass) and keep the same spring constant $k$: the critical stiffness drops by four, so to keep those springs stable the time step must halve. This is the first appearance of a fact that drives the rest of the course: explicit methods for fine, stiff materials need tiny time steps, and the methods of the coming chapters exist to escape that.

Damping does not rescue you here; it makes the limit slightly tighter. For a damped mode, symplectic Euler is stable while

$$h\,\omega \le 2\left(\sqrt{1 + \zeta^2} - \zeta\right),$$

which equals 2 at $\zeta = 0$ and falls as $\zeta$ grows. And a damper that is gentle on slow modes is strong on fast ones: because each damper acts on the same stretching as its spring, the fastest mode's damping ratio is $\zeta = c\,\omega_\text{max}/(2k)$, which grows with $\omega_\text{max}$. With $k = 300$ N/m and our default $c = 0.05$ N·s/m the fastest mode has $\zeta = 0.03$, but the formula predicts the square goes unstable at $c = 2.23$ N·s/m. In the demo it survives 2.2 and explodes at 2.25.

Two ways to combine a spring and a damper

Our spring-damper puts the spring and the dashpot side by side: both ends shared, both stretched by the same amount, forces added. Materials scientists call this the Kelvin-Voigt model. It is a solid: it always returns to its rest length. If you hang a constant load $F$ on it, it does not jump to its final stretch but creeps toward it,

$$u(t) = \frac{F}{k}\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right), \qquad \tau = \frac{c}{k},$$

with a time constant $\tau$, because the dashpot slows the spring down.

Put the spring and dashpot in series instead, end to end, and you get the Maxwell model. Now both carry the same force, and their stretches add. The spring stretches instantly; the dashpot stretches at a rate proportional to the force, $c_M\,\dot u_d = F$. Hold the total stretch fixed, and the dashpot slowly takes it over from the spring, so the force relaxes:

$$F(t) = F_0\,e^{-t/\tau}, \qquad \tau = \frac{c_M}{k}.$$

A Maxwell element is a fluid at heart: under a constant load it flows forever, and when released it does not go back. Putty, dough and many gels behave this way on long time scales while bouncing like solids on short ones.

The neat way to simulate a Maxwell spring is to notice that the dashpot's stretch is just a change of the spring's rest length. Give each spring a rest length $L$ that drifts toward the current length:

$$\frac{dL}{dt} = \frac{\ell - L}{\tau} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad L_{n+1} = \ell + (L_n - \ell)\,e^{-h/\tau}.$$

The second form solves the drift exactly over one step (holding $\ell$ fixed), so it is stable for any $h$. The spring force formula is unchanged; only $L$ moves. Turn on "Maxwell springs" in the demo and the square slowly slumps under its own weight like a lump of dough (faster at low stiffness), and keeps any shape you drag it into. You have just written your first plastic material, a theme the MPM chapters take much further in Plasticity: Snow and Sand.

forces from energies

The MPM course notes build every material the way we derived the spring force: write down a potential energy, then take its gradient. Their discussion of hyperelasticity defines an energy density $\Psi$ of the deformation gradient $\mathbf{F}$ (chapter 20), and the force on a grid node is minus the derivative of the total energy with respect to that node's position. Our $\tfrac12 k(\ell - L)^2$ is the one-dimensional ancestor of their $\Psi$; the stretch ratio $\ell / L$ plays the role of $\mathbf{F}$. Several of their energies have one term that penalises change of shape and another that penalises change of size, a job our edge and diagonal springs share between them without being told.

Summary

  • A spring between two particles pulls along its axis with tension $k(\ell - L)$; the two forces are equal and opposite. The force is minus the gradient of the energy $\tfrac12 k(\ell - L)^2$.
  • A damper resists the rate of stretching, $(\mathbf{v}_b - \mathbf{v}_a)\cdot\mathbf{n}$, so it does not slow rigid rotation. It always removes energy, at the rate $c\,\dot\ell^2$.
  • The damping ratio $\zeta = c / (2\sqrt{km})$ decides the regime: under, critical ($c = 2\sqrt{km}$) or overdamped.
  • A network's stability is set by its fastest mode. For a chain $\omega_\text{max} \approx 2\sqrt{k/m}$; for the soft square $\sqrt{7.57\,k/m}$. Keep $h\,\omega_\text{max}$ well below 2.
  • Kelvin-Voigt (parallel) creeps and recovers; Maxwell (series) relaxes and flows, and is simulated by letting the rest length drift with time constant $\tau$.
