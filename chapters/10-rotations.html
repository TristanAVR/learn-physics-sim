Rotations and Rigid Bodies

Things with size can turn. How to store that turning, integrate it, and constrain it, first in 2D and then in 3D.

So far everything has been a particle: a point with a position and a velocity. A chain of particles can bend, but each bead has no orientation of its own, so there is nothing for a bead to twist or point. Rods, which are the subject of the next three chapters, need exactly that: a direction along the rod and a direction across it at every point, so that bending and twisting have something to act on. The tool for that is the rigid body, an object that has a size, a mass distribution and an orientation, and can rotate as well as move.

In 2D, an orientation is one angle and everything is easy. In 3D, orientation is genuinely tricky: rotations do not commute, three angles are not a good way to store them, and a spinning object can change its axis of rotation with no torque acting on it at all. This chapter goes through both, in that order.

By the end you will be able to integrate a 2D rigid body, hit it off-centre and predict its spin, join bodies with XPBD pin joints that rotate them correctly, store and integrate a 3D orientation as a quaternion, and explain why a box spun about its middle axis flips over.

A rigid body in 2D

A rigid body is a set of points that keep their distances to each other. That leaves very few ways for it to move: its centre of mass $\mathbf{x}$ can translate, and the body can rotate about it by an angle $\theta$. A point fixed in the body, at position $\bar{\mathbf{r}}$ in the body's own coordinates (its body frame), is at

$$\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{x} + \mathbf{R}(\theta)\,\bar{\mathbf{r}}, \qquad \mathbf{R}(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$$

in the world. Write $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{R}\bar{\mathbf{r}}$ for the same lever arm in world coordinates. The state of the body is four numbers plus their rates: position $\mathbf{x}$, angle $\theta$, velocity $\mathbf{v}$, and angular velocity $\omega = \dot\theta$ in radians per second. The velocity of the point is

$$\dot{\mathbf{p}} = \mathbf{v} + \omega\,\mathbf{r}^\perp, \qquad \mathbf{r}^\perp = (-r_y,\ r_x),$$

the centre's velocity plus the rotation, which moves the point perpendicular to its lever arm.

A force $\mathbf{F}$ applied at the point does two things. It accelerates the centre of mass exactly as if it were applied there, $m\dot{\mathbf{v}} = \mathbf{F}$, and it creates a torque about the centre of mass,

$$\tau = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F} = r_x F_y - r_y F_x ,$$

the 2D cross product, a scalar. Torque is to rotation what force is to translation: $I\dot\omega = \tau$, where $I$ is the moment of inertia, the body's resistance to angular acceleration, in kg m2. Integrating the two together, with symplectic Euler from chapter 3, is two pairs of lines:

body.vx += h * Fx / body.mass;    body.vy += h * Fy / body.mass;
body.omega += h * torque / body.inertia;
body.x += h * body.vx;            body.y += h * body.vy;
body.theta += h * body.omega;

In 2D the angle is an ordinary number and adding $h\omega$ to it is exact for the rotation part. Enjoy it while it lasts.

The moment of inertia

For a collection of point masses $m_i$ at distances $d_i$ from the axis, $I = \sum_i m_i d_i^2$; for a continuous body, $I = \int d^2\,dm$. Mass far from the axis counts quadratically more, which is why a figure skater spins faster when they pull their arms in.

A thin rod of mass $m$ and length $L$, about its centre: put the rod along $s \in [-L/2, L/2]$ with mass per length $m/L$. Then

$$I_{\text{centre}} = \int_{-L/2}^{L/2} \frac{m}{L}\,s^2\,ds = \frac{m}{L}\,\frac{s^3}{3}\Big|_{-L/2}^{L/2} = \frac{mL^2}{12}.$$
BodyAxisMoment of inertia
thin rod, length $L$centre, perpendicular to the rod$mL^2/12$
solid rectangle, sides $w \times d$centre, perpendicular to the plane$m(w^2 + d^2)/12$
solid disk, radius $r$centre, perpendicular to the disk$mr^2/2$
thin ring, radius $r$centre, perpendicular to the ring$mr^2$
solid box, sides $2a \times 2b \times 2c$centre, along the $2a$ side$m(b^2 + c^2)/3$

Moments of inertia about other axes follow from the parallel axis theorem: about an axis parallel to one through the centre of mass, at distance $d$ from it, $I = I_{\text{cm}} + md^2$. You will use it in the last exercise to get the rod's moment of inertia about its end.

The bars in this chapter's demo are 0.5 m long, 6 cm thick and weigh 1 kg. As rectangles they have $I = 1 \times (0.5^2 + 0.06^2)/12 = 0.02113$ kg m2, a little above the thin-rod value of 0.02083.

Hitting a body off-centre

Collisions and joints act through very short, very strong forces, so it is convenient to work with their time integral, the impulse $\mathbf{J} = \int\mathbf{F}\,dt$ in N s. An impulse at a point changes the velocities instantly:

$$\Delta\mathbf{v} = \frac{\mathbf{J}}{m}, \qquad \Delta\omega = \frac{\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{J}}{I}.$$

Hit the end of a demo bar with 0.5 N s perpendicular to it: the centre moves off at $0.5$ m/s, and the bar spins at $0.5 \times 0.25/0.02113 = 5.91$ rad/s. Now ask how fast the point that was hit moves along $\hat{\mathbf{J}}$, the direction of the impulse. Its velocity change is $\Delta\mathbf{v} + \Delta\omega\,\mathbf{r}^\perp$, and its component along $\hat{\mathbf{J}}$ works out as

$$\Delta\dot{\mathbf{p}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{J}} = |\mathbf{J}|\left(\frac1m + \frac{(\mathbf{r}\times\hat{\mathbf{J}})^2}{I}\right).$$

The bracket is an inverse mass: it says how much the point gives per unit of impulse in that direction. Call it the generalised inverse mass of the body at that point along that direction,

$$w = \frac1m + \frac{(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{n})^2}{I}.$$

At the end of a demo bar, pushed sideways, $w = 1 + 0.25^2/0.02113 = 3.96$ kg-1: the end feels like a mass of 0.25 kg, a quarter of the bar, because pushing it also turns the bar. Pushed along the bar, $\mathbf{r}\times \mathbf{n} = 0$ and the end feels the full kilogram. This one quantity is all XPBD needs to handle rigid bodies.

XPBD joints for rigid bodies

A pin joint says that a point $\bar{\mathbf{r}}_A$ on body $A$ and a point $\bar{\mathbf{r}}_B$ on body $B$ are in the same place. As a constraint, with world points $\mathbf{p}_A$, $\mathbf{p}_B$,

$$C = |\mathbf{p}_B - \mathbf{p}_A|, \qquad \mathbf{n} = \frac{\mathbf{p}_B - \mathbf{p}_A}{|\mathbf{p}_B - \mathbf{p}_A|}.$$

This is chapter 7's distance constraint with rest length zero, between two points that are not particles. Müller, Macklin, Chentanez, Jeschke and Kim, in Detailed Rigid Body Simulation with Extended Position Based Dynamics (2020), handle it the way you would hope. Think of the correction as a positional impulse $\mathbf{p} = \Delta\lambda\,\mathbf{n}$ applied at the joint point, an impulse measured in kg m instead of kg m/s. Exactly like a velocity impulse, it moves and turns each body:

$$\Delta\mathbf{x}_B = \frac{\mathbf{p}}{m_B}, \qquad \Delta\theta_B = \frac{\mathbf{r}_B\times\mathbf{p}}{I_B}, \qquad \Delta\mathbf{x}_A = -\frac{\mathbf{p}}{m_A}, \qquad \Delta\theta_A = -\frac{\mathbf{r}_A\times\mathbf{p}}{I_A}.$$

The joint points then move towards each other along $\mathbf{n}$ by $\Delta\lambda\,(w_A + w_B)$, with the generalised inverse masses from the last section. Asking for that to cancel $C$, plus compliance, gives the familiar XPBD update with the familiar denominator:

$$\Delta\lambda = \frac{-C - \tilde\alpha\,\lambda}{w_A + w_B + \tilde\alpha}, \qquad w_A = \frac{1}{m_A} + \frac{(\mathbf{r}_A\times\mathbf{n})^2}{I_A}.$$

For a particle, $I$ is irrelevant and $w = 1/m$: rigid bodies are a strict extension of everything so far. A fixed anchor has $w = 0$. In code:

function solvePointConstraint(A, pa, B, pb, alphaTilde) {
  const dx = pb.x - pa.x, dy = pb.y - pa.y, c = Math.hypot(dx, dy);
  if (c < 1e-12) return;
  const nx = dx / c, ny = dy / c;
  let wA = 0, wB = 0, rAn = 0, rBn = 0;
  if (A) { rAn = (pa.x - A.x) * ny - (pa.y - A.y) * nx; wA = A.invMass + rAn * rAn * A.invInertia; }
  if (B) { rBn = (pb.x - B.x) * ny - (pb.y - B.y) * nx; wB = B.invMass + rBn * rBn * B.invInertia; }
  const dLambda = -c / (wA + wB + alphaTilde);       // one visit per substep, so lambda = 0 here
  if (A) { A.x -= A.invMass * dLambda * nx; A.y -= A.invMass * dLambda * ny; A.theta -= A.invInertia * rAn * dLambda; }
  if (B) { B.x += B.invMass * dLambda * nx; B.y += B.invMass * dLambda * ny; B.theta += B.invInertia * rBn * dLambda; }
}

Here rAn is $\mathbf{r}_A\times\mathbf{n}$, so A.invInertia * rAn * dLambda is $(\mathbf{r}_A \times\mathbf{p})/I_A$. The step around it is the XPBD loop with substeps from chapter 8, extended to angles: predict $\theta \mathrel{+}= h_s\,\omega$ along with the position, solve the joints, then recover $\omega = (\theta - \theta_{\text{prev}})/h_s$ along with the velocity.

How good is it? Choose the pendulum scene: one bar pinned at its end, released from 10 degrees. Its period, with the moment of inertia about the pivot that you will derive in the exercises, is 1.1603 s for small swings, or 1.1625 s corrected for the 10 degree amplitude. The demo measures 1.1613 s with 5 substeps, 1.1619 s with 20 and 1.1621 s with 40, within 0.1 % of the exact value; the small remainder is the numerical damping of the method. With a single substep the bar visibly jitters on its pivot and the timing fails. As in chapter 8, run rigid bodies with substeps.

Why a joint turns the bodies the right amount. The ratio between the translation and rotation of each body is set by $1/m$ versus $(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{n})^2/I$, the same split an impulse at that point would cause. A position-level correction that respected only the masses, ignoring the inertia, would weld the bars together: try it in the starter, where the joints treat bars as point masses and the chain sticks out sideways like a stiff beam.

Orientation in 3D

In 3D, a rotation can be about any axis, and that changes everything. Rotate a book 90 degrees about a vertical axis and then 90 degrees about a horizontal one; now do it in the other order. The book ends up in different orientations. Rotations do not commute, so there is no way to store an orientation as three angles you can simply add to. There are four common representations, each good at something.

Rotation matrices. A 3×3 matrix $\mathbf{R}$ whose columns are the body's three axes written in world coordinates. It maps body coordinates to world coordinates, $\mathbf{r} = \mathbf{R}\bar{\mathbf{r}}$, and composes by multiplication. It is orthonormal ($\mathbf{R}^T\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$, so its inverse is its transpose) with $\det\mathbf{R} = 1$. That is nine numbers for three degrees of freedom, and after many small updates rounding makes the columns drift away from orthonormal, so the matrix has to be re-orthonormalised from time to time.

Euler angles. Three angles applied in a fixed sequence of axes, for example yaw about $z$, then pitch about $y$, then roll about $x$. They are compact and easy for a human to read, and bad for simulation. When the pitch reaches 90 degrees, the first and last rotations turn about the same axis, so two of the three angles do the same thing and one degree of freedom is gone: gimbal lock. Near that configuration a small physical rotation needs large, fast changes of the angles, and interpolating two orientations by interpolating their angles takes strange, wobbly paths.

Axis-angle and the rotation vector. Every rotation is a turn by some angle $\varphi$ about some unit axis $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ (Euler's rotation theorem). Packing both into one vector gives the rotation vector $\boldsymbol\phi = \varphi\,\hat{\mathbf{a}}$, three numbers for three degrees of freedom. It is the natural representation for small rotations and rates: an angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega$ is a rotation vector per second, and $h\boldsymbol\omega$ is the rotation during one step. Composing two finite rotation vectors, though, has no simple formula.

Quaternions. A quaternion is four numbers, $q = (w, x, y, z)$, written as a scalar part and a vector part, $q = (s, \mathbf{u})$. The rotation by $\varphi$ about $\hat{\mathbf{a}}$ is the unit quaternion

$$q = \left(\cos\frac{\varphi}{2},\ \sin\frac{\varphi}{2}\,\hat{\mathbf{a}}\right), \qquad |q| = 1.$$

Quaternions multiply by the rule

$$(s_1, \mathbf{u}_1)(s_2, \mathbf{u}_2) = \bigl(s_1 s_2 - \mathbf{u}_1\cdot\mathbf{u}_2,\ \ s_1\mathbf{u}_2 + s_2\mathbf{u}_1 + \mathbf{u}_1\times\mathbf{u}_2\bigr),$$

which is not commutative, because of the cross product, exactly like rotations. With the conjugate $q^* = (s, -\mathbf{u})$, which is the inverse of a unit quaternion:

  • Rotating a vector: treat $\mathbf{v}$ as $(0, \mathbf{v})$; then $\mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}$ is the vector part of $q\,(0, \mathbf{v})\,q^*$.
  • Composing: rotating by $q_1$ and then by $q_2$ is the single rotation $q_2 q_1$.
  • Normalising: after an update, divide by $|q|$. Four numbers and one square root, far cheaper and simpler than re-orthonormalising a matrix.
  • Double cover: $q$ and $-q$ are the same rotation (turning by $\varphi + 2\pi$ flips the sign of both parts). Remember it when comparing or interpolating two quaternions: pick the sign that makes their dot product positive.
  • Interpolation: moving along the great circle between two unit quaternions (slerp) rotates at a constant rate about a fixed axis, the shortest path between the orientations, with no gimbal effects.

The rule of thumb used by most simulators: store orientation as a unit quaternion, convert to a matrix when you need to transform many vectors, and use rotation vectors for angular velocities and small corrections.

The exponential and logarithm maps

The formula $q = (\cos(\varphi/2), \sin(\varphi/2)\,\hat{\mathbf{a}})$ turns a rotation vector into a quaternion. It is called the exponential map, because it really is the quaternion exponential of $(0, \boldsymbol\phi/2)$:

$$\exp(\boldsymbol\phi) = \left(\cos\frac{|\boldsymbol\phi|}{2},\ \ \sin\frac{|\boldsymbol\phi|}{2}\, \frac{\boldsymbol\phi}{|\boldsymbol\phi|}\right) \approx \left(1,\ \frac{\boldsymbol\phi}{2}\right)\ \text{for small }\boldsymbol\phi.$$

Its inverse, the logarithm map, recovers the rotation vector of a unit quaternion $q = (s, \mathbf{u})$:

$$\log(q) = 2\,\operatorname{atan2}(|\mathbf{u}|, s)\,\frac{\mathbf{u}}{|\mathbf{u}|}.$$

(Pick the sign of $q$ with $s \ge 0$ first, so that the angle comes out between 0 and $\pi$.) The pair is how you move between "rates and small differences" and "orientations". A body turning at a constant world angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega$ for a time $h$ ends up at exactly

$$q_{n+1} = \exp(h\boldsymbol\omega)\,q_n,$$

and given two orientations, the angular velocity that takes one to the other in time $h$ is $\boldsymbol\omega = \log(q_{n+1}q_n^*)/h$.

Integrating an orientation

Differentiate $q(t) = \exp(t\boldsymbol\omega)\,q_0$ at $t = 0$, using the small-angle form, and you get the equation of motion of a quaternion:

$$\dot q = \tfrac12\,(0, \boldsymbol\omega)\,q ,$$

with $\boldsymbol\omega$ in world coordinates. (With $\boldsymbol\omega$ in body coordinates, the product goes on the other side: $\dot q = \tfrac12 q\,(0, \bar{\boldsymbol\omega})$.) One explicit Euler step of it, followed by normalisation, is the update almost every simulator uses:

$$q \leftarrow \operatorname{normalize}\!\left(q + \frac{h}{2}\,(0, \boldsymbol\omega)\,q\right).$$

It is first-order accurate, and the normalisation keeps it on the unit sphere. For the small rotations of a substep that is plenty; when the rotation per step is large, use $\exp(h\boldsymbol\omega)\,q$ instead. The reverse operation, recovering the angular velocity after an XPBD solve, is the small-angle logarithm:

$$\Delta q = q\,q_{\text{prev}}^*, \qquad \boldsymbol\omega = \frac{2}{h}\,(\Delta q_x, \Delta q_y, \Delta q_z), \qquad\text{negated if } \Delta q_w \lt 0.$$

The inertia tensor and Euler's equations

In 3D, the moment of inertia becomes a 3×3 symmetric matrix $\mathbf{I}$, the inertia tensor, and the angular momentum $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{I}\boldsymbol\omega$ is generally not parallel to $\boldsymbol\omega$. Every rigid body has three perpendicular principal axes in which its tensor is diagonal, $\bar{\mathbf{I}} = \operatorname{diag}(I_1, I_2, I_3)$; for a box they are its edges, with the moments from the table above. That diagonal form lives in the body frame. In the world frame the tensor turns with the body:

$$\mathbf{I}_{\text{world}} = \mathbf{R}\,\bar{\mathbf{I}}\,\mathbf{R}^T, \qquad \mathbf{I}_{\text{world}}^{-1} = \mathbf{R}\,\bar{\mathbf{I}}^{-1}\mathbf{R}^T.$$

Newton's law for rotation says $d\mathbf{L}/dt = \boldsymbol\tau$ in the world frame. But $\mathbf{L} = \mathbf{I}_{\text{world}}\boldsymbol\omega$, and $\mathbf{I}_{\text{world}}$ changes as the body turns. Writing the law in the body frame, where the tensor is constant, picks up one extra term:

$$\bar{\mathbf{I}}\,\dot{\bar{\boldsymbol\omega}} + \bar{\boldsymbol\omega}\times(\bar{\mathbf{I}}\,\bar{\boldsymbol\omega}) = \bar{\boldsymbol\tau}.$$

These are Euler's equations, and $\bar{\boldsymbol\omega}\times\bar{\mathbf{I}}\bar{\boldsymbol\omega}$ is the gyroscopic term. It vanishes when the body spins about a principal axis, because then $\bar{\mathbf{I}}\bar {\boldsymbol\omega}$ is parallel to $\bar{\boldsymbol\omega}$. Otherwise, even with zero torque, the angular velocity changes: the angular momentum is fixed in space, and the body tumbles around it. Integrating it explicitly is one line per component, before the quaternion update:

// body-frame angular velocity wb, principal moments I[0..2], no torque
const Iw = [I[0] * wb[0], I[1] * wb[1], I[2] * wb[2]];
const gyro = cross(wb, Iw);
for (let k = 0; k < 3; k++) wb[k] -= h * gyro[k] / I[k];

The tennis racket

Take a box of 1.0 × 0.6 × 0.2 m and 1 kg. Its principal moments are $I_1 = 0.0333$, $I_2 = 0.0867$ and $I_3 = 0.1133$ kg m2, about its long, middle and short edges. Spin it at $\Omega$ about one of them, with a tiny extra rotation $\omega_j, \omega_k$ about the other two. For a spin about axis 2, Euler's equations with no torque read, to first order in the small components,

$$I_1\dot\omega_1 = (I_2 - I_3)\,\Omega\,\omega_3, \qquad I_3\dot\omega_3 = (I_1 - I_2)\,\Omega\,\omega_1 .$$

Differentiate the first and substitute the second:

$$\ddot\omega_1 = \Omega^2\,\frac{(I_3 - I_2)(I_2 - I_1)}{I_1 I_3}\,\omega_1 .$$

When $I_2$ is the intermediate moment, both brackets are positive, and the solution grows exponentially, like $e^{\sigma t}$ with

$$\sigma = \Omega\sqrt{\frac{(I_3 - I_2)(I_2 - I_1)}{I_1 I_3}} .$$

For a spin about the smallest or the largest axis, the same calculation gives a negative coefficient: the small components oscillate, a wobble, and the spin is stable. This is the intermediate axis theorem, also called the tennis racket theorem: toss a racket spinning about the axis in the plane of its face and perpendicular to its handle, and it makes a half-turn about the handle every time it comes round.

For our box at $\Omega$ = 4 rad/s, $\sigma = 0.614\,\Omega = 2.45$ s-1. A perturbation of one part in a thousand grows to the size of the spin itself after about $\ln(1000)/\sigma = 2.8$ s, and then the box flips. The demo measures the growth rate while the perturbation is still small: 2.47 s-1. Switch to the stable axes and it reports the predicted wobble frequency, $\Omega\sqrt{(I_k - I_j)(I_k - I_l)/(I_j I_l)}$: 2.636 rad/s about the long axis and 3.437 rad/s about the short one. The simulation's wobble has 2.636 and 3.440 rad/s.

Watch the plot: the green component, the spin about the intermediate axis, sits at +1, then swings to -1 and back, again and again, while the dashed line, the angular momentum, stays fixed in space. Untick "gyroscopic term" and nothing happens at all: without that term, the body keeps whatever angular velocity it has, which is wrong for any spin that is not exactly about a principal axis.

Explicit gyroscopics gain energy. The explicit update above is not energy-conserving. With 20 substeps per frame the box gains 1.2 % of its kinetic energy over 20 seconds, and with 5 substeps 5 %; the readouts show it. For games that is usually fine. For long runs or fast spins, treat the gyroscopic term implicitly (one Newton step on Euler's equations), or rescale $\bar{\boldsymbol\omega}$ after each step so that the energy and $|\mathbf{L}|$ stay at their initial values.

XPBD rigid bodies in 3D

Everything from the 2D joint carries over, with vectors in place of scalars. The generalised inverse mass of body $A$ at lever arm $\mathbf{r}_A$ along $\mathbf{n}$ is

$$w_A = \frac{1}{m_A} + (\mathbf{r}_A\times\mathbf{n})^T\,\mathbf{I}_A^{-1}\,(\mathbf{r}_A\times\mathbf{n}),$$

with the world-frame inverse inertia. After computing $\Delta\lambda$ exactly as before, the positional impulse $\mathbf{p} = \Delta\lambda\,\mathbf{n}$ updates position and orientation:

$$\mathbf{x}_A \leftarrow \mathbf{x}_A - \frac{\mathbf{p}}{m_A}, \qquad q_A \leftarrow \operatorname{normalize}\!\left(q_A - \tfrac12\,\bigl(0,\ \mathbf{I}_A^{-1}(\mathbf{r}_A\times\mathbf{p})\bigr)\,q_A\right),$$

and with the opposite signs for body $B$. The orientation update is the quaternion integration step with the rotation vector $\mathbf{I}^{-1}(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{p})$ in place of $h\boldsymbol\omega$. A ball joint is one such positional constraint. A hinge adds an angular constraint that aligns an axis on $A$ with an axis on $B$: its correction is a pure rotation $\Delta\boldsymbol\phi = \Delta\lambda\,\mathbf{n}$ about some axis $\mathbf{n}$, with generalised inverse mass $\mathbf{n}^T\mathbf{I}^{-1}\mathbf{n}$, and the same update without the translation. The paper builds every joint type, and contacts with friction, from these two building blocks, inside the substep loop of chapter 8.

hit a bar off-centre

Choose the free bar scene. The kick button applies 0.5 N s straight up at the bar's right end, through applyImpulse, which so far only changes the linear velocity. Before you fix it, predict the speed and spin after one kick, first treating the bar as a thin rod and then with its 6 cm thickness. Add the angular part, then kick once and compare with the readout. Bonus: where along the bar would you have to hit it so that its left end starts with zero velocity?

Hint

$\Delta v = J/m$ and $\Delta\omega = rJ/I$ with $r = L/2$. For the bonus, the left end's velocity is $\Delta v - \Delta\omega\,L/2$ for a hit at distance $d$ to the right of the centre, with $\Delta\omega = dJ/I$.

Solution 
const rx = px - body.x, ry = py - body.y;
body.omega += (rx * jy - ry * jx) * body.invInertia;

Speed 0.5 m/s either way. Thin rod: $\omega = 0.5 \times 0.25/(1 \times 0.5^2/12) = 6$ rad/s, or $6J/(mL)$. With thickness, $I = 0.02113$ kg m2 and $\omega = 5.915$ rad/s, which the demo shows. Bonus: zero velocity at the left end needs $J/m = (dJ/I)(L/2)$, so $d = 2I/(mL) = L/6$ for a thin rod: hit it two thirds of the way along from the end you want to stay still. That point is the centre of percussion, the sweet spot of a bat or a racket.

integrate a quaternion and watch the flip

Open the tumbling box starter (run it, read it). The box does not move. First implement the quaternion update $q \leftarrow \operatorname{normalize}(q + \tfrac{h}{2}(0, \boldsymbol\omega)q)$ in integrate: the box should spin steadily about its green axis, and never flip. Then add the gyroscopic term and watch the flips start. Compare the measured growth rate with $\sigma$, try the two stable axes, and find out how the time to the first flip changes when you make the perturbation ten times smaller.

Hint

The helpers qmul and qnormalize exist. Build the pure quaternion { w: 0, x: omegaW[0], y: omegaW[1], z: omegaW[2] }, multiply it by q (in that order, because omegaW is in world coordinates), scale by $h/2$, add to q component by component, and normalise. For the time to the first flip, remember that the perturbation grows like $e^{\sigma t}$.

Solution

Compare with tumble.js:

const Iw = [inertia[0] * wb[0], inertia[1] * wb[1], inertia[2] * wb[2]];
const gyro = cross(wb, Iw);
wb = [wb[0] - h * gyro[0] / inertia[0], wb[1] - h * gyro[1] / inertia[1], wb[2] - h * gyro[2] / inertia[2]];
omegaW = qrotate(q, wb);
const dq = qmul({ w: 0, x: omegaW[0], y: omegaW[1], z: omegaW[2] }, q);
q = qnormalize({ w: q.w + 0.5 * h * dq.w, x: q.x + 0.5 * h * dq.x, y: q.y + 0.5 * h * dq.y, z: q.z + 0.5 * h * dq.z });

A perturbation ten times smaller needs $\ln(10)/\sigma = 0.94$ s longer to grow to the same size, at $\Omega$ = 4 rad/s. The flip is delayed but never prevented: any perturbation at all, including rounding error, eventually flips the box.

derive the moment of inertia of a rod about its end

On paper, compute the moment of inertia of a thin rod of mass $m$ and length $L$ about one end, twice: once by integrating $\int s^2\,dm$ over $s \in [0, L]$, and once from $mL^2/12$ with the parallel axis theorem. Then use it to predict the small-swing period of a rod pivoted at its end, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{I/(m g d)}$, where $d$ is the distance from the pivot to the centre of mass. Predict the period for the demo's bar (0.5 m, treat it as thin first, then include the 6 cm thickness), and check it with the pendulum scene. Then try fewer substeps.

Hint

$\int_0^L (m/L) s^2\,ds$. For the parallel axis theorem, the centre is $d = L/2$ from the end. In the period, the rod's length cancels against itself except for one factor: $T = 2\pi\sqrt{2L/(3g)}$.

Solution

Both routes give $I_{\text{end}} = mL^2/3$: the integral is $(m/L)\,L^3/3$, and $mL^2/12 + m(L/2)^2 = mL^2/3$. The thin-rod period is $2\pi\sqrt{2 \times 0.5/(3 \times 9.81)} = 1.1582$ s. With thickness, $I_{\text{end}} = 0.02113 + 0.0625 = 0.08363$ kg m2 and $T = 1.1603$ s; the 10 degree amplitude lengthens that to 1.1625 s, and the demo measures 1.1615 s at the default 10 substeps. With one substep the jitter on the pivot spoils the measurement; from two substeps on it is within 0.1 %.

angular momentum in APIC

Rotations come back in the MPM papers in a different guise. A particle in APIC (Jiang et al. 2015) carries, besides its velocity, a 3×3 matrix $\mathbf{C}_p$ describing how the velocity varies across its neighbourhood. For a blob of material spinning rigidly at $\boldsymbol\omega$, the velocity field is $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{x}) = \boldsymbol\omega\times \mathbf{x}$, so $\mathbf{C}_p$ is the skew-symmetric matrix of $\boldsymbol\omega$: the antisymmetric part of $\mathbf{C}_p$ is the local angular velocity. Plain PIC throws $\mathbf{C}_p$ away, and with it the spin, which is why PIC visibly damps rotation; APIC keeps it, and the paper's headline property is that the particle-grid transfers conserve angular momentum exactly. The APIC chapter comes back to this; a spinning disk of particles is the classic test.

Summary

  • A 2D rigid body has position, angle, velocity and angular velocity. Torque $\tau = \mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{F}$ drives $I\dot\omega = \tau$; moments of inertia come from $\int d^2\,dm$ and the parallel axis theorem.
  • An impulse $\mathbf{J}$ at lever arm $\mathbf{r}$ changes $\mathbf{v}$ by $\mathbf{J}/m$ and $\omega$ by $(\mathbf{r}\times\mathbf{J})/I$. The point responds with the generalised inverse mass $w = 1/m + (\mathbf{r}\times \mathbf{n})^2/I$.
  • XPBD joints on rigid bodies use $w$ in the denominator and apply the correction as a positional impulse that moves and turns each body. With substeps, a pinned bar's period comes out within 0.1 % of the exact value.
  • In 3D, store orientation as a unit quaternion: rotate vectors with $q\mathbf{v}q^*$, compose by multiplication, normalise after updates. Euler angles suffer gimbal lock; rotation vectors are for rates and small changes.
  • The exponential map turns a rotation vector into a quaternion and the logarithm back. Integrate with $q \leftarrow \operatorname{normalize}(q + \tfrac{h}{2}(0,\boldsymbol\omega)q)$.
  • The inertia tensor is diagonal in the body frame and rotates with the body. Euler's equations add the gyroscopic term $\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf{I}\boldsymbol\omega$, which makes rotation about the intermediate axis unstable with growth rate $\Omega\sqrt{(I_3 - I_2)(I_2 - I_1)/(I_1I_3)}$.
