The Material Point Method

Particles that remember how they have been stretched, a grid that turns that memory into forces, and one loop that simulates jelly and water alike.

In the APIC chapter, particles handed their velocities to a grid and took them back, and nothing ever pushed back. A block of APIC particles thrown at a wall would splatter like dust. What is missing is the material: the jelly's wish to return to its shape, the water's refusal to be squeezed. You already have both halves of the answer. The deformation gradient $\mathbf F$ says how a piece of material has been stretched and turned, and hyperelasticity turns $\mathbf F$ into an energy $\Psi$ and a stress $\mathbf P$.

The Material Point Method (MPM) joins them. Each particle carries an $\mathbf F$; every step, the grid collects the particles' stresses as forces on its nodes, updates its velocities, and hands them back; the particles use those velocities to move and to update their $\mathbf F$. Because the grid is rebuilt every step, the particles can tear apart, merge and collide with each other without any collision detection at all.

By the end of this chapter you will have written the full explicit MPM loop, the eight steps of section 10.5 of the MPM course notes, and you will know where each line comes from. You will be able to predict the largest time step a material allows, make a fluid out of the same code with a one-line equation of state, and read sections 9 and 10 of the course notes with the code beside you.

A particle is a piece of material

The word "particle" is misleading here. An MPM particle is not a grain or a molecule; it stands for a small region of continuous material, and everything it stores describes that region:

SymbolMeaningUnits (2D)
$x_p,\ v_p$position and velocity of the region's centrem, m/s
$m_p$mass of the region, fixed foreverkg (per metre of depth)
$V_p^0$volume of the region before any deformation, fixed foreverm²
$\mathbf F_p$deformation gradient: how the region has been stretched and rotatednone
$\mathbf C_p$APIC affine velocity, the local velocity gradient seen by the particle1/s

We start with a block of material, a density $\rho$ and a grid of spacing $\Delta x$. Seeding two particles per cell along each axis (four per cell in 2D), each particle owns a square of side $\Delta x/2$, so

$$V_p^0 = \left(\tfrac{\Delta x}{2}\right)^2, \qquad m_p = \rho\, V_p^0.$$

With the demo's $\Delta x = 1/64$ m = 15.6 mm and jelly of density 1000 kg/m³, that is $V_p^0 = 6.10\times10^{-5}$ m² and $m_p = 0.061$ kg per metre of depth. The current volume of a particle is never stored: it is $V_p = J_p V_p^0$, with $J_p = \det\mathbf F_p$, as you saw in the deformation gradient chapter.

There is a second, more useful way to see the particles: as quadrature points. Any integral over the material can be approximated by visiting the particles and adding up their contributions,

$$\int_{\text{material}} f(x)\,dx \;\approx\; \sum_p V_p\, f(x_p),$$

exactly like sampling a function at a few points to estimate its area. The total mass is $\sum_p m_p$, the total elastic energy is $\sum_p V_p^0\,\Psi(\mathbf F_p)$ (energy density times undeformed volume), and, as we will see, the force on a grid node is a sum of particle stresses. MPM is a finite element method on the grid whose integration points happen to move with the material and remember its history.

Here is where we are heading. Three jelly blocks, one thrown, about 2,000 particles, a 64 by 64 grid. Click and hold to push the material around, switch to the dam break, and then raise the Young's modulus or the time step until it explodes.

The course notes make this point at the start of section 5 (Kinematics Theory): material points are pieces of the continuum and can be read as quadrature points of a finite element discretisation. Section 8 introduces the particle state and the grid basis functions; the notes write the basis function of node $\mathbf i$ as $N_{\mathbf i}(x)$ and shorten $N_{\mathbf i}(x_p)$ to $w_{\mathbf{i}p}$ and its gradient to $\nabla w_{\mathbf{i}p}$, which is the notation we use. The quadratic B-spline we use is their equation (123). Their bold $\mathbf i$ is a multi-index, $(i, j)$ in 2D; in our code it is the pair of loop counters a, b added to the particle's base cell.

Newton's law, one grid node at a time

For a continuum, Newton's second law holds at every point: density times acceleration equals the divergence of the stress plus gravity,

$$\rho\, a = \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma + \rho\, g.$$

We cannot satisfy that at every point; we have a grid with a few thousand velocities. The finite element trick is to ask for something weaker. Multiply both sides by a smooth "test" function $q(x)$, integrate over the material, and require the result to hold for every $q$ we can build on the grid. Integrating the stress term by parts moves the derivative from $\boldsymbol\sigma$ (which we only know at particles) onto $q$ (which we know everywhere):

$$\int \rho\, q\cdot a\,dx \;=\; -\int \nabla q : \boldsymbol\sigma\,dx \;+\; \int \rho\, q\cdot g\,dx \;+\; \text{boundary terms}.$$

This is the weak form. Now pick $q$ to be one grid basis function $N_i$ at a time, in one direction at a time. Each choice gives one equation per grid node: on the left, the node's mass times its acceleration (after "lumping" the mass, so that node $i$ simply gets $m_i = \sum_p w_{ip} m_p$); on the right, the force on node $i$. The stress integral becomes a sum over particles, our quadrature points:

$$f_i \;=\; -\int \boldsymbol\sigma\,\nabla N_i\,dx \;\approx\; -\sum_p V_p\,\boldsymbol\sigma_p\,\nabla w_{ip}.$$

Finally, write the Cauchy stress with the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress, $\boldsymbol\sigma = \frac1J \mathbf P \mathbf F^T$, and the current volume as $V_p = J_p V_p^0$. The $J$s cancel and leave the formula every MPM code uses:

$$\boxed{\,f_i = -\sum_p V_p^0\, \mathbf P(\mathbf F_p)\, \mathbf F_p^T\, \nabla w_{ip}\,}$$

the weak form and its discretisation

Section 7.3 of the course notes derives the weak form twice: in material coordinates, their equations (113) to (118), and pushed forward to the current configuration, equation (120), which is the one MPM discretises. Section 9 does the discretisation in the same order as this page: replace the acceleration by a difference of velocities (9.1, equations 133 to 135), replace $q$ and $v$ by grid interpolants (9.2, equations 136 to 141), lump the mass matrix (equations 142 to 146; note their remark that the full mass matrix can be singular), and approximate the stress integral with particles (equation 148). Section 9.3 gives two ways to estimate the particle volume; we use the second, $V_p^n \approx J_p^n V_p^0$ (equation 153), which turns the force into the $\mathbf P\mathbf F^T$ form of their equations (154) and (155). They write $\Delta t$ for our $h$ and use Greek letters for vector components.

The same force, from the energy

There is a second derivation that is shorter and, for elastic materials, more illuminating. It is also the one the implicit and position-based chapters build on, so it is worth doing carefully.

Imagine, for one step only, that the grid nodes move with their velocities: node $i$ would end the step at $\hat x_i = x_i + h\,v_i$. A particle's deformation gradient would then change the way the grid around it deforms. The velocity gradient at the particle is $\nabla v_p = \sum_i v_i\,(\nabla w_{ip})^T$, so after the step

$$\hat{\mathbf F}_p(\hat x) = \Big(\mathbf I + \sum_i (\hat x_i - x_i)\,(\nabla w_{ip})^T\Big)\,\mathbf F_p.$$

The elastic energy of the whole body, with the particles as quadrature points, is

$$\Phi(\hat x) = \sum_p V_p^0\, \Psi\big(\hat{\mathbf F}_p(\hat x)\big).$$

Forces are minus the gradient of the potential energy, so differentiate with respect to one node position. In index notation, $\hat F_{\alpha\beta} = F_{\alpha\beta} + \sum_j (\hat x_{j\alpha} - x_{j\alpha})\,(\nabla w_{jp})_\gamma F_{\gamma\beta}$, hence $\partial \hat F_{\alpha\beta} / \partial \hat x_{i\sigma} = \delta_{\alpha\sigma}\,(\nabla w_{ip})_\gamma\,F_{\gamma\beta}$, and with $\partial\Psi/\partial\mathbf F = \mathbf P$,

$$\frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial \hat x_{i\sigma}} = \sum_p V_p^0\, P_{\sigma\beta}\,F_{\gamma\beta}\,(\nabla w_{ip})_\gamma = \sum_p V_p^0\,\big(\mathbf P\,\mathbf F^T\,\nabla w_{ip}\big)_\sigma .$$

Evaluate at $\hat x = x$ (the grid has not moved yet, so $\hat{\mathbf F}_p = \mathbf F_p$) and you get exactly the boxed force. Two derivations, one formula: the weak form says it is a finite element force, the energy says it is conservative. The matrix $V_p^0\mathbf P\mathbf F^T = V_p\boldsymbol\sigma$ is the particle's stress times its current volume; in the code it is called A, and each particle scatters $-\mathbf A\,\nabla w_{ip}$ to its nine nodes.

// A = V_p^0 P(F_p) F_p^T
fixedCorotatedP(F00, F01, F10, F11, mu, la, P);
A00 = V * (P[0] * F00 + P[1] * F01);   A01 = V * (P[0] * F10 + P[1] * F11);
A10 = V * (P[2] * F00 + P[3] * F01);   A11 = V * (P[2] * F10 + P[3] * F11);
// ... then for each of the 3 x 3 nodes, with gx, gy = grad w_ip:
gfx[node] -= A00 * gx + A01 * gy;
gfy[node] -= A10 * gx + A11 * gy;

Why this matters later. The energy view makes the explicit step a special case: it evaluates the force at zero grid motion. Implicit MPM evaluates it at the end of the step, at $\hat x_i = x_i + h v_i^{n+1}$, and minimises $\Phi$ plus a kinetic term; that is the Implicit MPM chapter. Position-based MPM drives $\hat{\mathbf F}_p$ directly toward a target shape; that is the PB-MPM chapter.

The explicit loop, step by step

Now we have everything. One step of explicit MPM, in the order of the course notes, is eight short stages. Everything in the grid is thrown away at the start of each step; only the particles persist.

1. Particle to grid (P2G). Scatter mass and APIC momentum, exactly as in the APIC chapter, with $x_i - x_p$ the offset from the particle to the node:

$$m_i = \sum_p w_{ip}\,m_p, \qquad (m v)_i = \sum_p w_{ip}\,m_p\big(v_p + \mathbf C_p (x_i - x_p)\big).$$

2. Grid velocities. $v_i = (mv)_i / m_i$.

3. Active nodes. Only nodes with $m_i > 0$ take part; the rest keep zero velocity. The course notes advise comparing against exactly zero: a node with a tiny mass may get a large velocity, but a particle reading it back weights it by the same tiny $w_{ip}$.

4. Grid forces. The boxed formula. In our code this happens in the same loop as P2G, since both visit the same nine nodes.

5. Grid velocity update. Symplectic Euler on the grid, then the walls:

$$v_i \leftarrow v_i + h\left(\frac{f_i}{m_i} + g\right).$$

Boundary conditions are applied to node velocities, which is one of MPM's pleasures. A sticky wall sets $v_i = 0$ on nodes near it. A slip wall removes only the velocity component pointing into the wall, so material slides along it and can lift off it. The demo marks the three outermost rows of nodes as wall nodes.

let vx = gvx[node] / m, vy = gvy[node] / m;
vx += h * gfx[node] / m;
vy += h * (gfy[node] / m + gravity);
if (left || right || bottom || top) {
  if (sticky) { vx = 0; vy = 0; }
  else {
    if ((left && vx < 0) || (right && vx > 0)) vx = 0;
    if ((bottom && vy < 0) || (top && vy > 0)) vy = 0;
  }
}

6. Deformation gradient update. Each particle reads the velocity gradient of the new grid velocities and updates its $\mathbf F$, as in the deformation gradient chapter:

$$\nabla v_p = \sum_i v_i\,(\nabla w_{ip})^T, \qquad \mathbf F_p \leftarrow (\mathbf I + h\,\nabla v_p)\,\mathbf F_p.$$

Note that the grid never actually moved. The update applies the deformation that the imagined motion $\hat x_i = x_i + h v_i$ would have caused, which is precisely the $\hat{\mathbf F}_p$ of the energy derivation.

7. Grid to particle (G2P). The APIC gather: $v_p = \sum_i w_{ip} v_i$ and $\mathbf C_p = \frac{4}{\Delta x^2}\sum_i w_{ip}\,v_i (x_i - x_p)^T$, the factor being $\mathbf D_p^{-1}$ for quadratic B-splines.

8. Advection. $x_p \leftarrow x_p + h\,v_p$.

// G2P with the F update. G = grad v_p, accumulated next to v_p and B_p:
G00 += ux * gx;  G01 += ux * gy;  G10 += uy * gx;  G11 += uy * gy;
// ...
const L00 = 1 + h * G00, L01 = h * G01, L10 = h * G10, L11 = 1 + h * G11;   // I + h grad v
pF[k]     = L00 * F00 + L01 * F10;   pF[k + 1] = L00 * F01 + L01 * F11;
pF[k + 2] = L10 * F00 + L11 * F10;   pF[k + 3] = L10 * F01 + L11 * F11;
px[p] += h * vx;  py[p] += h * vy;

That is the whole method. Elasticity lives entirely in the one function that computes $\mathbf P(\mathbf F)$; the rest of the loop does not know whether it is moving jelly, water or, in the next chapter, snow.

Keep particles away from the edge of the grid. A particle reads the 3 by 3 nodes around its base cell. If a particle reaches the outermost cell, the stencil leaves the arrays and you read garbage (in JavaScript, silently undefined, which becomes NaN). The walls stop material three nodes in, and the code also clamps particle positions one cell in, as a last safeguard. The PB-MPM code does the same with what it calls a "guardian" region.

Does it get the physics right? Two checks against closed forms. A 0.2 m jelly block resting on a slip floor should shorten under its own weight by $\rho g H^2 (1-\nu^2) / (2E)$, the integral of the plane-strain strain from top to bottom: with $E = 20$ kPa and $\nu = 0.3$ that is 8.9 mm, and the simulated block's height oscillates around a value 8.7 mm below its rest height. And a resting pool of the demo's water should have $J = 1 - \rho g d / K$ at depth $d$ (next section); averaged over a few seconds, the simulated $J$ matches that line to within half a percent from the surface down to 0.2 m.

  2. Grid velocities: let vx = gvx[node] / m.
  3. Active nodes: if (m <= 0) { ...; continue; }.
  4. Explicit forces, equation (189): the matrix A and the two gfx, gfy lines, inside the P2G loop.
  5. Velocity update, equation (183), plus collision objects (section 12.1, which also notes that a Dirichlet condition on grid nodes is the same thing as a sticky collision): the vx += h * gfx[node] / m lines and the wall block.
  6. F update, equation (181), which is (162) of section 9.4 with $\nabla w$ written out: the L00 to pF[k + 3] lines in the G2P loop.
  7. G2P, equations (175) and (176): vx += w * ux and the B00 to B11 sums, then pC[k] = s * B00 with $s = 4/\Delta x^2$.
  8. Advection: px[p] += h * vx. The notes point out that this simple form is right for PIC and APIC; FLIP would need positions interpolated from the moved grid.

The notes do step 6 before step 7, as we do; both read the same grid velocities, so the order does not matter. The only liberty we take is merging steps 1 and 4 into one loop.

A compact form: MLS-MPM

Look at the two loops. P2G needs $w_{ip}$ for the momentum and $\nabla w_{ip}$ for the force; G2P needs $w_{ip}$ for $v_p$ and $\mathbf C_p$, and $\nabla w_{ip}$ again for $\nabla v_p$. Hu and colleagues (A Moving Least Squares Material Point Method with Displacement Discontinuity and Two-Way Rigid Body Coupling, SIGGRAPH 2018) noticed that APIC already contains a velocity gradient: $\mathbf C_p$ is a least-squares fit of the grid velocities around the particle. If we use it everywhere, two things simplify.

First, the F update uses $\mathbf C_p$ in place of $\nabla v_p$: $\mathbf F_p \leftarrow (\mathbf I + h\,\mathbf C_p) \mathbf F_p$. Second, the gradient of the weights in the force is replaced by its moving least squares approximation,

$$\nabla w_{ip} \;\approx\; \frac{4}{\Delta x^2}\, w_{ip}\,(x_i - x_p),$$

which is exact for the linear part of any field because, for quadratic B-splines, $\sum_i w_{ip}(x_i - x_p) = 0$ and $\sum_i w_{ip}(x_i - x_p)(x_i-x_p)^T = \frac14\Delta x^2\,\mathbf I$. The force on node $i$ now has the same shape as the affine momentum term, so both fold into one line of P2G:

$$(mv)_i \mathrel{+}= w_{ip}\Big[m_p v_p + \Big(m_p\mathbf C_p - \tfrac{4}{\Delta x^2}\,h\,V_p^0\,\mathbf P\mathbf F_p^T\Big)(x_i - x_p)\Big],$$

and the grid update is only $v_i = (mv)_i/m_i + h g$ plus walls. The particle's stress has become part of its affine momentum. No $\nabla w$ is computed anywhere, the force loop disappears, and the whole method fits in about a hundred lines. That is why most modern MPM codes, PB-MPM included, use this form; the plasticity and PB-MPM chapters use it too.

// MLS-MPM P2G: the stress enters the affine momentum.
const s = 4 * invDx * invDx;
const Q00 = pm[p] * C00 - h * s * A00, Q01 = pm[p] * C01 - h * s * A01;
const Q10 = pm[p] * C10 - h * s * A10, Q11 = pm[p] * C11 - h * s * A11;
// for each node:
gvx[node] += w * (pm[p] * pvx[p] + Q00 * rx + Q01 * ry);
gvy[node] += w * (pm[p] * pvy[p] + Q10 * rx + Q11 * ry);

Try it: in your finished solution, replace the force lines with this, drop h * gfx[node] / m from the grid update (keep gravity), and use pC instead of G in the F update. The jelly behaves the same to the eye. The two forms are not identical, since the MLS gradient only approximates $\nabla w$. But the F update and the force now use the same approximate gradient, so if you repeat the energy derivation above with $\frac{4}{\Delta x^2} w_{ip}(x_i - x_p)$ in place of $\nabla w_{ip}$, you find that the MLS force is still exactly minus the gradient of the discrete elastic energy.

How large can the time step be?

Raise the time step slider in the demo and the jelly eventually explodes, just like the spring of the time integration chapter. Explicit MPM has two limits, and you should know both by heart.

The CFL limit. A particle must not cross more than about one cell per step, or the grid cannot see it coming: $h < \Delta x / v_{\max}$, where $v_{\max}$ is the fastest particle. For a jelly block falling half a metre, $v_{\max} \approx \sqrt{2 g \cdot 0.5} = 3.1$ m/s and $\Delta x / v_{\max} = 5$ ms. This is rarely the limit that bites.

The elastic wave limit. Elastic materials carry sound. A disturbance travels through a bar at $c = \sqrt{E/\rho}$, and a compression wave through a 2D or 3D solid at

$$c = \sqrt{\frac{\lambda + 2\mu}{\rho}},$$

with $\lambda$ and $\mu$ the Lamé parameters. In one time step, information on the grid moves one stencil width at most; physics must not move it further. So the time step must satisfy

$$h \;\lesssim\; C\,\frac{\Delta x}{c},$$

with a constant $C$ of order one. It is the MPM version of $h\omega < 2$ from the time integration chapter: the stiffest mode of the grid oscillates with a frequency $\omega_{\max}$ proportional to $c/\Delta x$, and symplectic Euler needs $h\,\omega_{\max} < 2$. For a 1D grid with two particles per cell, the eigenvalues of the lumped mass and stiffness matrices give $\omega_{\max} = 1.15\,c/\Delta x$, so $C = 1.73$. In 2D, and at the surface of a body, where edge nodes carry tiny masses, it is lower. Measured in the demo:

jelly, $E$ = 100 kPa, $\nu$ = 0.311.6 m/s1.35 ms1.27 ms0.94

The rule of thumb $h \approx \Delta x / c$ is right to within a factor of two, which is what you need to choose a step. The table also shows why explicit MPM is expensive for stiff materials: $c$ grows with $\sqrt E$, so a material a hundred times stiffer needs ten times more steps. Real water has a bulk modulus of 2.2 GPa, so $c$ = 1,480 m/s, and on our grid $\Delta x / c = 11$ µs: about 2,600 steps per frame. That is why simulated water is made compressible on purpose, and why the next chapters look for methods whose time step does not depend on the stiffness.

Read the readouts. The demo prints $h c/\Delta x$ and $h\,v_{\max}/\Delta x$ every frame. When the first goes much above 1 for a solid, or about 0.6 for the water, expect an explosion. When it explodes, the energy appears in the highest grid mode first: a checkerboard of particles shaking against each other, then everything flies apart.

Water is a material too

A liquid at rest resists being compressed but not being sheared. The simplest way to say that in MPM is an equation of state: pressure as a function of the volume ratio $J$ alone. Take the energy density

$$\Psi(J) = \frac{K}{2}\,(J - 1)^2,$$

where $K$ is the bulk modulus (Pa). This is the $\lambda$ term of the fixed corotated model with $\mu = 0$: no resistance to shape change. Its stress follows from $\partial J / \partial \mathbf F = J\mathbf F^{-T}$:

$$\mathbf P = K (J-1)\,J\,\mathbf F^{-T}, \qquad \mathbf P\mathbf F^T = K(J-1)J\,\mathbf I, \qquad p = -K(J-1).$$

Squeeze the water ($J < 1$) and the pressure $p$ is positive; the force on the nodes pushes outward. Because only $J$ matters, water particles do not need $\mathbf F$ at all; they store $J$ and update it with the same velocity gradient, $J \leftarrow \det(\mathbf I + h\nabla v_p)\, J$. That also avoids a real problem: a fluid's $\mathbf F$ is sheared without limit as it flows, and would soon be numerically meaningless.

function waterPFt(J, K) {
  return K * (J - 1) * J;        // P F^T = s I
}
// in P2G, for water particles: A = V_p^0 s I

The bulk modulus is a compromise. A pool of depth $d$ should have $p = \rho g d$ at the bottom, so it is compressed to $J = 1 - \rho g d / K$. With the demo's $K$ = 30 kPa, a 0.2 m pool is squeezed by 6.5 % at the bottom: visibly squashy, but with a wave speed of 5.5 m/s and a comfortable 1 ms step. Make $K$ ten times larger and the water is ten times stiffer and needs a step about three times smaller. This kind of fluid is called weakly compressible. Many codes use a stiffer-than-linear law (the Tait equation, $p \propto J^{-\gamma} - 1$ with $\gamma = 7$) to keep compression small without raising $K$ too much; the idea is the same.

Because jelly and water go through the same grid, the "jelly into water" scene needs no coupling code at all. A node that receives mass from both materials simply adds their momenta and forces. This automatic coupling, and the automatic self-collision it implies, is why MPM is so popular for scenes where materials mix.

Choosing the material model

The demo offers both elastic models from the hyperelasticity chapter. Fixed corotated measures the distance from $\mathbf F$ to its closest rotation $\mathbf R$; neo-Hookean uses invariants and $\log J$:

$$\mathbf P_{\text{FCR}} = 2\mu(\mathbf F - \mathbf R) + \lambda (J-1) J \mathbf F^{-T}, \qquad \mathbf P_{\text{NH}} = \mu(\mathbf F - \mathbf F^{-T}) + \lambda \log J\,\mathbf F^{-T}.$$

In 2D the closest rotation is cheap: it is the rotation by $\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(F_{10} - F_{01},\, F_{00} + F_{11})$, and $J\mathbf F^{-T}$ is the cofactor matrix $\begin{pmatrix} F_{11} & -F_{10} \\ -F_{01} & F_{00}\end{pmatrix}$, which needs no division. For small deformations the two models agree. They differ when a particle is crushed: neo-Hookean's $\log J$ goes to minus infinity as $J \to 0$, a barrier that resists inversion with unbounded force, while fixed corotated stays finite and lets a particle invert and recover. In an explicit code the barrier is a liability, since an unbounded force means an unbounded wave speed, so neo-Hookean particles need a clamp on $J$.

pF[k + 2] = L10 * F00 + L11 * F10;

switch to neo-Hookean

Fill in neoHookeanP and select it in the jelly model menu. Compare the two models when the blocks land on each other at $E$ = 20 kPa. Then drop the modulus to 2 kPa, so that the blocks' own weight is enough to squash them, and compare again. Which model lets a block flatten more, and why?

Solution 
function neoHookeanP(F00, F01, F10, F11, mu, la, P) {
  const J = Math.max(F00 * F11 - F01 * F10, 1e-4);   // the energy is infinite for J <= 0; clamp
  const iJ = 1 / J;
  const T00 = F11 * iJ, T01 = -F10 * iJ, T10 = -F01 * iJ, T11 = F00 * iJ;   // F^-T
  const logJ = Math.log(J);
  P[0] = mu * (F00 - T00) + la * logJ * T00;
  P[1] = mu * (F01 - T01) + la * logJ * T01;
  P[2] = mu * (F10 - T10) + la * logJ * T10;
  P[3] = mu * (F11 - T11) + la * logJ * T11;
}

Under strong compression the $\log J$ barrier makes neo-Hookean stiffer, so its blocks flatten less. In our runs, when the orange block lands on the red one at 20 kPa, the red block's height drops from 0.195 m to 0.104 m with fixed corotated and to 0.135 m with neo-Hookean. At 2 kPa, the fixed corotated block is crushed almost flat, while the neo-Hookean one keeps 9 cm. At small strains the two models agree.

$J = 1 - \rho g d / K = 1 - 1000 \cdot 9.81 \cdot 0.2 / 30000 = 0.935$. For the Tait law, $\mathbf P\mathbf F^T = -\frac{K}{7}(J^{-7} - 1)\,J\,\mathbf I$; near $J = 1$ it has the same slope as the linear law, so the same small-strain wave speed, but it stiffens quickly under compression, so splashes need a somewhat smaller step.

Solution 
function waterPFt(J, K) {
  return K * (J - 1) * J;
}

What explicit MPM costs

Each step visits every particle twice, nine nodes each time, and every grid node once. With 2,000 particles and 17 steps per frame, that is about 600,000 particle-node visits per frame, a few milliseconds in JavaScript. The cost per step barely depends on the material. The number of steps does, through $c$, and that is the whole story of the next three chapters: plasticity changes $\mathbf P$ but not the loop, implicit MPM pays more per step to take far fewer, and position-based MPM changes what the loop solves so that any step is stable.

Summary

  • An MPM particle is a piece of material with fixed mass $m_p = \rho V_p^0$ and initial volume $V_p^0$; it carries $x_p$, $v_p$, $\mathbf C_p$ and $\mathbf F_p$, and acts as a quadrature point for integrals over the body.
  • The grid force is $f_i = -\sum_p V_p^0\mathbf P(\mathbf F_p)\mathbf F_p^T\nabla w_{ip}$. It comes from the weak form of Newton's law, and equally from differentiating the elastic energy $\sum_p V_p^0\Psi(\hat{\mathbf F}_p)$ with respect to imagined grid motion.
  • One explicit step: P2G, grid velocities, active nodes, grid forces, velocity update with gravity and walls, $\mathbf F_p \leftarrow (\mathbf I + h\nabla v_p)\mathbf F_p$, G2P, advection. Boundary conditions are set on node velocities.
  • MLS-MPM uses $\mathbf C_p$ as the velocity gradient and folds the stress into the affine momentum, so no weight gradients are needed.
  • Explicit MPM is stable while $h \lesssim C\Delta x/c$, with $c = \sqrt{(\lambda+2\mu)/\rho}$ and $C$ about 1, and while particles move less than a cell per step.
  • A weakly compressible fluid is the energy $\frac K2 (J-1)^2$, with only $J$ stored per particle. Mixed materials couple automatically through the grid.
