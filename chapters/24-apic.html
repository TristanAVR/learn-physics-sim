APIC

Give each particle a small matrix, and the grid stops forgetting how the material spins and shears.

The last chapter left us with a choice nobody likes. PIC sends everything through the grid, which keeps it stable but makes it forget: a spinning disk loses $(\Delta x/R)^2$ of its angular momentum every step. FLIP keeps the particles' own velocities, which removes the forgetting but lets noise the grid cannot see pile up. Blends split the difference and need tuning.

The Affine Particle-In-Cell method (APIC) of Jiang, Schroeder, Selle, Teran and Stomakhin (2015) removes the choice. Its observation is that PIC's dissipation is not caused by the grid; it is caused by the particle being too simple to receive what the grid sends it. Nine nodes describe how the velocity varies across a particle's neighbourhood, and PIC keeps only their average. Let the particle keep a little more, an affine velocity field described by one 2×2 matrix, and three things happen: rigid rotations and shears survive the round trip exactly, angular momentum is conserved to the last digit, and PIC's filtering, with its stability, is untouched.

APIC is also the transfer used by essentially every modern MPM code, including MLS-MPM and PB-MPM. By the end of this chapter you will have implemented it, derived its one mysterious matrix, and proved its conservation law with the demo as your witness.

What a particle throws away

In G2P a particle at $x_p$ reads velocities $\tilde v_i$ from the nine nodes around it. Suppose you had to summarise those nine vectors with a small formula valid near $x_p$. A constant, $v_p$, is what PIC uses. The next best thing is a linear function of position, an affine field:

$$v(x) \approx v_p + \mathbf{C}_p\,(x - x_p).$$

The 2×2 matrix $\mathbf{C}_p$ is a local velocity gradient: it can express a rotation (its skew-symmetric part), a shear or a stretch (its symmetric part). A rigidly spinning disk has exactly such a field everywhere, with $\mathbf{C} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\omega \\ \omega & 0 \end{pmatrix}$, which is why PIC's failure on the disk was so clean: the information PIC discarded was precisely $\mathbf{C}$.

A first attempt: RPIC

The paper starts with something smaller, which is a good way to see the idea. Rigid PIC (RPIC, section 5.2) stores only the part of the local field that PIC visibly loses: its spin. In G2P each particle also records the angular momentum of its stencil about itself (APIC eq. 4):

$$L_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\,(x_i - x_p) \times m_p\,\tilde v_i.$$

That is exactly the quantity we computed in the last chapter as "what PIC throws away", so with it stored, G2P conserves angular momentum trivially (eq. 5 adds $L_p$ to the particle total). In P2G the particle must hand the spin back. Treat it as a tiny rigid body with angular velocity $\omega_p = L_p / K_p$, whose velocity at node $i$ is $v_p + \omega_p \times (x_i - x_p)$ (eq. 6). The inertia $K_p$ is that of the masses $w_{ip} m_p$ placed at the nodes (eq. 7); in 2D it is a single number,

$$K_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\, m_p\, |x_i - x_p|^2 = m_p\, \frac{\Delta x^2}{2} \quad \text{(quadratic weights)}.$$

RPIC conserves angular momentum exactly, and it spins the disk perfectly. But rotation is only one of the things a 2×2 velocity gradient can do. Shear is still averaged away, so a flow that shears, such as two blobs sliding past each other, still loses energy almost as fast as with PIC. You can see this in the demo, and implementing RPIC is the last exercise.

The APIC transfers

APIC stores the whole affine field. Each particle carries $v_p$ and a matrix, and its particle-to-grid transfer uses the affine velocity at each node instead of the constant one (eq. 8):

$$m_i = \sum_p w_{ip}\, m_p, \qquad m_i v_i = \sum_p w_{ip}\, m_p \left(v_p + \mathbf{B}_p \mathbf{D}_p^{-1}(x_i - x_p)\right).$$

The matrix is written as a product: $\mathbf{C}_p = \mathbf{B}_p\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}$, where

$$\mathbf{D}_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\,(x_i - x_p)(x_i - x_p)^\mathsf{T} \qquad \text{(eq. 9)}$$

depends only on where the particle sits relative to the grid, and $\mathbf{B}_p$ is what the grid-to-particle transfer gathers (eq. 10):

$$v_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\,\tilde v_i, \qquad \mathbf{B}_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\,\tilde v_i\,(x_i - x_p)^\mathsf{T}.$$

The velocity is read exactly as in PIC. The new part is $\mathbf{B}_p$, a weighted sum of outer products: node velocity times node offset. If the nodes to the right of the particle move up and those to the left move down, $\mathbf{B}_p$ picks that up as a positive $(y, x)$ entry, which is a counter-clockwise spin.

Where $\mathbf{D}_p$ comes from

Why divide by $\mathbf{D}_p$? Because it is exactly what is needed to make affine fields survive. Suppose the particles' velocities all come from one affine field, $v(x) = a + \mathbf{C}x$, and each particle stores the matching $\mathbf{C}_p = \mathbf{C}$. P2G gives each node

$$m_i v_i = \sum_p w_{ip} m_p \left(a + \mathbf{C}x_p + \mathbf{C}(x_i - x_p)\right) = m_i\,(a + \mathbf{C}x_i),$$

the exact field at the node, for every node with mass, whatever the particle spacing. Now G2P. Linear reproduction gives $v_p = \sum_i w_{ip}(a + \mathbf{C}x_i) = a + \mathbf{C}x_p$, exact. And

$$\mathbf{B}_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\,(a + \mathbf{C}x_p + \mathbf{C}(x_i - x_p))(x_i - x_p)^\mathsf{T} = (a + \mathbf{C}x_p)\underbrace{\sum_i w_{ip}(x_i - x_p)^\mathsf{T}}_{0} + \mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{D}_p,$$

so $\mathbf{B}_p\mathbf{D}_p^{-1} = \mathbf{C}$, exact. $\mathbf{B}_p$ is the velocity gradient "blurred" by the stencil, and $\mathbf{D}_p$, the second moment of the weights, undoes the blur. Another way to say the same thing: $v_p$ and $\mathbf{C}_p$ are the weighted least-squares fit of an affine field to the nine node velocities. Minimise $\sum_i w_{ip}|\tilde v_i - v_p - \mathbf{C}(x_i - x_p)|^2$, and the normal equations are $v_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\tilde v_i$ and $\mathbf{C}\mathbf{D}_p = \mathbf{B}_p$.

Select APIC and the rotating disk: the "L / L0 - 1" readout sits at about $10^{-15}$, and the spoke turns at a steady rate forever. Switch to "colliding blobs": two blobs of particles meet off-centre and splash. PIC turns them into a slow, sticky lump. FLIP sprays particles. APIC splashes and swirls, and its angular momentum stays at 1 to round-off until the first particle reaches a wall, at about 0.7 s (walls push, so they can exert torque).

$\mathbf{D}_p$ is a constant

$\mathbf{D}_p$ looks expensive: a 2×2 matrix to build from nine nodes, then invert, for every particle every step. For B-spline kernels it is neither. In 2D the weight is a product $w = N_x N_y$, so

$$(\mathbf{D}_p)_{xy} = \Big(\sum_a N_x^a\, r_x^a\Big)\Big(\sum_b N_y^b\, r_y^b\Big) = 0,$$

since each factor is $\sum w\,(x_i - x_p)$ along one axis, which linear reproduction makes zero. The diagonal entry is a 1D sum, $\sum_a N^a (r^a)^2$ (the other factor sums to 1). Measure positions in cells and write the particle's offset from the middle node as $t = f - 1 \in [-\tfrac12, \tfrac12)$. Its distances to the three nodes are $-1 - t$, $-t$ and $1 - t$, with weights $\tfrac12(\tfrac12 - t)^2$, $\tfrac34 - t^2$ and $\tfrac12(\tfrac12 + t)^2$. Then

$$\sum_a N^a (r^a)^2 = \tfrac12(\tfrac12 - t)^2(1 + t)^2 + (\tfrac34 - t^2)\,t^2 + \tfrac12(\tfrac12 + t)^2(1 - t)^2.$$

Write $(\tfrac12 - t)(1 + t) = A - B$ and $(\tfrac12 + t)(1 - t) = A + B$ with $A = \tfrac12 - t^2$ and $B = \tfrac t2$. The outer two terms add up to $A^2 + B^2 = \tfrac14 - \tfrac34 t^2 + t^4$, the middle one is $\tfrac34 t^2 - t^4$, and the total is exactly $\tfrac14$, for every $t$. So, back in metres,

$$\mathbf{D}_p = \tfrac14\,\Delta x^2\,\mathbf{I} \quad\text{(quadratic)}, \qquad \mathbf{D}_p = \tfrac13\,\Delta x^2\,\mathbf{I} \quad\text{(cubic)},$$

independent of where the particle sits, and $\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}$ is the constant $4/\Delta x^2$. The code never forms $\mathbf{D}_p$; it stores $\mathbf{C}_p$ directly:

// G2P, inside the 3x3 stencil loop: r = x_i - x_p
b00 += w * vx * rx; b01 += w * vx * ry;
b10 += w * vy * rx; b11 += w * vy * ry;
// after the loop: C_p = B_p D_p^-1 = (4 / dx^2) B_p
const DInv = 4 / (dx * dx);
pC[4 * p] = b00 * DInv; pC[4 * p + 1] = b01 * DInv; pC[4 * p + 2] = b10 * DInv; pC[4 * p + 3] = b11 * DInv;

// P2G, inside the stencil loop: the affine velocity at the node
gvx[node] += mw * (pvx[p] + c00 * rx + c01 * ry);
gvy[node] += mw * (pvy[p] + c10 * rx + c11 * ry);

The tent (linear) kernel is the exception. Its 1D second moment is $t(1 - t)$ for a particle a fraction $t$ of a cell past a node, which is zero when the particle sits on a node: $\mathbf{D}_p$ becomes singular. The paper points out the way around it. For the tent kernel, $w_{ip}\,\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}(x_i - x_p) = \nabla w_{ip}$ (in 1D, in cells: $(1 - t)(-t)/(t(1 - t)) = -1$, the tent's slope), so the affine term can be written with weight gradients and no inverse at all. The MPM course notes give that version as eqs. (177)-(180).

Angular momentum, conserved exactly

Where does a particle's spin live in APIC? Define the particle state's angular momentum as the grid angular momentum that P2G would produce from it (the paper's eq. 11). With $r_i = x_i - x_p$,

$$\sum_i x_i \times m_i v_i = \sum_p m_p \sum_i w_{ip}\,(x_p + r_i) \times (v_p + \mathbf{C}_p r_i).$$

Expand. The term $x_p \times v_p$ survives (weights sum to 1). The cross terms $\sum w\, r_i \times v_p$ and $x_p \times \mathbf{C}_p\sum w\, r_i$ vanish (linear reproduction). The last term is $\sum_i w_{ip}\, r_i \times \mathbf{C}_p r_i$, which in 2D, using $\sum w r_x^2 = \sum w r_y^2 = \Delta x^2/4$ and $\sum w r_x r_y = 0$, equals $\tfrac{\Delta x^2}{4}(C_{yx} - C_{xy}) = B_{yx} - B_{xy}$. So

$$L = \sum_p m_p \left( x_p \times v_p + (B_{p,yx} - B_{p,xy}) \right).$$

The spin of a particle is the skew-symmetric part of $\mathbf{B}_p$, the paper's $(\mathbf{B}_p)^\mathsf{T} : \boldsymbol\epsilon$ in 3D. P2G conserves it by construction. For G2P, substitute the new $v_p$ and $\mathbf{B}_p$: $B_{yx} - B_{xy} = \sum_i w_{ip}\, r_i \times \tilde v_i$, so a particle's contribution is $m_p \sum_i w_{ip}(x_p + r_i) \times \tilde v_i = \sum_i w_{ip} m_p\, x_i \times \tilde v_i$, and summing over particles gives $\sum_i m_i\, x_i \times \tilde v_i$, the grid's angular momentum. Exactly. Finally, advection $x_p \leftarrow x_p + h v_p$ does not change $x_p \times v_p$, because $v_p \times v_p = 0$. So in a whole APIC step, angular momentum can change only through the grid forces. The demo's two force fields were chosen to exert no torque (a pull toward the centre, and a pressure gradient by central differences), so its readout measures the transfers alone.

$\mathbf{D}_p$ is "analogous to an inertia tensor". Compare with RPIC: there, spin = $K_p\,\omega_p$. Here, $\mathbf{B}_p = \mathbf{C}_p\mathbf{D}_p$, with $\mathbf{C}_p$ playing the role of the angular velocity and $\mathbf{D}_p$ the inertia. They are not identical (in 2D, RPIC's $K_p = m_p\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D}_p$), which is why the paper says $\mathbf{D}_p$ does not strictly have the properties of an inertia tensor. But the analogy is how to remember what $\mathbf{B}_p$ means.

Noise, dissipation, and what APIC does not fix

APIC's G2P still rebuilds every particle's state entirely from the grid, so it keeps PIC's filtering. Press "add particle noise" with APIC: 97 % of the added kinetic energy is gone one step later, against 98 % for PIC and none for FLIP. The noise FLIP accumulates has nowhere to hide. Yet affine motions, which include rotations and uniform shears, pass through unharmed. What APIC still loses is the part of the velocity that is curved on the scale of a cell, which the grid could not represent anyway.

The colliding blobs make the comparison concrete. Half a second after the start (frame 30), the fraction of kinetic energy left was 13 % for PIC, 29 % for RPIC, 83 % for the 0.95 FLIP blend, 84 % for APIC, and 119 % for pure FLIP: FLIP had gained energy, through noise. The paper's Figures 13 and 14 make similar measurements on an oscillating elastic disk: APIC's transfers lose energy at a much lower rate per frame than PIC's, but over a long run they still remove a large share of it (82 % against PIC's 95 %), and FLIP often keeps more energy than APIC, hidden in its null space. APIC's limitations are the honest flip side. It neither improves nor worsens the ringing instability itself (paper, section 9), it does not conserve energy exactly, and it costs four more numbers per particle in 2D (nine in 3D), which in practice is negligible.

Moving the particles

After G2P, APIC particles move with their new velocity, $x_p \leftarrow x_p + h\,v_p$, as in PIC. The MPM course notes point out (section 10.5, step 8) that this is the same as moving the particle with the grid, $x_p = \sum_i w_{ip}(x_i + h\tilde v_i)$, by linear reproduction; for FLIP the two differ and the grid version is the consistent one. The affine matrix does not move the particle, but it does more than carry spin: $\mathbf{C}_p$ is an estimate of the velocity gradient $\nabla v$ at the particle, the very matrix the deformation gradient update of chapter 21 needs.

From APIC to MLS-MPM and PB-MPM

That last remark is the seed of two later methods. The MLS-MPM method of Hu et al. (2018) noticed that if $\mathbf{C}_p$ already approximates $\nabla v$, the MPM deformation update can use it directly, $\mathbf{F}_p \leftarrow (\mathbf{I} + h\mathbf{C}_p)\mathbf{F}_p$, and the stress force can be folded into the same affine P2G term. That makes an MPM step barely longer than an APIC transfer, and it is the form you will meet in most compact MPM codes.

PB-MPM (Lewin 2024) goes one step further. It transfers displacements over the step instead of velocities: each particle carries $d_p = h\,v_p$ and an affine matrix gathered from grid displacements with exactly the APIC formula, including the factor 4 that is $\Delta x^2\,\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}$ in grid units (the open-source code literally multiplies its $\mathbf{B}$ by 4). PB-MPM calls that matrix $\mathbf{D}$, the deformation displacement; on this site we write $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p = h\,\mathbf{C}_p$. Do not confuse it with APIC's $\mathbf{D}_p$, the constant second-moment matrix of this chapter. The two papers use the same letter for unrelated things.

Summary

  • APIC gives each particle an affine velocity field $v_p + \mathbf{C}_p(x - x_p)$, with $\mathbf{C}_p = \mathbf{B}_p\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}$.
  • P2G: $m_i v_i = \sum_p w_{ip} m_p (v_p + \mathbf{C}_p(x_i - x_p))$. G2P: $v_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\tilde v_i$, $\mathbf{B}_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\tilde v_i(x_i - x_p)^\mathsf{T}$.
  • $\mathbf{D}_p = \sum_i w_{ip}(x_i - x_p)(x_i - x_p)^\mathsf{T}$ is $\tfrac14\Delta x^2\mathbf{I}$ for quadratic and $\tfrac13\Delta x^2\mathbf{I}$ for cubic B-splines, independent of position; for the tent kernel use weight gradients.
  • Affine velocity fields survive a round trip exactly; G2P is a weighted least-squares affine fit.
  • Total angular momentum, counting each particle's spin $m_p(B_{yx} - B_{xy})$, is conserved exactly by both transfers and by advection.
  • APIC keeps PIC's filtering, so no FLIP-style noise; RPIC conserves angular momentum but still damps shear.
  • $\mathbf{C}_p$ estimates $\nabla v$; MLS-MPM and PB-MPM build on that. PB-MPM's $\mathbf{D}$ is a different matrix.
