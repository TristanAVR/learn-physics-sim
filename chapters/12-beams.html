Bending, Twisting, Buckling

What makes a rod a rod: three stiffnesses, two classic formulas, and one constraint per joint.

A rope and a fishing rod are both long and thin, and a chain of particles joined by distance constraints is a fine rope. It is a terrible fishing rod. Hold it out horizontally and it falls straight down, because nothing in it resists bending. Real rods resist bending, resist twisting, and almost refuse to stretch, and the three resistances differ by factors of hundreds of thousands. Getting those ratios right is most of what makes a simulated rod look like steel, carbon fibre or nylon instead of wet string.

This chapter is about the physics that engineers have used for rods since the eighteenth century: the Euler-Bernoulli beam. It gives you closed-form answers for how far a rod droops, how much it twists and when it suddenly buckles. Those answers are the test cases for everything we build in the next two chapters. Then we turn the physics into one XPBD constraint per joint, and find out what its compliance must be.

By the end you will be able to compute a rod's bending stiffness from its material and diameter, predict the deflection of a cantilever and the load at which a column buckles, build a particle rod that reproduces both, and explain why a joint at a change of stiffness needs a different compliance from the joints around it.

What bending is

Bend a rod into a gentle arc. Its centreline, the line through the middle of every cross-section, becomes a curve, and we describe how sharply it curves by the curvature $\kappa$ (kappa): one over the radius of the circle that fits the curve locally, $\kappa = 1/R$, in units of 1/m. Equivalently, if $\theta(s)$ is the angle of the centreline's tangent at arc length $s$ (the distance along the rod from one end), then

$$\kappa = \frac{d\theta}{ds}.$$

A straight rod has $\kappa = 0$; a rod bent into a 10 cm circle has $\kappa = 10$ m-1.

Now look inside. When the rod bends, the material on the outside of the curve is stretched and the material on the inside is compressed. Somewhere in between is the neutral axis, which keeps its length (for a symmetric cross-section it is the centreline). A fibre of material at distance $y$ from the neutral axis lies on a circle of radius $R - y$ instead of $R$, so its length changes by the fraction

$$\varepsilon = -\frac{y}{R} = -\kappa\, y.$$

This is the strain: change in length over original length. It grows linearly from zero at the centre to its largest value at the surface. The material answers with a stress (force per area) proportional to the strain, $\sigma = E\varepsilon$, where $E$ is Young's modulus, the stiffness of the material itself in pascals (N/m2). Each fibre's force, $\sigma\,dA$, acts at lever arm $y$, so together they produce a turning effect, the bending moment:

$$M = -\int_A \sigma\, y \, dA = E\kappa \int_A y^2\, dA = EI\,\kappa.$$

That is the central law of this chapter. Moment equals bending stiffness times curvature, $M = EI\kappa$, the rod's version of Hooke's law. The moment has units of N·m; the bending stiffness $EI$ has units of N·m2. It is called the Euler-Bernoulli model, and it assumes that cross-sections stay flat and perpendicular to the centreline, that strains are small (curvatures can still be large: a steel wire coiled into a spring is strained only near its surface), and that the material is linear. All three hold well for thin rods.

Where $EI$ comes from

$EI$ is a product of two factors with very different personalities. $E$ belongs to the material. $I$, the second moment of area, belongs to the shape of the cross-section:

$$I = \int_A y^2\, dA.$$

For a solid round rod of diameter $d$, and for a round tube of outer diameter $D$ and inner diameter $d$,

$$I_{\text{rod}} = \frac{\pi d^4}{64}, \qquad I_{\text{tube}} = \frac{\pi (D^4 - d^4)}{64}.$$

(For a rectangle of width $b$ and height $t$, bending across the height, it is $b t^3/12$.) The fourth power is the important part. Double the diameter of a rod and it becomes sixteen times stiffer in bending. That is also why tubes are everywhere: the material near the centre contributes almost nothing to $I$, because $y^2$ is small there, so you can remove it and keep most of the stiffness. A steel tube of 10 mm outside and 8 mm inside has $I = 2.90\times 10^{-10}$ m4. A solid rod with the same cross-sectional area (and therefore the same weight) has a diameter of 6 mm and $I = 6.36\times 10^{-11}$ m4: the tube is 4.6 times stiffer for the same mass.

Some values of $E$, to calibrate your intuition: steel about 200 GPa, aluminium 70 GPa, glass-fibre composite around 40 GPa, nylon 2 to 3 GPa, soft rubber a few MPa. Throughout this chapter we use one worked example: a steel rod 1 m long and 5 mm in diameter.

$$I = \frac{\pi (0.005)^4}{64} = 3.07\times 10^{-11}\ \text{m}^4, \qquad EI = 200\times 10^9 \times 3.07\times 10^{-11} = 6.14\ \text{N m}^2.$$

The cantilever

Clamp one end of the rod in a wall so that it sticks out horizontally (a cantilever), and hang a weight $P$ (a force, in newtons) on the free end. How far does the tip go down?

Cut the rod at arc length $s$ from the wall. The part beyond the cut has the load $P$ at distance $L - s$ from the cut, so the moment there is $M(s) = P\,(L - s)$: largest at the wall, zero at the tip. The curvature follows from $M = EI\kappa$. For small deflections the tangent angle $\theta$ is the slope of the rod, and the deflection $\delta$ is the integral of the slope, so we integrate twice, starting from the wall where both are zero:

$$\theta(s) = \int_0^s \frac{P(L - u)}{EI}\,du = \frac{P}{EI}\left(Ls - \tfrac12 s^2\right), \qquad \delta(s) = \int_0^s \theta(u)\,du = \frac{P}{6EI}\left(3Ls^2 - s^3\right).$$

At the tip, $s = L$:

$$\delta_{\text{tip}} = \frac{P L^3}{3EI}.$$

If instead the rod sags under its own weight, a distributed load $w = \rho A g$ newtons per metre ($\rho$ the density, $A$ the cross-sectional area), the moment is $M(s) = \tfrac12 w (L - s)^2$ and the same two integrations give

$$\delta_{\text{tip}} = \frac{w L^4}{8EI}.$$

For our steel rod, a 1 N load on the tip moves it down $1/(3 \times 6.14) = 54$ mm. Its own weight is $w = 7850 \times 1.96\times 10^{-5} \times 9.81 = 1.51$ N/m, which sags the tip by $1.51/(8 \times 6.14) = 31$ mm. Both formulas are linear in the load, so you can add them.

Notice how the length enters: $L^3$ for a tip load, $L^4$ for self-weight. A rod twice as long sags sixteen times as much under its own weight. Rewrite the self-weight formula as

$$\frac{\delta_{\text{tip}}}{L} = \frac18 \left(\frac{L}{\ell_g}\right)^3, \qquad \ell_g = \left(\frac{EI}{\rho A g}\right)^{1/3}.$$

The elastogravity length $\ell_g$ is the natural length scale of a rod in gravity. A rod much shorter than $\ell_g$ sticks out nearly straight; a rod much longer hangs down like a rope, whatever it is made of. At $L = \ell_g$ the tip sags by an eighth of the length. Our steel rod has $\ell_g = (6.14 / 1.51)^{1/3} = 1.60$ m, so at 1 m it is comfortably rod-like; at 5 m it would flop. This one number tells you, before any simulation, whether a rod in your scene should behave like a stick or a noodle.

The demo below is a 1 m rod of mass 0.1 kg/m with $EI = 1.25$ N m2, which gives $\ell_g = 1.08$ m and a self-weight sag of $0.981/(8 \times 1.25) = 98.1$ mm. The red dashed line is the beam theory curve; the blue rod is the simulation you are about to write. Change the number of segments, add a tip load, and then switch the scene to buckling.

With 10 segments and self-weight only, the simulated tip sits within half a percent of 98.1 mm. Add a large tip load and the two start to part: the formulas above assume the slope is small, and when the tip drops by a fifth of the length the real rod is already shortening its horizontal reach. The simulation, which makes no such assumption, is the more accurate of the two there.

Twisting and stretching

A rod has two more ways to deform, and each comes with its own stiffness.

Torsion. Twist one end of a rod relative to the other with a torque $T$ about its axis and it twists at a uniform twist rate $\tau$, in radians per metre:

$$T = GJ\,\tau.$$

$G$ is the shear modulus of the material, related to Young's modulus through Poisson's ratio $\nu$ by $G = E/(2(1+\nu))$, and $J$ is the polar second moment of area: for a round section $J = \pi d^4/32 = 2I$. So for a round rod

$$\frac{GJ}{EI} = \frac{2G}{E} = \frac{1}{1+\nu} \approx 0.77 \quad (\nu = 0.3).$$

For a round rod, twisting and bending stiffness are similar, and you cannot ignore either. Our steel rod has $GJ = 4.72$ N m2; a 0.1 N·m torque twists it by $0.1/4.72 = 0.021$ rad per metre, about 1.2°/m. Non-circular and built-up sections (an open channel, a braided cable, a ribbon) can have very different ratios, which is why we keep $GJ$ as its own number.

Stretching. Pull on the ends with a force $F$ and the rod stretches by $F L / (EA)$, where $EA$ is the axial stiffness. For our steel rod $EA = 200\times 10^9 \times 1.96 \times 10^{-5} = 3.9\times 10^6$ N, so 1 N stretches the metre of rod by a quarter of a micrometre. Compare the compliances, the deflection per newton, of the two ways of loading the same rod:

$$\frac{L^3/(3EI)}{L/(EA)} = \frac{AL^2}{3I} = \frac{16}{3}\left(\frac{L}{d}\right)^2 = 213{,}000.$$

The rod is two hundred thousand times easier to bend sideways than to stretch, and the ratio grows with the square of the slenderness $L/d$. This is the single most important fact for simulating rods: they are effectively inextensible but freely bendable. In practice we keep segment lengths with constraints that are as stiff as the solver allows, and spend our care on bending and twisting.

Twist only becomes visible in three dimensions, where a rod can curve out of a plane. This chapter's demos are 2D, so we treat bending only, and leave twist to the next chapter, where each segment carries its own orientation.

Buckling

Stand the rod upright, clamp its foot, and press down on its top. A perfectly straight rod under a perfectly centred load just shortens a tiny amount, as $EA$ says. But above a certain load the straight shape stops being stable: any tiny sideways disturbance grows, and the rod bows out. That load is the critical load, and Euler found it in 1744.

Take the simplest case first: a rod of length $L$ on hinges at both ends (free to rotate, not to move sideways), squeezed by a force $P$. If the rod is bent out by $y(s)$, the load acts with lever arm $y$, and the moment it causes must be carried by bending: $EI\,y'' = -P\,y$. That is the equation of a harmonic oscillator in $s$ instead of time, with solutions $y = \sin(s\sqrt{P/EI})$. To fit between two hinges the sine must reach zero again at $s = L$, which needs $L\sqrt{P/EI} = \pi$. Below that load no bent shape is in equilibrium, and the rod stays straight; at that load a bent shape is possible:

$$P_{\text{cr}} = \frac{\pi^2 EI}{(KL)^2}.$$

The effective length factor $K$ accounts for the supports, by asking what fraction of the rod looks like one half-wave of the sine:

Our steel rod, which takes 3.9 MN to stretch by its own length in theory, buckles under about 1.5 kg on its top. In compression, slender rods fail by bending, not by squashing. For the demo's rod (clamped foot, free top), $P_{\text{cr}} = \pi^2 \times 1.25 / 2^2 = 3.08$ N.

Real rods are never perfectly straight, and the demo adds a sideways force of 2 mN at the tip as an imperfection. Below the critical load, the sideways deflection that this causes is amplified by roughly

$$\frac{\delta}{\delta_0} \approx \frac{1}{1 - P/P_{\text{cr}}},$$

where $\delta_0$ is the deflection with no axial load (here $0.002/(3 \times 1.25) = 0.53$ mm). At 90 % of the critical load the rod deflects ten times as far; at the critical load the amplification diverges, and the rod responds more and more slowly as you approach it. Above $P_{\text{cr}}$ the column does not collapse: it settles into a bent equilibrium called the elastica, which Euler also solved, with elliptic integrals. At $P = 3.5$ N, which is $1.135\,P_{\text{cr}}$, the elastica's tip sits 569 mm to the side; the demo's 10-segment rod settles at 574 mm, within 1 %.

Gravity can buckle a column too. A vertical rod clamped at its foot buckles under its own weight once it is taller than about $1.99\,\ell_g$ (Greenhill, 1881), with the same elastogravity length as before. For our steel rod that is 3.2 m. This is why the elastogravity length is the number to check for anything tall and thin, from grass stems to antennas.

Twist-bend instability

Twist can buckle a rod as well. Hold a straight rod or cable by both ends and twist it: at first it twists in place, but beyond a critical twist the straight shape becomes unstable and the rod jumps sideways into a helix. Pull the ends together a little and the helix collapses into a loop, a small tight coil that sticks out of the line. Anyone who has coiled a garden hose or an extension cable the wrong way has met it: the loops that form when you uncoil are stored twist turning into bend. Sailors and cable engineers call this hockling.

The classical results are Greenhill's, for a twisted straight shaft (on hinges, with no axial load, it buckles at a torque $T = 2\pi EI/L$), and Michell's 1889 analysis of a twisted closed ring, which stays flat only while its twist is below a threshold set by the ratio $EI/GJ$. Both say the same thing: twist and bend are coupled, because a twisted rod can lower its energy by converting twist into writhe, the coiling of the centreline in space. You cannot see this in a 2D simulation or in a rod model without a twist degree of freedom, which is a good reason for the next chapter.

Discretising bending

Now let's build a rod that bends. Start from the chain of the earlier chapters: particles $x_0, \dots, x_N$, each consecutive pair joined by a distance constraint of rest length $l = L/N$. Given that $EA$ is enormous, we make the distance constraints rigid ($\alpha = 0$).

Bending lives at the particles between two segments. At particle $i$, the segments $e_1 = x_i - x_{i-1}$ and $e_2 = x_{i+1} - x_i$ meet with a turning angle $\theta_i$: the signed angle from $e_1$ to $e_2$, positive when the rod turns anticlockwise. A straight rod has $\theta_i = 0$. We make that a constraint on the triple of particles:

$$C_i = \theta_i - \bar\theta_i, \qquad \theta_i = \operatorname{atan2}(e_1 \times e_2,\; e_1 \cdot e_2),$$

where $\bar\theta_i$ is the rest angle (zero for a straight rod) and $e_1 \times e_2 = e_{1x}e_{2y} - e_{1y}e_{2x}$ is the 2D cross product. Using atan2 of the cross and dot products gives the angle in $(-\pi, \pi]$ directly, with no wrapping problems at small angles.

For XPBD we need the gradient. The angle of a vector $e$ is $\operatorname{atan2}(e_y, e_x)$, and its derivative with respect to the vector is $e^\perp / |e|^2$, with $e^\perp = (-e_y, e_x)$, the vector turned a quarter turn anticlockwise. Since $\theta_i$ is the angle of $e_2$ minus the angle of $e_1$:

$$\nabla_{x_{i-1}} C = \frac{e_1^\perp}{|e_1|^2}, \qquad \nabla_{x_{i+1}} C = \frac{e_2^\perp}{|e_2|^2}, \qquad \nabla_{x_i} C = -\nabla_{x_{i-1}} C - \nabla_{x_{i+1}} C.$$

The three gradients sum to zero, so the constraint never pushes the rod as a whole: it only bends it. Each gradient has size $1/l$ for a straight rod, which makes sense: moving an end by $\Delta$ sideways turns its segment by $\Delta/l$. The XPBD update is the one from chapter 8:

$$\Delta\lambda = \frac{-C - \tilde\alpha\,\lambda}{\sum_j w_j |\nabla_{x_j} C|^2 + \tilde\alpha}, \qquad \Delta x_j = w_j\, \nabla_{x_j} C\,\Delta\lambda, \qquad \tilde\alpha = \frac{\alpha}{h^2}.$$

The joint's compliance

What should $\alpha$ be? Compare energies. The continuous rod stores bending energy

$$E_{\text{bend}} = \int_0^L \tfrac12 EI\,\kappa^2\, ds.$$

Joint $i$ represents a piece of rod of length $l$ (half of each neighbouring segment) over which the tangent turns by $\theta_i$, so its average curvature is $\kappa \approx \theta_i/l$ and its energy is $\tfrac12 EI (\theta_i / l)^2 \, l = \tfrac12 (EI/l)\,\theta_i^2$. An XPBD constraint with compliance $\alpha$ stores $\tfrac12 C^2 / \alpha$. Matching the two:

$$\alpha_i = \frac{l}{EI}.$$

Read it as a rotational spring: a joint of stiffness $EI/l$ N·m per radian. Finer segments make stiffer joints, because each joint stands for a shorter piece of rod and must turn less for the same curvature. The units check: $C$ is an angle (dimensionless), so $\alpha$ is in 1/(N·m), and $l/EI$ is m / (N·m2).

function solveBend(k, h) {
  const a = k - 1, b = k, c = k + 1;
  const e1x = x[b] - x[a], e1y = y[b] - y[a];
  const e2x = x[c] - x[b], e2y = y[c] - y[b];
  const C = Math.atan2(e1x * e2y - e1y * e2x, e1x * e2x + e1y * e2y);   // turning angle
  const l1 = e1x * e1x + e1y * e1y, l2 = e2x * e2x + e2y * e2y;
  const gax = -e1y / l1, gay = e1x / l1;        // perp(e1) / |e1|^2
  const gcx = -e2y / l2, gcy = e2x / l2;        // perp(e2) / |e2|^2
  const gbx = -gax - gcx, gby = -gay - gcy;
  // ... then the usual XPBD update with alphaTilde = alphaJoint[k] / h^2
}

Here is how well it works, with the series rule of the next section at the clamp. With $N$ equal segments and masses lumped at the particles, the small-deflection tip of the discrete rod can be worked out exactly. It is $P L^3/(3EI)\,(1 + 1/(2N^2))$ under a tip load and $wL^4/(8EI)\,(1 + 1/N^2)$ under self-weight: the error falls as $1/N^2$, and ten segments are already within 1 %. The discrete column buckles at 3.078 N with ten segments, 0.2 % below Euler's 3.084 N.

Clamps and stiffness changes: the series rule

Two places in a rod break the "each joint stands for length $l$" picture: its ends and any point where the stiffness changes.

Consider a joint between a segment of stiffness $EI_a$ and one of stiffness $EI_b$. It stands for half of each segment. The two halves bend in series: the same moment passes through both, and their rotations add. For springs in series, compliances add, so

$$\alpha_i = \frac{l_a/2}{EI_a} + \frac{l_b/2}{EI_b}.$$

This is the series rule. For a uniform rod it gives back $l/EI$. A rigid part contributes zero, which handles the clamp: in the demo, the wall holds a fixed ghost particle behind the root, so the root joint sits between a rigid segment and a rod segment and gets $\alpha = (l/2)/EI$, half the interior compliance. The free tip has no joint at all. That half-length root joint is exactly what produces the $1 + 1/(2N^2)$ convergence above; give the root a full $l/EI$ instead and the rod is too soft by half a segment's worth of flexibility at the place where the moment is largest.

What goes wrong with a more obvious choice? A natural first guess is to give the joint the average stiffness of its two neighbours, $\alpha = l / \big(\tfrac12 (EI_a + EI_b)\big)$. That averages stiffnesses, which is right for springs in parallel, but these are in series. The guess is wrong in the direction that matters: it is dominated by the stiffer neighbour, so a stiff part makes the joint next to it too stiff. Next to a rigid clamp it gives $\alpha = 0$, a rigid joint, and the whole first half-segment of flexibility disappears.

The error does not go away with resolution as fast as you would like. One joint's mistake is half a segment's worth of compliance, so it shrinks only as $1/N$, not $1/N^2$. With the demo's stiff base (four times stiffer for the first half of the rod) and ten segments, the average-stiffness rule makes the tip deflection 15 % too small; with thirty segments it is still 5 % off. The series rule gets 0.9 % and 0.1 %. In a rod with many stiffness changes (a fishing rod with ferrules, a cable with rigid connectors, a robot arm with stiff links between flexible sections) the errors pile up at every joint.

The same rule covers non-uniform segment lengths, which you will want when you refine a rod near where it bends most: each joint gets half of each of its neighbouring segments' $l/EI$.

Why stiff rods need small steps

Run the demo with 20 segments and set the substeps to 10. The rod sags nearly four times too far, and it is calm about it: no jitter, no explosion, just a rod that is wrong. With 30 substeps it is still 17 % too soft. What happened?

Each joint is a rotational spring of stiffness $EI/l$ acting on particles of mass $\mu l$ ($\mu$ the mass per length) through lever arms of length $l$. Its natural frequency is roughly

$$\omega_{\text{joint}} = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_j w_j |\nabla C|^2}{\alpha}} = \sqrt{\frac{6\,EI}{\mu\, l^4}},$$

which grows as $1/l^2$, that is as $N^2$. Ten segments of the demo's rod give 866 rad/s; twenty give 3,464 rad/s. When the substep $h$ is large compared with $1/\omega_{\text{joint}}$, $\tilde\alpha$ is tiny compared with $\sum w|\nabla C|^2$, and each joint behaves almost like a hard constraint. A Gauss-Seidel sweep of hard constraints does not reach the true equilibrium in a few iterations: information travels about one joint per sweep, while gravity is pulling the whole rod every substep. The sweep settles into a compromise that is too soft. Only when $h\,\omega_{\text{joint}}$ is small does each joint's spring force dominate its own update and the answer converge to the right one.

The demo's auto setting keeps $h\,\omega_{\text{joint}} \le 0.5$ for the stiffest joint, which for the root joint of a ten-segment rod means 41 substeps per frame, and 164 for twenty segments. The cost grows as $N^3$. That is affordable for tens of segments and painful for hundreds. It is the reason serious rod simulators use the direct solvers from chapter 10: a rod is a chain, so its system is banded and can be solved exactly in time proportional to $N$. The next chapter cites a solver that does exactly that for rods with orientations.

A dynamic check. The first bending mode of a cantilever has angular frequency $\omega_1 = 3.516\sqrt{EI/(\mu L^4)}$, which for the demo rod is 12.4 rad/s, a period of 0.505 s. Start the demo with self-weight on and count: the simulated rod (20 segments, with the demo's light damping) swings with a period of 0.507 s.

Solution

Compare with solution.js. The two functions are:

function jointCompliance(k) {
  const EIa = segmentEI(k - 1), EIb = segmentEI(k);
  if (rule.value === 'naive (average EI)') return l / (0.5 * (EIa + EIb));
  return (0.5 * l) / EIa + (0.5 * l) / EIb;
}

function solveBend(k, h) {
  const a = k - 1, b = k, c = k + 1;
  const e1x = x[b] - x[a], e1y = y[b] - y[a];
  const e2x = x[c] - x[b], e2y = y[c] - y[b];
  const C = Math.atan2(e1x * e2y - e1y * e2x, e1x * e2x + e1y * e2y);
  const l1 = e1x * e1x + e1y * e1y, l2 = e2x * e2x + e2y * e2y;
  const gax = -e1y / l1, gay = e1x / l1;
  const gcx = -e2y / l2, gcy = e2x / l2;
  const gbx = -gax - gcx, gby = -gay - gcy;
  const alphaTilde = alphaJoint[k] / (h * h);
  const denom = w[a] * (gax * gax + gay * gay) + w[b] * (gbx * gbx + gby * gby) +
                w[c] * (gcx * gcx + gcy * gcy) + alphaTilde;
  if (denom === 0) return;
  const dlambda = (-C - alphaTilde * lambdaBend[k]) / denom;
  lambdaBend[k] += dlambda;
  x[a] += w[a] * gax * dlambda; y[a] += w[a] * gay * dlambda;
  x[b] += w[b] * gbx * dlambda; y[b] += w[b] * gby * dlambda;
  x[c] += w[c] * gcx * dlambda; y[c] += w[c] * gcy * dlambda;
}

Measure it with the series rule and with the naive average-EI rule, for 10 and 20 segments. Then predict, joint by joint, how much compliance the naive rule loses at the clamp and at the change of stiffness, and turn that into a predicted error in millimetres.

Hint

The exact value is $0.3 \times (0.875/15 + 0.125/3.75) = 27.5$ mm. The series rule gives 27.7 mm at ten segments and 27.5 mm at twenty; the naive rule gives 23.4 mm and 25.4 mm. For ten segments ($l = 0.1$ m) the naive rule sets the clamp joint to zero instead of $0.05/5 = 0.01$ (N·m)-1, costing $P \cdot L^2 \cdot 0.01 = 3.0$ mm, and the joint at the change to $0.1/3.125 = 0.032$ instead of $0.01 + 0.04 = 0.05$, costing $P\,(L - 0.5)^2 \times 0.018 = 1.35$ mm. Together that is 4.35 mm.

Summary

  • A rod resists bending with moment $M = EI\kappa$. $E$ is the material; $I$ is the shape: $\pi d^4/64$ for a solid round rod, $\pi(D^4 - d^4)/64$ for a tube. Diameter enters to the fourth power.
  • A cantilever's tip deflects by $PL^3/(3EI)$ under a tip load and $wL^4/(8EI)$ under its own weight. The elastogravity length $(EI/\rho A g)^{1/3}$ tells you whether a rod in gravity behaves like a stick or a rope.
  • Twisting stiffness $GJ$ is comparable with $EI$ for round rods; axial stiffness $EA$ is hundreds of thousands of times larger in practice, so we treat rods as inextensible.
  • A column buckles at $\pi^2 EI/(KL)^2$, far below the load that would crush it. Twist can buckle a rod too, into helices and loops.
  • A particle rod bends through one turning-angle constraint per joint, with compliance $l/EI$. At ends and changes of stiffness use the series rule, $\sum (l_i/2)/EI_i$ over the joint's two half-segments, with rigid parts contributing zero.
  • Bending joints get stiffer as $N^2$; an iterative solver needs substeps that resolve them, or it returns a rod that is quietly too soft.
