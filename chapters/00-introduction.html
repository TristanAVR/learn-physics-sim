Introduction

What you will build, how the site works, and how to get the most out of it.

Physics simulation looks like magic from the outside. A rope swings, cloth drapes over a chair, a snowball bursts into powder, water sloshes around a glass. Under the hood, every one of these is the same small loop run many times a second: look at the current state of the world, work out the forces or constraints acting on it, and move everything a tiny step forward in time. The craft is in the details of that step, and those details are what this site teaches.

We will start with the smallest possible simulation, one particle falling under gravity, and add one idea per chapter until you can write the methods used for snow, sand and fluid in modern film and games. You will not just read about them. Each chapter ends with you implementing the idea yourself.

What you will build

The chapters fall into eight parts.

  • Foundations. Particles, forces and Newton's laws; the integrator, the few lines of code that decide whether your simulation is stable or explodes; springs, damping and energy.
  • Constraints. Instead of springs that try to keep a rope's length, constraints that simply enforce it: Position-Based Dynamics (PBD), its modern form XPBD that gets stiffness right, substepping, and what a linear solver actually does when it "solves" a system.
  • Rods. Rotations and rigid bodies, the beam theory every engineer learns, Cosserat rods that bend and twist, and cable-driven tentacles that curl when you pull a wire.
  • Contact. Finding collisions quickly and without missing fast objects, then resolving them with friction, including the surprising rope-around-a-post effect that lets a sailor hold a ship with one hand.
  • Soft bodies. Cloth and thin shells, inflated bodies under pressure, coupling a stiff object to a soft one when each is solved differently, and materials that move on their own, like muscles.
  • Continuum mechanics and MPM. The deformation gradient, stress, and hyperelastic materials; the particle-grid methods PIC, FLIP and APIC; the Material Point Method for jelly, snow and sand; implicit MPM; and Position-Based MPM.
  • Reading the papers. Guided walks through three papers, section by section, with every result derived.
  • Making it real. How to hunt down explosions systematically, and how to verify that your simulator computes what you think it does.

The papers

One goal shapes the whole site: by the end you should be able to sit down with three important papers and read them without getting lost.

  1. The Affine Particle-In-Cell Method by Jiang, Schroeder, Selle, Teran and Stomakhin (SIGGRAPH 2015). It shows how to move velocity between particles and a grid without the smearing of PIC or the noise of FLIP, by giving each particle a little affine velocity field.
  2. The Material Point Method for Simulating Continuum Materials by Jiang, Schroeder, Teran, Stomakhin and Selle (SIGGRAPH 2016 course notes). A 52-page tutorial covering continuum mechanics, hyperelasticity, and the explicit and implicit MPM algorithms.
  3. A Position Based Material Point Method by Chris Lewin (SIGGRAPH 2024 talk). It combines MPM with the position-based solvers used in games, so that MPM stays stable at any time step.

Papers are dense because their readers are experts: they skip steps that are "standard", use notation without introducing it, and leave derivations to supplements. The chapters before the reading guides build every one of those skipped steps. Along the way, boxes like this one point out where a paper says the same thing in its own words:

The MPM course notes call the green integrator from the Time Integration chapter "symplectic Euler". When the course writes $\mathbf{v}^{n+1}_i$, it means the velocity of grid node $i$ after the step; on this site that would be $v_i$ at step $n+1$.

How each chapter works

The chapters follow the pattern that LearnOpenGL made popular for graphics: introduce an idea properly, show it working, and then let you build it.

  1. The problem. Each chapter opens with something that does not work yet, or a question we cannot answer yet.
  2. The idea. The physics and the maths, built up step by step, with small code snippets. Derivations are included where they help you understand; the rest is stated with a pointer.
  3. A running demo. Every technique runs right in the page. Play with its sliders before reading the maths: having a feel for what happens makes the equations easier.
  4. Your turn. Each chapter has a starter file that runs but is missing the interesting part, marked with TODO comments, and exercises that ask you to fill it in, predict a number, extend it, or break it. Hints are folded away; a reference solution is linked at the end.
  5. Questions to come back to. A few short questions to answer from memory a few days later.

Predict before you run. Many exercises ask you to guess the result before you measure it. It feels slower, but it is the single most effective habit in this site. A wrong prediction tells you exactly which part of your mental model to fix; a right one tells you that you understand something, not just that the code works.

What you need

A browser and a text editor. All the code is plain JavaScript that runs in the browser, with no install and no build step. Download or clone the site, then open a starter through the runner page:

code/run.html?src=04-time-integration/starter.js

Edit code/04-time-integration/starter.js, save, and refresh the page. Some browsers do not allow pages opened straight from disk to load other scripts; if the runner shows a blank page, serve the folder locally instead, for example with python -m http.server in the site folder, and open http://localhost:8000.

Some programming. If you can write loops, functions and arrays in any C-like language, you can follow the code. It is deliberately plain: no frameworks, no clever idioms, so that it ports easily to C#, C++, Rust or Python. If you prefer to work in another language or engine, do: the starters are a reference for what to build, not a cage.

Maths, introduced as needed. Vectors and derivatives are enough to begin. Matrices, eigenvalues, partial derivatives and a little linear algebra appear gradually, each explained the first time it is needed. The continuum chapters are the most mathematical; they build up slowly and use the earlier parts as stepping stones.

Conventions

  • Units are SI throughout: metres, kilograms, seconds, newtons. A simulation that mixes units is a simulation with bugs.
  • The world is 2D with y pointing up, and gravity is $9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$ downward. Nearly every idea on this site carries over to 3D unchanged; where it does not (rotations, twisting rods, shells), the text explains the 3D version.
  • Notation is kept consistent across chapters and matched to the papers where possible: $h$ is the time step, $m$ a mass, $w = 1/m$ an inverse mass, $C$ a constraint, $\lambda$ a Lagrange multiplier, $\alpha$ a compliance, $\mathbf{F}$ the deformation gradient. Each symbol is introduced where it first appears.

A word on accuracy

Every demo on this site states what it should do and, where possible, checks itself against an exact answer: a pendulum's period, a beam's deflection, a rope's holding ratio. When a method is an approximation, the chapter says how good the approximation is and when it breaks. Simulation is full of methods that look right and are not; learning to tell the difference is half of the skill.
