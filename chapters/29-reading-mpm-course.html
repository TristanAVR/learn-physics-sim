Reading: The MPM Course (2016)

Fifty-two pages that take you from a flow map to a Newton solver, read with a pencil.

The SIGGRAPH 2016 course notes on the Material Point Method are the closest thing the field has to a textbook chapter. They start from the definition of motion, build continuum mechanics, discretise it on a grid with particles, and end with explicit and implicit time stepping. They are the standard reference for graphics MPM, and the PB-MPM paper sends its readers there for "standard MPM techniques".

They are also uneven. Some results are derived in loving detail; others appear in one line; a few sections are written for people who already know finite elements. This chapter walks through the notes in their own order. For each section you get what it says, what to read slowly and what to skim, the key equations restated in this site's notation, and full derivations where the notes are brief. Questions with hidden answers follow each major part.

By the end you should be able to derive the MPM grid force from an energy, explain why the stress in the force formula is $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{F}^T$ and not $\mathbf{P}$, write the explicit algorithm from memory, and set up the Newton system for implicit MPM.

the notes

Chenfanfu Jiang, Craig Schroeder, Joseph Teran, Alexey Stomakhin, Andrew Selle. The Material Point Method for Simulating Continuum Materials. SIGGRAPH 2016 Courses, Article 24, 52 pages. DOI 10.1145/2897826.2927348. Version 1, May 2016. The authors were at the University of California, Los Angeles, the University of California, Riverside, and Walt Disney Animation Studios.

What the notes are, and where they sit

MPM began in computational mechanics: Sulsky, Chen and Schreyer (1994) and Sulsky, Zhou and Schreyer (1995) turned the 1960s Particle-In-Cell method into a method for solids, by letting each particle carry its own deformation history. Graphics met hybrid particle-grid methods through FLIP liquids (Zhu and Bridson, 2005), and MPM itself through Stomakhin et al.'s snow paper (2013), developed for feature-film production. A burst of follow-ups came from the same group: phase change (2014), APIC transfers (2015, chapter 28), an optimisation-based implicit integrator (Gast et al., 2015), sand (Klár et al., 2016). The course gathers that work into one tutorial. Later developments the notes predate include MLS-MPM (2018) and PB-MPM (2024, chapter 30).

The problem the notes solve is a teaching problem. MPM papers each assume the previous ones. The course lays out the whole chain in one place, from "what is a deformation" to "here is the linear system you solve".

Section 3: Introduction

Two pages; read them. The key paragraphs: MPM is the generalisation of PIC and FLIP to solids; its strengths are automatic self-collision, automatic fracture and merging, easy multi-material coupling; and, the paragraph to remember, MPM is really a Lagrangian method that uses an Eulerian grid to compute derivatives. Particles are not little balls; each one stands for a chunk of continuous material, and acts as a quadrature point for integrals over the body. The notes also admit the price: accuracy for elastic solids is lower than with a good mesh.

Section 5: Kinematics theory

Section 5 is chapter 20 in the notes' words. Read it carefully on the first pass; the notation built here is used to the end.

5.1 Continuum motion, equations (1) to (5)

Motion is a map from rest positions to current positions, $\mathbf{x} = \phi(\mathbf{X}, t)$ (1). Two examples: translation $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{X} + tv\mathbf{n}$ (2), and rigid motion $\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{X} + \mathbf{b}$ (3). Velocity and acceleration of a fixed piece of material are time derivatives at fixed $\mathbf{X}$: $\mathbf{V} = \partial\phi/\partial t$ (4), $\mathbf{A} = \partial^2\phi/\partial t^2$ (5). The paragraph after (5) introduces the Lagrangian/Eulerian distinction: measuring on a moving piece of material, or sitting at a fixed place and measuring whatever passes.

5.2 Deformation, equations (6) and (7)

The deformation gradient is the Jacobian of the map, $\mathbf{F} = \partial\phi/\partial\mathbf{X}$ (6), in components $F_{ij} = \partial x_i/\partial X_j$ (7). It maps a tiny rest vector to its deformed version, $d\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{F}\,d\mathbf{X}$ (Figure 1), and $J = \det\mathbf{F}$ is the local volume ratio. For (2) $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{I}$; for (3) $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}$: rigid motions do not deform, and should cause no internal force. Two more worked examples to add to the notes' two:

  • Simple shear, $x = X + \gamma Y$, $y = Y$: $\mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix}1&\gamma\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, $J = 1$. Shape changes, area does not.
  • Squash, $x = 2X$, $y = Y/2$: $\mathbf{F} = \operatorname{diag}(2, \tfrac12)$, $J = 1$. Again area-preserving; a liquid would not resist this, an elastic solid would.

The notes' remarks on $J \le 0$ (zero volume, inversion) are worth reading: numerically it happens, and the fixed corotated model of section 6.3 is chosen partly because it behaves sensibly there.

5.3 Push forward and pull back, equations (8) to (18)

Any function of $\mathbf{X}$ can be turned into a function of $\mathbf{x}$ by composing with the inverse map, and back. That is all "push forward" and "pull back" mean. The section then derives something important. Differentiate the Lagrangian velocity $\mathbf{V}(\mathbf{X}, t) = \mathbf{v}(\phi(\mathbf{X}, t), t)$ in time, holding $\mathbf{X}$ fixed, with the chain rule (14), (15):

$$A_i = \frac{\partial v_i}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j}\frac{\partial\phi_j}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial v_i}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_j}v_j.$$

So the Eulerian acceleration (17) is not $\partial\mathbf{v}/\partial t$; the second term accounts for the material moving to a place where the velocity is different. Equation (18) in the notes makes exactly that point, $\mathbf{a} \ne \partial\mathbf{v}/\partial t$; if a copy you are reading seems to say they are equal, a symbol was lost in text extraction.

5.4 Material derivative, equations (19) to (24)

The combination above gets a name, the material derivative $\frac{D}{Dt} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \mathbf{v}\cdot\nabla$ (19) to (21): the rate of change seen by a particle riding with the flow. Then the most important result of section 5 for MPM, the evolution of $\mathbf{F}$, equations (22) to (24). The derivation (23), slowly:

$$\frac{\partial F_{ij}}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial\phi_i}{\partial X_j} = \frac{\partial V_i}{\partial X_j} = \frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_k}\frac{\partial\phi_k}{\partial X_j} = \frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_k}F_{kj}.$$

Swap the order of the two derivatives, write the Lagrangian velocity as the Eulerian one evaluated on the moving point, and apply the chain rule. In matrix form,

$$\dot{\mathbf{F}} = (\nabla\mathbf{v})\,\mathbf{F}. \tag{24}$$

The velocity gradient multiplies from the left: it is a world-space quantity acting on the already-deformed material. Check it on simple shear flow, $\mathbf{v} = (\dot\gamma y, 0)$: $\nabla\mathbf{v} = \begin{pmatrix}0&\dot\gamma\\0&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{F}(t) = \begin{pmatrix}1&\dot\gamma t\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$, and indeed $(\nabla\mathbf{v})\mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix}0&\dot\gamma\\0&0\end{pmatrix} = \dot{\mathbf{F}}$. Section 9.4 discretises exactly this equation.

5.5 Volume and area change, equations (25) to (35)

Volumes scale by $J$: $dv = J\,dV$ (25) to (27). Areas are subtler, and the result, Nanson's formula, is what later converts between the two stress measures. The notes' argument, expanded: take a tiny rest area element with normal $\mathbf{N}$, $d\mathbf{S} = dS\,\mathbf{N}$ (28), and any tiny vector $d\mathbf{L}$; they span a prism of volume $dV = d\mathbf{S}\cdot d\mathbf{L}$ (30). After deformation, $dv = d\mathbf{s}\cdot d\mathbf{l} = d\mathbf{s}\cdot\mathbf{F}\,d\mathbf{L}$ (31). Since $dv = J\,dV$,

$$J\,d\mathbf{S}\cdot d\mathbf{L} = d\mathbf{s}\cdot\mathbf{F}\,d\mathbf{L} = (\mathbf{F}^Td\mathbf{s})\cdot d\mathbf{L}\quad\text{for every } d\mathbf{L},$$

so $\mathbf{F}^Td\mathbf{s} = J\,d\mathbf{S}$, that is

$$\mathbf{n}\,ds = J\,\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{N}\,dS. \tag{34}$$

Equation (35) is the same statement under an integral sign: a flux through a deformed surface equals a flux through the rest surface with the integrand multiplied by $J\mathbf{F}^{-T}$.

Check your understanding

1. In a uniform stretch $\mathbf{F} = \operatorname{diag}(1.1, 1)$, how do lengths, areas and the area of a face with normal $y$ change?

Lengths along $x$ grow by 10 %, along $y$ not at all; volume (area in 2D) grows by $J = 1.1$. A face with normal $\mathbf{N} = (0, 1)$ is a segment along $x$, so its length grows by 1.1. Nanson agrees: $J\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{N} = 1.1\cdot(0, 1) $, so $ds = 1.1\,dS$ with unchanged normal.

2. Why does $\dot{\mathbf{F}} = \mathbf{F}\,\nabla\mathbf{v}$ (right multiplication) give wrong answers?

$\nabla\mathbf{v}$ is a derivative with respect to world positions, so it acts on world-space vectors, which is what $\mathbf{F}$ produces. $\mathbf{F}\nabla\mathbf{v}$ would apply the world-space velocity gradient to rest-space vectors. For a rotated body the two differ, and the right-multiplied version spins the material incorrectly. The same mistake appears as a typo in the PB-MPM paper (chapter 30).

Section 6: Hyperelasticity

Section 6 is chapter 21 plus the start of 25. Read 6.1 to 6.3 carefully, skim 6.4, read 6.5 when you reach plasticity.

6.1 First Piola-Kirchhoff stress, equations (36) to (45)

A hyperelastic material is defined by an energy density $\Psi(\mathbf{F})$, energy per unit rest volume. Its stress is the derivative, $\mathbf{P} = \partial\Psi/\partial\mathbf{F}$ (36), (37). Equation (38) relates it to the Cauchy stress:

$$\boldsymbol\sigma = \frac1J\mathbf{P}\mathbf{F}^T = \frac{1}{\det\mathbf{F}}\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial\mathbf{F}}\mathbf{F}^T. \tag{38}$$

The notes state (38) here and justify it in section 7.2; we derive it there too. Then the requirement that rigid motion causes no stress leads to $\Psi(\mathbf{F}) = \tilde\Psi(\mathbf{F}^T\mathbf{F})$ (40), isotropy to a function of invariants (41) to (44), and finally to a function of singular values, $\Psi(\mathbf{F}) = \hat\Psi(\boldsymbol\Sigma)$ with the "polar SVD" $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^T$, where $\mathbf{U}, \mathbf{V}$ are rotations (45). The polar decomposition is $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}$ with $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^T$, the rotation closest to $\mathbf{F}$, and $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^T$ symmetric.

In 2D you do not need an SVD for $\mathbf{R}$. For $\mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}$ with $\det\mathbf{F} > 0$, the polar rotation is the rotation by $\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(c - b,\ a + d)$. The checks at the end of this chapter use exactly this.

6.2 Neo-Hookean, equations (46) to (48)

$$\Psi(\mathbf{F}) = \frac\mu2\big(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{F}^T\mathbf{F}) - d\big) - \mu\log J + \frac\lambda2\log^2J, \tag{46}$$ $$\mu = \frac{E}{2(1+\nu)}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{E\nu}{(1+\nu)(1-2\nu)}, \tag{47}$$ $$\mathbf{P} = \mu(\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{F}^{-T}) + \lambda\log J\,\mathbf{F}^{-T}. \tag{48}$$

The notes give (48) without derivation. Term by term: $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{F}^T\mathbf{F}) = \sum F_{ij}^2$, whose derivative is $2\mathbf{F}$, so the first term gives $\mu\mathbf{F}$. For the logs you need the derivative of the determinant, $\partial J/\partial\mathbf{F} = J\mathbf{F}^{-T}$ (it appears as equation (100); proof in section 7.1 below), so $\partial\log J/\partial\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}^{-T}$, giving $-\mu\mathbf{F}^{-T}$ and $\lambda\log J\,\mathbf{F}^{-T}$. A number to anchor it: $E = 10^4$ Pa (a soft jelly) and $\nu = 0.3$ give $\mu = 3846$ Pa and Lamé $\lambda = 5769$ Pa.

6.3 Fixed corotated, equations (49) to (59)

$$\Psi = \mu\sum_i(\sigma_i - 1)^2 + \frac\lambda2(J - 1)^2, \qquad \mathbf{P} = 2\mu(\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{R}) + \lambda(J - 1)J\mathbf{F}^{-T}. \tag{49, 52}$$

The step (51) the notes call "can be shown": $\sum_i\sigma_i^2 = \|\mathbf{F}\|_F^2$, whose derivative is $2\mathbf{F}$; and each singular value has derivative $\partial\sigma_i/\partial\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{u}_i\mathbf{v}_i^T$ (columns of $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{V}$), so $\partial\sum\sigma_i/\partial\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^T = \mathbf{R}$. Then $\mu\sum(\sigma_i^2 - 2\sigma_i + 1)$ gives $2\mu\mathbf{F} - 2\mu\mathbf{R}$, and $\tfrac\lambda2(J-1)^2$ gives $\lambda(J-1)\,J\mathbf{F}^{-T}$. Notice $\sum(\sigma_i - 1)^2 = \|\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{R}\|_F^2$, the squared distance from $\mathbf{F}$ to the nearest rotation. Keep that in mind for PB-MPM, whose elastic constraint is exactly "move $\mathbf{F}$ to $\mathbf{R}$".

Worked example: stretch 10 % along $x$, $\mathbf{F} = \operatorname{diag}(1.1, 1)$, with the jelly above. Then $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$, $J = 1.1$, and

$$\mathbf{P} = 2\mu\operatorname{diag}(0.1, 0) + \lambda(0.1)(1.1)\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac1{1.1}, 1) = \operatorname{diag}(1346,\ 635)\ \text{Pa}.$$

The $y$ entry is nonzero although nothing was stretched in $y$: the material wants its area back. Hold onto these numbers for section 7.2.

Equations (53) to (59) compute the differential $\delta\mathbf{P}$ for implicit solvers. The only hard part is $\delta\mathbf{R}$. The notes' trick (57) to (59): differentiate $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}$, multiply by $\mathbf{R}^T$, and subtract the transpose so that the unknown symmetric $\delta\mathbf{S}$ cancels:

$$\mathbf{R}^T\delta\mathbf{F} - \delta\mathbf{F}^T\mathbf{R} = (\mathbf{R}^T\delta\mathbf{R})\mathbf{S} + \mathbf{S}(\mathbf{R}^T\delta\mathbf{R}),$$

using that $\mathbf{R}^T\delta\mathbf{R}$ is antisymmetric. In 3D it is a $3\times3$ linear system for the three entries of that antisymmetric matrix. In 2D it has a closed form worth having: with $\mathbf{R}^T\delta\mathbf{R} = \delta\theta\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, the right-hand side is $\delta\theta\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{S})$ times the same matrix, so

$$\delta\theta = \frac{(\mathbf{R}^T\delta\mathbf{F})_{21} - (\mathbf{R}^T\delta\mathbf{F})_{12}}{\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{S}}.$$

Also in 2D, $J\mathbf{F}^{-T}$ is the cofactor matrix $\begin{pmatrix}d&-c\\-b&a\end{pmatrix}$, linear in the entries of $\mathbf{F}$, so its differential is trivial.

6.4 A practical differentiation strategy, equations (60) to (77)

Skim on a first pass. The useful results: for any isotropic model, $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{U}\hat{\mathbf{P}}\mathbf{V}^T$ with $\hat{\mathbf{P}} = \operatorname{diag}(\partial\hat\Psi/\partial\sigma_i)$ (63). The proof is short and nice: rotating after deforming does not change energy, which gives $\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}) = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{F})$; isotropy gives $\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{R}) = \mathbf{P}(\mathbf{F})\mathbf{R}$; apply both to $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^T$. The same rotation argument gives $\partial\mathbf{P}/\partial\mathbf{F}$ in terms of its value at a diagonal $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ (65) to (72), which can be computed with symbolic software (the Mathematica listings; skip them). The result (75) to (77) is block diagonal, with $2\times2$ blocks

$$\mathbf{B}_{ij} = \frac{1}{\sigma_i^2 - \sigma_j^2}\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_i\hat\Psi_{,i} - \sigma_j\hat\Psi_{,j} & \sigma_j\hat\Psi_{,i} - \sigma_i\hat\Psi_{,j}\\ \sigma_j\hat\Psi_{,i} - \sigma_i\hat\Psi_{,j} & \sigma_i\hat\Psi_{,i} - \sigma_j\hat\Psi_{,j}\end{pmatrix} = \frac{\hat\Psi_{,i} - \hat\Psi_{,j}}{2(\sigma_i - \sigma_j)}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&1\end{pmatrix} + \frac{\hat\Psi_{,i} + \hat\Psi_{,j}}{2(\sigma_i + \sigma_j)}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix}.$$

The first form divides by zero when two singular values are equal; the second (77) shows that the first fraction has a finite limit for a symmetric $\hat\Psi$ (a difference quotient that tends to a combination of second derivatives) and only the second fraction can genuinely blow up, when $\sigma_i + \sigma_j \to 0$ in inverted states. The notes clamp that denominator at $10^{-6}$. You can check the equality of the two forms by putting them over a common denominator.

6.5 Snow plasticity, equations (78) to (87)

Read with chapter 25. $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}_E\mathbf{F}_P$ (78): the plastic part is deformation the material has forgotten, only $\mathbf{F}_E$ makes stress. Each step: assume the new deformation is all elastic, $\tilde{\mathbf{F}}_E = \mathbf{F}^{n+1}(\mathbf{F}_P^n)^{-1}$ (81); clamp its singular values to $[1 - \theta_c, 1 + \theta_s]$ (82) to (84); put the excess into $\mathbf{F}_P^{n+1} = (\mathbf{F}_E^{n+1})^{-1}\mathbf{F}^{n+1}$ (86). Hardening: $\mu$ and $\lambda$ scale by $e^{\xi(1 - J_P)}$ (87), stiffer under compression, weaker under stretching, with $\xi$ between 3 and 10. The notes are candid that (87) is empirical and that a principled model must obey thermodynamics; for that they point to the Drucker-Prager sand paper.

Check your understanding

1. Show that both models give $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{0}$ at $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{I}$, and at any rotation.

Neo-Hookean: at $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}$, $\mathbf{F}^{-T} = \mathbf{R}$ and $J = 1$, so $\mu(\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{R}) + 0 = \mathbf{0}$. Fixed corotated: the polar rotation of $\mathbf{R}$ is $\mathbf{R}$, so $2\mu(\mathbf{R} - \mathbf{R}) + 0 = \mathbf{0}$.

2. Which model can survive $J \le 0$, and why does it matter for MPM?

Neo-Hookean contains $\log J$, undefined for $J \le 0$. Fixed corotated is a polynomial in $J$ and $\sigma_i$ and stays finite. MPM evolves $\mathbf{F}$ by a grid velocity gradient that knows nothing about inversion, so a particle can end up inverted after a violent step; the snow model is built on fixed corotated partly for this reason.

Section 7: Governing equations

The physics, in both views: mass conservation $R J = R_0$ (88) or $\frac{D\rho}{Dt} + \rho\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v} = 0$ (90), and momentum $R_0\mathbf{A} = \nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\cdot\mathbf{P} + R_0\mathbf{g}$ (89) or $\rho\frac{D\mathbf{v}}{Dt} = \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma + \rho\mathbf{g}$ (91). Read all of section 7; it is where the two stresses meet.

7.1 Conservation of mass, equations (92) to (102)

Mass in any piece of material is constant: $\int_{B^t}\rho\,d\mathbf{x} = \int_{B^0}R\,J\,d\mathbf{X} = \int_{B^0}R_0\,d\mathbf{X}$ (95), so $RJ = R_0$ (96). To get the Eulerian form the notes need $\dot J$ (99), which uses the derivative of the determinant (100). Here is that derivative, which the notes quote. For a small change $\epsilon\mathbf{G}$,

$$\det(\mathbf{F} + \epsilon\mathbf{G}) = \det\mathbf{F}\,\det(\mathbf{I} + \epsilon\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{G}) \approx J\big(1 + \epsilon\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{G})\big),$$

because $\det(\mathbf{I} + \epsilon\mathbf{M}) = 1 + \epsilon\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{M} + O(\epsilon^2)$. And $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{G}) = \mathbf{F}^{-T}:\mathbf{G}$, so $\partial J/\partial\mathbf{F} = J\mathbf{F}^{-T}$ (100). With (24), $\dot J = J\mathbf{F}^{-T}:\dot{\mathbf{F}} = J\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{F}^{-1}\nabla\mathbf{v}\,\mathbf{F}) = J\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$ (99). Divergence of velocity is the rate of volume change; PB-MPM's liquid constraint is built on the discrete version of this line.

7.2 Conservation of momentum, and the push-forward of stress, equations (103) to (112)

Stress is defined through traction: the force per area across a surface with normal $\mathbf{n}$ is $\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf{n}$ (104). That is the Cauchy stress, measured per current area. Newton's law for a piece of material (105) leads to $\rho\mathbf{a} = \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol\sigma + \mathbf{f}^{\text{ext}}$ (108). Then the step that defines $\mathbf{P}$: pull the surface force back to the rest shape with Nanson's formula (34),

$$\int_{\partial B^t}\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf{n}\,ds = \int_{\partial B^0}\boldsymbol\sigma\,J\mathbf{F}^{-T}\mathbf{N}\,dS = \int_{\partial B^0}\mathbf{P}\mathbf{N}\,dS, \qquad \mathbf{P} = J\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf{F}^{-T}. \tag{109, 110}$$

So $\mathbf{P}$ is the stress that gives the same force when you integrate over the rest surface. Solve for $\boldsymbol\sigma$ and you get (38), $\boldsymbol\sigma = \tfrac1J\mathbf{P}\mathbf{F}^T$. In the worked example of 6.3, $\boldsymbol\sigma = \operatorname{diag}(1346\cdot1.1,\ 635)/1.1 = \operatorname{diag}(1346,\ 577)$ Pa. The $yy$ entries differ by the factor 1.1, exactly the stretch of the faces they act on: the same force spread over a face 10 % longer.

Two side remarks in this section deserve a note. Angular momentum conservation requires $\boldsymbol\sigma$ to be symmetric, which holds for any $\Psi$ that depends on $\mathbf{F}^T\mathbf{F}$ (we will use this to show the MPM grid forces conserve angular momentum). And $\mathbf{P}$ is generally not symmetric, which is why it is less used in engineering but convenient in code: it is what $\Psi$ hands you.

7.3 Weak form, equations (113) to (120)

The step that makes MPM a finite-element method in disguise. Take the Lagrangian momentum equation without external force, $R_0A_i = P_{ij,j}$ (113), (114), multiply by an arbitrary test function $Q_i(\mathbf{X})$ and integrate (115). Integrate by parts, using $Q_iP_{ij,j} = (Q_iP_{ij})_{,j} - Q_{i,j}P_{ij}$ (116), and turn the divergence into a boundary term (117):

$$\int_{\Omega^0}Q_iR_0A_i\,d\mathbf{X} = \int_{\partial\Omega^0}Q_iT_i\,dS - \int_{\Omega^0}Q_{i,j}P_{ij}\,d\mathbf{X}, \tag{118}$$

with $T_i = P_{ij}N_j$ the boundary traction. The stress now appears without a derivative on it; the derivative has moved onto the test function. Finally push to the current configuration: $Q_{i,j} = q_{i,k}F_{kj}$ (119), and $\int Q_{i,j}P_{ij}\,d\mathbf{X} = \int q_{i,k}\,P_{ij}F_{kj}\,d\mathbf{X} = \int q_{i,k}\,J\sigma_{ik}\,d\mathbf{X} = \int q_{i,k}\sigma_{ik}\,d\mathbf{x}$, which gives the Eulerian weak form (120):

$$\int_{\Omega^t}q_i\rho a_i\,d\mathbf{x} = \int_{\partial\Omega^t}q_it_i\,ds - \int_{\Omega^t}q_{i,k}\sigma_{ik}\,d\mathbf{x}. \tag{120}$$

"Holds for every $q$" is what makes this equivalent to the original equation. Section 9 discretises it by letting $q$ range over the grid's basis functions.

Check your understanding

1. Why integrate by parts at all?

Two reasons. The stress is only known at particles (quadrature points), and we cannot differentiate a set of point values; after integration by parts, only the smooth, known basis functions are differentiated. And the boundary term is where tractions and free surfaces enter: a free surface simply means that term is zero.

2. A material has $\boldsymbol\sigma = p\mathbf{I}$ (pure pressure) and $\mathbf{F} = \operatorname{diag}(2, 1)$. What is $\mathbf{P}$?

$\mathbf{P} = J\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf{F}^{-T} = 2p\operatorname{diag}(\tfrac12, 1) = \operatorname{diag}(p, 2p)$. The pressure pushes equally in every direction, but the face with normal $y$ is twice as long as at rest, so per unit rest length it carries twice the force.

Section 9: Discretization

The heart of the notes. Read it slowly, twice. Sections 9.1 to 9.3 discretise the weak form; 9.4 the deformation gradient; 9.5 shows the result is the gradient of an energy. Below, the whole chain is assembled in one place, with the steps the notes leave out.

From the weak form to grid forces, equations (133) to (155)

Time (133) to (135). Replace the acceleration by $(\mathbf{v}^{n+1} - \mathbf{v}^n)/\Delta t$, and do all integrals over the body at time $t^n$. From here the notes switch to Greek letters for components.

Space (136) to (141). Write the test function and both velocities in the grid basis: $q_\alpha = \sum_i q_{i\alpha}N_i(\mathbf{x})$, $v_\alpha^{n+1} = \sum_j v_{j\alpha}^{n+1}N_j(\mathbf{x})$, and so on. Substituting turns the left side into $\sum_{ij}q_{i\alpha}\,m_{ij}(v_{j\alpha}^{n+1} - v_{j\alpha}^n)/\Delta t$ with the mass matrix

$$m_{ij} = \int_{\Omega^{t^n}}N_i\,\rho\,N_j\,d\mathbf{x} \approx \sum_p m_pN_i(\mathbf{x}_p)N_j(\mathbf{x}_p). \tag{142, 143}$$

Choosing $q$ to be 1 for one component of one node and 0 elsewhere (144) gives one equation per grid degree of freedom. That is how "for every test function" becomes "for every node".

Mass lumping (145), (146). The full matrix may be singular (wherever a region has fewer particles than nodes; question 3 below), and it couples every node to its neighbours. Replace each row by its sum. Because $\sum_jN_j = 1$, the row sum is $\int N_i\rho\,d\mathbf{x} \approx \sum_pN_i(\mathbf{x}_p)m_p = m_i$, exactly the P2G mass. So

$$\frac{(m\mathbf{v})_i^{n+1} - (m\mathbf{v})_i^n}{\Delta t} = \underbrace{\int N_i\mathbf{t}\,ds}_{\text{boundary}} - \int\boldsymbol\sigma\nabla N_i\,d\mathbf{x}. \tag{147}$$

The right side is the force on node $i$.

Quadrature (148). Approximate the integral by a sum over particles, each with its current volume $V_p^n$: $\int\boldsymbol\sigma\nabla N_i\,d\mathbf{x} \approx \sum_p\boldsymbol\sigma_p\nabla w_{ip}V_p^n$. This is the sentence in which particles become quadrature points.

Volume (149) to (155). Two ways to get $V_p^n$: from the grid density (152), or from $V_p^n = J_p^nV_p^0$ (153), which is what you use when you track $\mathbf{F}_p$. With (38),

$$\sum_p\boldsymbol\sigma_p\nabla w_{ip}V_p^n = \sum_p\frac1{J_p}\mathbf{P}_p\mathbf{F}_p^T\nabla w_{ip}\,J_pV_p^0 = \sum_p\mathbf{P}_p\mathbf{F}_p^T\nabla w_{ip}V_p^0. \tag{154}$$

The $J$s cancel, and the grid force is

$$\mathbf{f}_i = -\sum_pV_p^0\,\mathbf{P}_p\mathbf{F}_p^T\nabla w_{ip}. \tag{155, 189}$$

This answers the question this chapter opened with: the force formula contains $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{F}^T$ because the weak form is integrated over the current shape, where the natural stress is $\boldsymbol\sigma = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{F}^T/J$, and the $1/J$ is absorbed into the current volume. $\mathbf{P}\mathbf{F}^T = J\boldsymbol\sigma$ is the Kirchhoff stress $\boldsymbol\tau$. Units check: Pa times m$^3$ times 1/m is N.

9.4 Deformation gradient evolution, equations (156) to (162)

Discretise (24) with a backward difference in time and the grid velocity in space: $\nabla\mathbf{v}^{n+1}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_i\mathbf{v}_i^{n+1}(\nabla N_i)^T$ (160), so

$$\mathbf{F}_p^{n+1} = \Big(\mathbf{I} + \Delta t\sum_i\mathbf{v}_i^{n+1}(\nabla w_{ip}^n)^T\Big)\mathbf{F}_p^n. \tag{162}$$

Left multiplication, as in (24). Note which velocity: the new grid velocity, which makes the scheme symplectic Euler.

9.5 Forces as an energy gradient, equations (163) to (171)

The second route to the same force, and the one implicit MPM builds on. Imagine the grid nodes move with their new velocities, $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i = \mathbf{x}_i + \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}_i^{n+1}$ (165), and let (162) define each particle's deformation gradient as a function of those imagined positions (166):

$$\mathbf{F}_p(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \Big(\mathbf{I} + \sum_j(\hat{\mathbf{x}}_j - \mathbf{x}_j)(\nabla w_{jp})^T\Big)\mathbf{F}_p^n.$$

The total elastic energy is $e(\hat{\mathbf{x}}) = \sum_pV_p^0\Psi(\mathbf{F}_p(\hat{\mathbf{x}}))$ (164). Differentiate with respect to component $\alpha$ of node $i$. Only the term $j = i$ depends on $\hat x_{i\alpha}$, and it changes row $\alpha$ of $\mathbf{F}_p$:

$$\frac{\partial(F_p)_{\beta\gamma}}{\partial\hat x_{i\alpha}} = \delta_{\alpha\beta}\sum_\delta(\nabla w_{ip})_\delta(F_p^n)_{\delta\gamma} = \delta_{\alpha\beta}\big((\mathbf{F}_p^n)^T\nabla w_{ip}\big)_\gamma. \tag{168}$$

Then the chain rule, with $\mathbf{P} = \partial\Psi/\partial\mathbf{F}$:

$$\frac{\partial e}{\partial\hat x_{i\alpha}} = \sum_pV_p^0\sum_{\beta\gamma}P_{\beta\gamma}\,\delta_{\alpha\beta}\big((\mathbf{F}_p^n)^T\nabla w_{ip}\big)_\gamma = \sum_pV_p^0\big(\mathbf{P}_p(\mathbf{F}_p^n)^T\nabla w_{ip}\big)_\alpha. \tag{169}$$

Evaluated at $\hat{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x}$ (no motion yet, so $\mathbf{F}_p = \mathbf{F}_p^n$) this is minus the force (155). The weak form and the energy give the same discrete force. That is not a coincidence: it is why MPM conserves energy well and why implicit MPM can be written as optimisation.

Check your understanding

1. Show that the grid forces (155) sum to zero, so internal forces conserve linear momentum.

$\sum_i\mathbf{f}_i = -\sum_pV_p^0\mathbf{P}_p\mathbf{F}_p^T\sum_i\nabla w_{ip}$, and $\sum_i\nabla w_{ip} = \nabla\sum_iw_{ip} = \nabla 1 = \mathbf{0}$.

2. Show they also conserve angular momentum, if $\boldsymbol\sigma$ is symmetric.

Differentiate linear reproduction $\sum_i\mathbf{x}_iw_{ip} = \mathbf{x}_p$ with respect to $\mathbf{x}_p$: $\sum_i\mathbf{x}_i(\nabla w_{ip})^T = \mathbf{I}$. The torque is $\sum_i\mathbf{x}_i\times\mathbf{f}_i$, whose components are $-\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\sum_pV_p^0\,\tau_{\gamma\delta}\sum_i x_{i\beta}(\nabla w_{ip})_\delta = -\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\sum_pV_p^0\tau_{\gamma\beta}$, with $\boldsymbol\tau = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{F}^T$. Contracting the antisymmetric $\epsilon$ with a symmetric $\boldsymbol\tau$ gives zero. Combined with APIC transfers, the whole explicit step conserves angular momentum.

3. Why does the mass matrix in (143) satisfy $\mathbf{z}^T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{z} \ge 0$, and why can it still be singular?

$\mathbf{z}^T\mathbf{M}\mathbf{z} = \sum_pm_p(\sum_iz_iN_{ip})^2 \ge 0$. It is singular whenever some nonzero $\mathbf{z}$ makes $\sum_iz_iN_{ip} = 0$ at every particle, which happens easily when there are fewer particles than nodes in a region, for instance near a free surface. Lumping replaces it by the diagonal $m_i$, which is positive at every node that any particle touches.

Section 10: Explicit time integration

Practitioner's summary of everything so far. Read it with your chapter-24 code open.

10.1 APIC transfers, equations (172) to (180)

The APIC transfers (172) to (176) exactly as in the APIC paper, with $\mathbf{D}_p = \tfrac14\Delta x^2\mathbf{I}$ for quadratic weights. Equations (177) to (180) give the version for linear weights that stores $\mathbf{C}_p$ directly, with $\mathbf{C}_p = \sum_i\mathbf{v}_i(\nabla w_{ip})^T$ (180); the notes point out that this is the velocity gradient the $\mathbf{F}$ update already computes. Both statements are derived in chapter 28.

What the notes do not say, and what came next: MLS-MPM. For quadratic weights, APIC's $\mathbf{C}_p = \tfrac{4}{\Delta x^2}\sum_iw_{ip}\mathbf{v}_i(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)^T$ and the $\mathbf{F}$ update's $\sum_i\mathbf{v}_i(\nabla w_{ip})^T$ are two estimates of the same velocity gradient. MLS-MPM (Hu et al. 2018) uses the first for both, which amounts to replacing $\nabla w_{ip}$ everywhere by $\tfrac{4}{\Delta x^2}w_{ip}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)$ (for linear weights this is the trilinear identity; for quadratic it is the gradient of a moving-least-squares fit). Substitute into P2G plus the force (155), and the grid momentum after forces becomes one sum:

$$(m\mathbf{v})_i + \Delta t\,\mathbf{f}_i = \sum_pw_{ip}\Big[m_p\mathbf{v}_p + \Big(m_p\mathbf{C}_p - \frac{4\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}V_p^0\mathbf{P}_p\mathbf{F}_p^T\Big)(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)\Big],$$

with $\mathbf{F}_p \leftarrow (\mathbf{I} + \Delta t\,\mathbf{C}_p)\mathbf{F}_p$ after G2P. One loop, one matrix per particle, no weight gradients. Because the same approximate gradient is used in the force and in the $\mathbf{F}$ update, the force is still exactly minus the gradient of $\sum_pV_p^0\Psi(\mathbf{F}_p(\hat{\mathbf{x}}))$: redo the derivation of (169) with the replaced gradient and nothing changes. Momentum is still conserved because $\sum_iw_{ip}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p) = \mathbf{0}$. This is the scheme PB-MPM uses for its transfers.

10.2 to 10.4 F update, state update, forces, equations (181) to (190)

(181) is (162) again. (182), (183) are symplectic Euler on the grid: velocity first, then (imaginary) positions. (184) to (186) make the point that the grid positions are a function of the grid velocities, so the explicit force is the force "at zero grid motion" (185). (187) to (189) restate the energy-gradient force; (190) gives the Cauchy-stress form for materials with no energy, $\mathbf{f}_i = -\sum_pV_p^n\boldsymbol\sigma_p\nabla w_{ip}$, which is how you would write a fluid with an equation of state.

10.5 The full algorithm

Eight steps. Here they are with what each one is for and what to watch out for.

  1. P2G. Grid mass and momentum with APIC (172), (173).
  2. Grid velocities, $\mathbf{v}_i = (m\mathbf{v})_i/m_i$. Nodes with zero mass get zero velocity. Read the notes' remark here: compare against exactly 0, not a small threshold. Tiny masses give large velocities but are weighted by tiny weights again on the way back; a threshold breaks momentum conservation.
  3. Identify grid degrees of freedom: the nodes with mass. Only these are unknowns, which matters for the implicit solve.
  4. Grid forces (189).
  5. Grid velocity update (183), then boundary conditions and collisions (section 12.1). Because the update is explicit, each node can be overwritten independently.
  6. Update $\mathbf{F}_p$ with (181). The grid never actually moves.
  7. G2P: new $\mathbf{v}_p$ and $\mathbf{B}_p$ (175), (176).
  8. Advect: $\mathbf{x}_p \leftarrow \mathbf{x}_p + \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}_p$. The notes remark that with FLIP you must instead interpolate the moved grid positions; for PIC and APIC the two are identical.

In code, the skeleton of one step (2D, quadratic weights; each loop is over a particle's 3 by 3 stencil):

// 1. P2G (APIC) and 4. forces, in one particle loop
for (const p of particles) {
  const C = scale(p.B, 4 / (dx * dx));
  const tau = mul(P(p.F), transpose(p.F));           // Kirchhoff stress P F^T
  forEachNode(p, (i, w, gradW, r) => {               // r = x_i - x_p
    grid.m[i] += w * p.m;
    grid.mv[i] = add(grid.mv[i], scale(add(p.v, mulVec(C, r)), w * p.m));
    grid.f[i] = sub(grid.f[i], scale(mulVec(tau, gradW), p.V0));
  });
}
// 2., 3., 5. grid velocities, forces, boundaries
for (const i of grid.activeNodes()) {
  grid.v[i] = scale(add(grid.mv[i], scale(grid.f[i], h)), 1 / grid.m[i]);
  applyBoundary(i);
}
// 6., 7., 8. F update, G2P, advect
for (const p of particles) {
  let v = zero(), B = zeroMat(), gradV = zeroMat();
  forEachNode(p, (i, w, gradW, r) => {
    v = add(v, scale(grid.v[i], w));
    B = add(B, scale(outer(grid.v[i], r), w));
    gradV = add(gradV, outer(grid.v[i], gradW));
  });
  p.F = mul(add(I, scale(gradV, h)), p.F);
  p.v = v; p.B = B;
  p.x = add(p.x, scale(v, h));
}

What the notes leave out: the time step. Explicit MPM is only conditionally stable, like the symplectic Euler spring of chapter 3. Two limits apply: particles should move less than a cell per step, $h \lesssim \Delta x/|\mathbf{v}|_{\max}$, and elastic waves should not cross more than a cell per step, $h \lesssim \Delta x/c$ with wave speed $c = \sqrt{(\lambda + 2\mu)/\rho}$. For the jelly above ($E = 10^4$ Pa, $\nu = 0.3$, $\rho = 1000$ kg/m$^3$), $c = 3.7$ m/s, so with $\Delta x = 1$ cm the step must be well under 2.7 ms; codes use a safety factor of about 0.5, about 1.4 ms. Chapter 24 has the details.

Check your understanding

1. In step 6, why use the new grid velocity rather than the old one?

Using $\mathbf{v}^{n+1}$ for both the $\mathbf{F}$ update and the particle advection, after computing the force from $\mathbf{F}^n$, is symplectic Euler: force from the old configuration, then positions (here, the configuration measured by $\mathbf{F}$) with the new velocity. Using the old velocity would be explicit Euler, which gains energy.

2. Where does the MLS-MPM update put the stress, and why is no separate force loop needed?

The term $-\tfrac{4\Delta t}{\Delta x^2}V_p^0\mathbf{P}_p\mathbf{F}_p^T$ is added to $m_p\mathbf{C}_p$, so the stress enters as an extra affine momentum contribution that P2G spreads to the nodes. The force loop and the P2G loop have the same structure once $\nabla w_{ip}$ is replaced by $w_{ip}\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)$, so they merge.

11.1 Force derivative, equations (191) to (198)

For Newton's method we need how the grid forces change when the grid nodes move. Perturb the moved positions by a small $\mathbf{u}_j$ at each node. From (193), the particle deformation gradient changes by

$$\delta\mathbf{F}_p = \sum_j\mathbf{u}_j(\nabla w_{jp})^T\mathbf{F}_p^n,$$

the stress by $\delta\mathbf{P}_p = \frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial\mathbf{F}\partial\mathbf{F}}:\delta\mathbf{F}_p = \mathbf{A}_p$ (197), and since (194) is linear in $\mathbf{P}$,

$$-\delta\mathbf{f}_i = \sum_j\frac{\partial^2e}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_j}\mathbf{u}_j = \sum_pV_p^0\mathbf{A}_p(\mathbf{F}_p^n)^T\nabla w_{ip}. \tag{196}$$

Read (196) as an algorithm: interpolate the node perturbations to a $\delta\mathbf{F}_p$ per particle (a G2P-like pass), apply the material's stress differential (section 6.3's $\delta\mathbf{P}$), and splat back like a force (a P2G-like pass). This is the "matrix-free" Hessian product that a Krylov solver needs; you never build the matrix.

11.2 to 11.4 Backward Euler, Newton, linearisation, equations (199) to (203)

Backward Euler evaluates the force at the end of the step (199): $\mathbf{v}_i^{n+1} = \mathbf{v}_i^n + \Delta t\,\mathbf{f}_i(\mathbf{x}^n + \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}^{n+1})/m_i$. Collected for all nodes, it is a nonlinear system (200):

$$\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{M}\mathbf{v} - \Delta t\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^n + \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}) - \mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}^n = \mathbf{0}.$$

Newton's method (201) repeats $\mathbf{v}^{(k+1)} = \mathbf{v}^{(k)} - \big(\partial\mathbf{h}/\partial\mathbf{v}\big)^{-1}\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{v}^{(k)})$, and by the chain rule $\partial\mathbf{h}/\partial\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{M} + \Delta t^2\,\partial^2e/\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}^2$: mass plus time step squared times the energy Hessian (11.1). Each Newton iteration recomputes every $\mathbf{F}_p$ from the current velocity guess, so keep $\mathbf{F}_p^n$ stored. The notes suggest MINRES for the linear solve; MINRES handles symmetric indefinite matrices, which you get when the energy is non-convex (see the warning below and chapter 9).

Section 11.4's linearised update is dense on the page; spelled out, it reads: blend the end-of-step force, $\mathbf{v}_i^{n+1} = \mathbf{v}_i^n + \Delta t\,m_i^{-1}\big((1 - \beta)\mathbf{f}_i^n + \beta\mathbf{f}_i^{n+1}\big)$, and linearise $\mathbf{f}^{n+1} \approx \mathbf{f}^n + \sum_j\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_i}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_j}\Delta t\,\mathbf{v}_j^{n+1}$. Rearranging,

$$\sum_j\Big(\delta_{ij}\mathbf{I} + \beta\,\Delta t^2m_i^{-1}\frac{\partial^2e^n}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_j}\Big)\mathbf{v}_j^{n+1} = \mathbf{v}_i^*, \qquad \mathbf{v}_i^* = \mathbf{v}_i^n + \Delta t\,m_i^{-1}\mathbf{f}_i^n. \tag{202, 203}$$

$\beta = 0$ is explicit, $\tfrac12$ trapezoidal, 1 backward Euler. Multiply row $i$ by $m_i$ and the matrix becomes $\mathbf{M} + \beta\Delta t^2\mathbf{H}$, symmetric, which is what "mass symmetric" means. The notes claim this equals one Newton step from a zero initial guess; check it: with $\mathbf{v}^{(0)} = \mathbf{0}$, $\mathbf{h}(\mathbf{0}) = -\Delta t\,\mathbf{f}^n - \mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}^n$ and the Jacobian is $\mathbf{M} + \Delta t^2\mathbf{H}(\mathbf{x}^n)$, so one Newton step solves $(\mathbf{M} + \Delta t^2\mathbf{H})\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}^n + \Delta t\,\mathbf{f}^n$, which is (202) with $\beta = 1$.

11.5 The optimisation integrator, equations (204) and (205)

The notes claim that solving (204) is equivalent to minimising

$$E(\mathbf{v}) = \sum_i\tfrac12m_i\|\mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_i^n\|^2 + e(\mathbf{x}^n + \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}). \tag{205}$$

Proof in one line: the gradient with respect to $\mathbf{v}_i$ is $m_i(\mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_i^n) + \Delta t\,\partial e/\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i = m_i(\mathbf{v}_i - \mathbf{v}_i^n) - \Delta t\,\mathbf{f}_i = \mathbf{h}_i(\mathbf{v})$. So stationary points of $E$ are exactly backward Euler solutions, and the Hessian of $E$ is the Newton matrix above. Why this helps: an optimisation problem lets Newton's method use a line search (never accept a step that increases $E$), which makes it converge from any starting guess and at large time steps. The catch: the Newton direction is only guaranteed to go downhill if the Hessian is positive definite, so practical codes modify it (project each particle's $\partial^2\Psi/\partial\mathbf{F}^2$ to its positive part). That definiteness fix is the pitfall the PB-MPM paper cites via Smith et al. (2019).

Non-convex energies make the Hessian indefinite. Fixed corotated and neo-Hookean are not convex in $\mathbf{F}$ (compression past inversion, buckling). Then $\mathbf{M} + \Delta t^2\mathbf{H}$ can have negative eigenvalues at large $\Delta t$, CG can fail, and an unmodified Newton step can go uphill. This is the main source of complexity in implicit MPM, and the reason PB-MPM exists.

Check your understanding

1. What does a semi-implicit step ($\beta = 1$, one linear solve) give up compared with full Newton?

It linearises the force around the start of the step, so for large deformations within one step the force is wrong; it can still blow up at large $\Delta t$ for strongly nonlinear materials. Full Newton with a line search on (205) iterates until the true backward Euler equations hold.

2. Why is backward Euler "stable" but still damped?

The same reason as for one spring in chapter 3: each mode's amplitude shrinks by about $1/(1 + \Delta t^2\omega^2)$ per step. The stiffest modes, which explicit methods cannot handle, are simply damped out.

12.1 Collision objects

Collisions are applied to grid velocities right after forces (and optionally to particle velocities before advection). The recipe, which is chapter 15's Coulomb friction on velocities: with object velocity $\mathbf{v}_{co}$ and normal $\mathbf{n}$ from a level set $\phi$,

  1. $\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}} = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_{co}$, $v_n = \mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}\cdot\mathbf{n}$. If $v_n \ge 0$ the bodies separate: do nothing.
  2. $\mathbf{v}_t = \mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}} - v_n\mathbf{n}$. If $\|\mathbf{v}_t\| \le -\mu_kv_n$, stick: $\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}' = \mathbf{0}$.
  3. Otherwise slide: $\mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}' = \mathbf{v}_t + \mu_kv_n\,\mathbf{v}_t/\|\mathbf{v}_t\|$ (the tangential speed drops by $\mu_k|v_n|$).
  4. $\mathbf{v}' = \mathbf{v}_{\text{rel}}' + \mathbf{v}_{co}$.

The section also covers moving level sets and "sticky" collisions for overhangs; skim.

Summary

  • Sections 5 to 7 build continuum mechanics: $\mathbf{F} = \partial\mathbf{x}/\partial\mathbf{X}$, $\dot{\mathbf{F}} = (\nabla\mathbf{v})\mathbf{F}$, $\dot J = J\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$, Nanson's formula, and the two stresses related by $\mathbf{P} = J\boldsymbol\sigma\mathbf{F}^{-T}$.
  • The weak form moves the derivative off the stress onto test functions; with grid basis functions, a lumped mass matrix and particles as quadrature points it gives $\mathbf{f}_i = -\sum_pV_p^0\mathbf{P}_p\mathbf{F}_p^T\nabla w_{ip}$.
  • The same force is minus the gradient of $\sum_pV_p^0\Psi(\mathbf{F}_p(\hat{\mathbf{x}}))$ with respect to imagined node positions, which is the bridge to implicit MPM.
  • Explicit MPM is eight steps: P2G, grid velocity, active nodes, forces, grid update with collisions, $\mathbf{F}$ update, G2P, advect. MLS-MPM fuses the force into P2G.
  • Implicit MPM solves $\mathbf{M}\mathbf{v} - \Delta t\,\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x}^n + \Delta t\,\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{M}\mathbf{v}^n$ with Newton; it is equivalent to minimising (205), which makes line searches possible but needs definiteness fixes.
