Iterative and Direct Solvers

Every constraint solver is secretly solving a linear system. Knowing which one, and how, tells you when to iterate and when to stop iterating.

The last two chapters measured something uncomfortable: a stiff chain with a heavy weight needs hundreds or thousands of Gauss-Seidel sweeps to reach its true stretch, and substepping, for all its virtues, only pushes the problem back. Both chapters waved at "the slowest error pattern" and "the spectral radius" without computing them. This chapter does.

We will write one XPBD step as a linear system, in two equivalent forms. We will look at Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel as what they are, iterative methods for that system, and compute by hand how fast they converge, which explains why a chain of $n$ links needs about $n^2$ sweeps. Then we will throw iteration away and solve the chain's system exactly, in time proportional to $n$, with a twenty-line algorithm from 1949. The same idea extends to any tree-shaped mechanism, and with one more trick to a tree plus a few loops.

By the end you will be able to implement a direct chain solver, predict how many iterations an iterative solver needs from the size and the masses of the system, and judge when a direct solve is worth it.

One step, one linear system

In chapter 7 we derived XPBD from implicit Euler and arrived at a Newton step. Write $\mathbf{J} = \nabla\mathbf{C}$ for the constraint Jacobian (one row per constraint, one column per coordinate) and evaluate it at the current positions. With the approximation $\mathbf{K} \approx \mathbf{M}$, one Newton step solves

$$\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{M} & -\mathbf{J}^T \\ \mathbf{J} & \tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \Delta\mathbf{x} \\ \Delta\boldsymbol\lambda \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{0} \\ -\mathbf{C} - \tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha}\,\boldsymbol\lambda \end{pmatrix}.$$

This is the KKT form (after Karush, Kuhn and Tucker, who studied such systems in optimisation): positions and multipliers are both unknowns. The first row gives $\Delta\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{J}^T\Delta\boldsymbol \lambda$; substituting it into the second row eliminates the positions and leaves the Schur complement form, one equation per constraint:

$$\underbrace{\left(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{J}^T + \tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha}\right)}_{\mathbf{A}} \Delta\boldsymbol\lambda = \underbrace{-\mathbf{C} - \tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha}\,\boldsymbol\lambda}_{\mathbf{b}} .$$

Entry $(j, k)$ of $\mathbf{A}$ is $\sum_i w_i\,\nabla_i C_j \cdot \nabla_i C_k$, summed over the particles the two constraints share. It is zero unless constraints $j$ and $k$ touch a common particle. So $\mathbf{A}$ is sparse, and its pattern is the "who shares a particle with whom" graph of the constraints.

For a chain hanging from a fixed point, number the links $j = 0, \dots, n-1$ from the top; link $j$ joins particles $j$ and $j+1$, with unit direction $\mathbf{n}_j$. Its gradient is $-\mathbf{n}_j$ on particle $j$ and $+\mathbf{n}_j$ on particle $j+1$. Link $j$ shares a particle only with links $j-1$ and $j+1$, so $\mathbf{A}$ is tridiagonal:

$$A_{jj} = w_j + w_{j+1} + \tilde\alpha, \qquad A_{j,j+1} = A_{j+1,j} = -w_{j+1}\,\mathbf{n}_j\cdot\mathbf{n}_{j+1}.$$

Keep this matrix in mind; everything in the chapter is about solving it.

Gauss-Seidel and Jacobi are linear solvers

Look again at the XPBD update for constraint $j$:

$$\Delta\lambda_j \leftarrow \frac{b_j - \sum_{k\ne j} A_{jk}\,\Delta\lambda_k}{A_{jj}} .$$

That is not how chapter 7 wrote it, but it is the same thing: after the earlier constraints have moved the particles, the current $-C_j - \tilde\alpha\lambda_j$ already contains their influence, which is exactly the $-\sum_{k \ne j} A_{jk}\Delta\lambda_k$ term to first order. Solving row $j$ for its own unknown with the others held fixed, and using each new value immediately, is the textbook Gauss-Seidel method. Computing every row from the previous sweep's values and applying them all at once is Jacobi. PBD's position updates are these two methods applied to the nonlinear problem, one linearisation per constraint visit.

Both are linear iterations. Split $\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{D} + \mathbf{L} + \mathbf{U}$ into its diagonal, strictly lower and strictly upper parts. Then one sweep is

$$\text{Jacobi: } \mathbf{D}\,\mathbf{z}^{k+1} = \mathbf{b} - (\mathbf{L} + \mathbf{U})\,\mathbf{z}^k, \qquad \text{Gauss-Seidel: } (\mathbf{D} + \mathbf{L})\,\mathbf{z}^{k+1} = \mathbf{b} - \mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{z}^k .$$

Subtract the same equations written for the exact solution $\mathbf{z}^*$, and the error $\mathbf{e}^k = \mathbf{z}^k - \mathbf{z}^*$ obeys $\mathbf{e}^{k+1} = \mathbf{G}\,\mathbf{e}^k$, with

$$\mathbf{G}_{\text{J}} = -\mathbf{D}^{-1}(\mathbf{L} + \mathbf{U}), \qquad \mathbf{G}_{\text{GS}} = -(\mathbf{D} + \mathbf{L})^{-1}\mathbf{U}.$$

Every sweep multiplies the error by the same matrix. As in chapter 3, where every time step multiplied the state by the same matrix, what decides the long-run behaviour is the largest eigenvalue magnitude, the spectral radius $\rho(\mathbf{G})$. The error shrinks like $\rho^k$; the iteration converges if and only if $\rho \lt 1$; and the number of sweeps needed to reduce the error by a factor of 100 is about $\ln 100 / (-\ln\rho)$. The $\mu$ of chapter 8 is $\rho(\mathbf{G}_{\text{GS}})$.

Computing the spectral radius by hand

Take the smallest interesting case: a chain of two rigid links ($\tilde\alpha = 0$), two particles of equal inverse mass $w$ below a fixed point. Link 0 touches the fixed particle ($w_0 = 0$) and particle 1; link 1 touches particles 1 and 2. So

$$\mathbf{A} = w\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix},\qquad \mathbf{G}_{\text{J}} = -\mathbf{D}^{-1}(\mathbf{L}+\mathbf{U}) = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ \tfrac12 & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The eigenvalues $\mu$ of $\mathbf{G}_{\text{J}}$ satisfy $\mu^2 - \tfrac12 = 0$, so $\mu = \pm 1/\sqrt2$ and $\rho_{\text{J}} = 0.7071$. For Gauss-Seidel,

$$\mathbf{G}_{\text{GS}} = -\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & \tfrac12 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \rho_{\text{GS}} = \tfrac12 = \rho_{\text{J}}^2 .$$

Gauss-Seidel halves the error every sweep; Jacobi needs two sweeps to do the same. The relation $\rho_{\text{GS}} = \rho_{\text{J}}^2$ is no accident: it holds for every tridiagonal matrix (more generally, for "consistently ordered" ones), so Gauss-Seidel always needs half as many sweeps as Jacobi on a chain.

With three links, $\mathbf{A} = w\,(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1&0\\-1&2&-1\\0&-1&2\end{smallmatrix})$ and the characteristic polynomial of $\mathbf{G}_{\text{J}}$ is $\mu^3 - \tfrac34\mu$, so $\rho_{\text{J}} = \sqrt3/2 = 0.8660$ and $\rho_{\text{GS}} = 0.75$. A pattern appears, $\rho_{\text{J}} = \cos(\pi/4)$, then $\cos(\pi/6)$, and it continues: for a uniform chain of $n$ rigid links hanging from a fixed point,

$$\rho_{\text{J}} = \cos\frac{\pi}{2n}, \qquad \rho_{\text{GS}} = \cos^2\frac{\pi}{2n} \approx 1 - \frac{\pi^2}{4n^2}.$$

(The eigenvectors of such a matrix are discrete sine waves along the chain, and the slowest one is a quarter of a sine wave, the smoothest pattern the chain can carry. In positions, it is the "whole chain moves together" pattern of chapter 8.)

Now the payoff. Since $-\ln\rho_{\text{GS}} \approx \pi^2/(4n^2)$, the number of Gauss-Seidel sweeps to reduce the error a hundredfold is

$$k_{1\%} \approx \frac{4\ln 100}{\pi^2}\,n^2 = 1.87\,n^2 .$$

Doubling the chain quadruples the number of sweeps, on top of doubling the cost of each sweep. The demo's "count sweeps" button measures this for a uniform rigid chain:

Why $n^2$? In a Jacobi sweep, each constraint only hears from its immediate neighbours, so information travels one link per sweep. Worse, it does not travel cleanly: each sweep averages a constraint with its neighbours, so the error diffuses along the chain, and diffusion covers a distance of about $\sqrt{k}$ in $k$ steps. To communicate across $n$ links takes about $n^2$ sweeps. Gauss-Seidel sends information down the whole chain in one sweep (in the order it visits the links) but back up only one link per sweep, which buys a factor of two and nothing more. Any method that only talks to neighbours has this problem; that is why long chains, tall stacks and fine cloth meshes are hard for iterative solvers.

When are these iterations guaranteed to work?

$\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{J}^T + \tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha}$ is symmetric. It is also positive definite (SPD), meaning $\mathbf{z}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} \gt 0$ for every nonzero $\mathbf{z}$, as long as the constraints are independent (no row of $\mathbf{J}$ is a combination of the others) or every constraint has some compliance. To see why: $\mathbf{z}^T\mathbf{A}\mathbf{z} = |\mathbf{M}^{-1/2}\mathbf{J}^T\mathbf{z}|^2 + \mathbf{z}^T\tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha}\mathbf{z}$, which is a squared length plus something non-negative.

That buys two guarantees. Gauss-Seidel converges on every SPD matrix, in any visiting order. Jacobi needs more: it converges, for example, when $\mathbf{A}$ is diagonally dominant (each diagonal entry at least the sum of the magnitudes of the others in its row, strictly so in at least one row of a connected system), which the chain matrix is, but not on every SPD matrix. Also note the sign of the Jacobi eigenvalues: for a chain (and any system whose constraint graph can be coloured with two colours) they come in pairs $\pm\mu$. The negative ones make the error flip sign every sweep, so a Jacobi step overshoots. This is why position-based Jacobi solvers under-relax, multiplying each correction by a factor below 1 or averaging the corrections a particle receives. The starter for this chapter contains an under-relaxed Jacobi sweep; without the factor of 0.5 it blows up.

Redundant constraints, such as a cloth with both edge and diagonal springs pinned at both ends, make $\mathbf{A}$ singular when $\tilde\alpha = 0$. Gauss-Seidel tolerates this as long as the constraints are consistent; a direct solver divides by zero. A small compliance on every constraint removes the problem: it adds $\tilde\alpha$ to every diagonal entry, a regularisation, and is one more reason to prefer XPBD's soft constraints over perfectly rigid ones.

The direct alternative: the Thomas algorithm

A tridiagonal system can be solved exactly with Gaussian elimination, and because every row has at most three entries, the elimination costs a few operations per row. Write the system as

$$a_i\,z_{i-1} + b_i\,z_i + c_i\,z_{i+1} = d_i, \qquad i = 0, \dots, n-1,$$

with $a_0 = c_{n-1} = 0$. Row 0 alone gives $z_0$ in terms of $z_1$:

$$z_0 = d'_0 - c'_0\,z_1, \qquad c'_0 = \frac{c_0}{b_0},\quad d'_0 = \frac{d_0}{b_0}.$$

Suppose that, after processing rows $0$ to $i-1$, we have $z_{i-1} = d'_{i-1} - c'_{i-1} z_i$. Substitute that into row $i$:

$$a_i\,(d'_{i-1} - c'_{i-1}z_i) + b_i\,z_i + c_i\,z_{i+1} = d_i \;\Longrightarrow\; z_i = \underbrace{\frac{d_i - a_i d'_{i-1}}{b_i - a_i c'_{i-1}}}_{d'_i} - \underbrace{\frac{c_i}{b_i - a_i c'_{i-1}}}_{c'_i}\,z_{i+1}.$$

That is the same form one row further down, so the recursion runs to the bottom. The last row has no $z_n$, so it gives $z_{n-1} = d'_{n-1}$ directly, and then $z_i = d'_i - c'_i z_{i+1}$ runs back up. This is the Thomas algorithm, named after Llewellyn Thomas, who described it in 1949. Two passes, about $8n$ arithmetic operations, and the answer is exact up to rounding:

function thomasSolve(a, b, c, d, z, n) {
  cp[0] = c[0] / b[0];
  dp[0] = d[0] / b[0];
  for (let i = 1; i < n; i++) {                 // forward elimination
    const m = b[i] - a[i] * cp[i - 1];
    cp[i] = c[i] / m;
    dp[i] = (d[i] - a[i] * dp[i - 1]) / m;
  }
  z[n - 1] = dp[n - 1];
  for (let i = n - 2; i >= 0; i--) z[i] = dp[i] - cp[i] * z[i + 1];   // back substitution
}

The quantity $m = b_i - a_i c'_{i-1}$ is the pivot. Thomas does no pivoting (no row swaps), which is safe when the matrix is SPD or diagonally dominant, as ours is: every pivot stays positive.

For the chain, one direct step is: predict; compute every link's direction $\mathbf{n}_j$ and violation $C_j$ at the predicted positions; fill in the three diagonals; solve for $\Delta\boldsymbol\lambda$; and move each particle by $\Delta\mathbf{x}_i = w_i(\mathbf{n}_{i-1}\Delta\lambda_{i-1} - \mathbf{n}_i\Delta\lambda_i)$, the sum of the two links it belongs to.

The direct chain sits on the green line; its error readout shows 0.0000 mm, and the actual error is around $10^{-13}$ mm, which is rounding. And it is cheap. It builds the system for a uniform rigid chain, solves it with your Thomas algorithm, then counts the Jacobi and Gauss-Seidel sweeps to get within 1 % of that answer, and prints the observed error reduction per sweep.

Now press "swing both weights". The direct chain explodes. The Gauss-Seidel chain, inaccurate as it is, does not. The direct solver solves the linearised system exactly, with every link direction frozen at the predicted positions. When the chain swings, each light bead between two tight links has a fast sideways vibration, with angular frequency about $\sqrt{2T/(mL)}$ for tension $T$, bead mass $m$ and link length $L$. Near the weight that is $\sqrt{2 \times 19.6/(0.02 \times 0.04)} = 221$ rad/s, so $h\omega = 3.7$. Freezing the directions treats that vibration explicitly, and chapter 3 told you what explicit integration does to a mode with $h\omega$ that large. Raise "direct: substeps per frame" to 8 ($h_s\omega = 0.46$) and the swing is calm again. Gauss-Seidel gets away with it because it re-linearises at every single constraint projection. The term we dropped when we wrote $\mathbf{K} \approx \mathbf{M}$, the geometric stiffness $\boldsymbol\lambda\cdot\nabla^2\mathbf{C}$, is exactly what would make the direct solve stable at large steps; Tournier and colleagues, Stable Constrained Dynamics (2015), show how to include it.

The lesson: a direct solver removes solver error, all of it, in one go. It does nothing about linearisation error. Real direct-solver simulators therefore combine it with substeps, a few Newton iterations, or geometric stiffness.

Beyond chains: trees

A rope is a chain, but a tree, a rope ladder, a creature's skeleton or a branching tentacle is not. Their constraint graphs branch. Is a direct solve still linear time?

Think about what Gaussian elimination does to the graph of a sparse matrix. Eliminating unknown $z_j$ means using its row to remove $z_j$ from every other row that mentions it. If $z_j$ was coupled to unknowns $p$ and $q$, those two rows each pick up a multiple of row $j$, and they become coupled to each other: fill-in, a new nonzero entry. But if $z_j$ is coupled to only one other unknown, a leaf of the graph, eliminating it changes only that neighbour's diagonal entry and right-hand side, and creates nothing new. Thomas's algorithm is exactly this: it eliminates the chain from one end, and each time the next link is a leaf.

Any tree can be eliminated the same way: always eliminate a leaf (children before parents), then substitute back from the root out to the leaves. Each elimination costs a constant amount per edge, so the whole solve is $O(n)$, and no fill-in ever appears.

There is one subtlety, and it is the heart of David Baraff's 1996 paper Linear-Time Dynamics using Lagrange Multipliers. In the Schur form $\mathbf{J}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{J}^T$, two constraints are coupled whenever they share a body. A body with three joints attached makes those three constraints mutually coupled, a triangle in the graph, and triangles are not trees. Baraff's way out is to solve the bigger KKT system instead. Its graph has a node for every body and a node for every constraint, with an edge between a constraint and each body it touches. For a mechanism without loops, that graph is a tree, whatever the branching. Ordering the elimination from the leaves to the root then factors the KKT matrix with no fill-in, in time linear in the number of bodies. Eliminating the positions first, as the Schur form does, is what creates the triangles; keeping them avoids it. Sometimes the bigger system is the sparser one.

Trees with a few loops

Close a loop, by tying the end of a rope back to the support or by adding a crossbar to a ladder, and the graph is no longer a tree. Eliminating around a loop produces fill-in. If there are only a few such loop-closing constraints, there is a neat way around it. Split the unknowns into the tree part $T$ and the $k$ extra constraints $E$:

$$\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{A}_{TT} & \mathbf{A}_{TE} \\ \mathbf{A}_{ET} & \mathbf{A}_{EE} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{z}_T \\ \mathbf{z}_E \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{b}_T \\ \mathbf{b}_E \end{pmatrix}.$$

The first row gives $\mathbf{z}_T = \mathbf{A}_{TT}^{-1}(\mathbf{b}_T - \mathbf{A}_{TE}\mathbf{z}_E)$, and every product with $\mathbf{A}_{TT}^{-1}$ is a linear-time tree solve. Substituting into the second row leaves a small dense $k \times k$ system, again a Schur complement:

$$\left(\mathbf{A}_{EE} - \mathbf{A}_{ET}\mathbf{A}_{TT}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{TE}\right)\mathbf{z}_E = \mathbf{b}_E - \mathbf{A}_{ET}\mathbf{A}_{TT}^{-1}\mathbf{b}_T .$$

Building it takes $k$ tree solves (one per column of $\mathbf{A}_{TE}$), solving it takes about $k^3$ operations, and then one more tree solve gives $\mathbf{z}_T$. The total is $O(kn + k^3)$: still linear in the size of the mechanism, as long as the number of loops is small. This technique is called bordering, after the border of extra rows and columns around the tree matrix. For cloth or a solid, where nearly every constraint closes a loop, it is hopeless, and that is where iterative methods, or general sparse factorisations, take over again.

Conditioning, precision and scaling

A direct solve is exact only up to rounding, and how much the rounding is amplified depends on the condition number $\kappa = \lambda_{\max}/\lambda_{\min}$ of the matrix (the ratio of its largest and smallest eigenvalues). The relative error of the solution can be up to about $\kappa$ times the machine precision $\varepsilon$. For the demo's 50-link rigid chain:

$\kappa\,\varepsilon$, single ($\varepsilon \approx 6\times10^{-8}$)$2.5\times10^{-4}$$1.4\times10^{-3}$$1.2\times10^{-2}$

In double precision, which is what JavaScript uses, the error is invisible. In single precision, as on most GPUs, a mass ratio of 1000 can leave a percent of error in the multipliers, which shows up as a visible jitter in a hanging chain. The same condition number also governs iterative convergence: the slow eigenvalue that limits Gauss-Seidel is the small one at the bottom of $\kappa$. Heavy-on-light is ill-conditioned for every solver; direct solvers pay in precision, iterative ones in sweeps.

Scaling matters when constraints of different kinds share a system. A distance constraint has its multiplier in N s2 and its row entries in kg-1; an angle constraint on a rigid body has them in N m s2 and kg-1 m-2. Change your length unit from metres to millimetres and the angle rows' entries shrink by a factor of a million while the distance rows' stay the same, so $\kappa$ changes enormously although the physics has not changed at all. Gauss-Seidel does not care, because it divides each row by its own diagonal (that is, it is invariant to scaling the rows). A direct or Krylov solver does: scale each row and column by $1/\sqrt{A_{jj}}$ first, the Jacobi preconditioner, and pick units that keep the numbers near 1.

function thomasSolve(a, b, c, d, x, n) {
  cp[0] = c[0] / b[0];
  dp[0] = d[0] / b[0];
  for (let i = 1; i < n; i++) {
    const m = b[i] - a[i] * cp[i - 1];
    cp[i] = c[i] / m;
    dp[i] = (d[i] - a[i] * dp[i - 1]) / m;

  x[n - 1] = dp[n - 1];
  for (let i = n - 2; i >= 0; i--) x[i] = dp[i] - cp[i] * x[i + 1];
}

where the papers solve linear systems

Implicit MPM, which you will build in chapter 26, needs to solve a large sparse SPD system on the grid every step; the MPM course notes, in their section on implicit time integration, treat the step as a Newton method whose linear systems are solved iteratively with a Krylov method (the conjugate-gradient family), for the reasons this chapter gave: a 3D grid is full of loops, so no tree trick applies, and Krylov methods converge much faster than Gauss-Seidel on such systems. The PB-MPM paper goes the other way, back to position-based constraint iterations of the kind in chapters 6 to 8, and accepts their convergence behaviour in exchange for simplicity and robustness. You now have the vocabulary to compare the two: both are approximately solving a system like $\mathbf{A}\Delta\boldsymbol\lambda = \mathbf{b}$; they differ in how much effort they spend per step and in what they do with the error they leave.

Summary

  • One linearised XPBD step is a linear system: the KKT form in $(\Delta\mathbf{x}, \Delta\boldsymbol\lambda)$, or the Schur form $(\mathbf{J}\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{J}^T + \tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha})\Delta\boldsymbol\lambda = -\mathbf{C} - \tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha}\boldsymbol\lambda$. For a chain the matrix is tridiagonal.
  • Gauss-Seidel and Jacobi are linear iterations $\mathbf{e}^{k+1} = \mathbf{G}\mathbf{e}^k$; their speed is the spectral radius. On a chain $\rho_{\text{GS}} = \rho_{\text{J}}^2 = \cos^2(\pi/2n)$, so they need about $1.87\,n^2$ sweeps for a hundredfold reduction, because information diffuses one link per sweep.
  • The matrix is SPD for independent constraints or any compliance; Gauss-Seidel then always converges, Jacobi needs diagonal dominance and in practice under-relaxation.
  • The Thomas algorithm solves a tridiagonal system exactly in $O(n)$. Eliminating leaves first extends it to any tree without fill-in; Baraff's insight is to do this on the KKT graph, which is a tree for any loop-free mechanism. A few loops cost a small dense Schur complement (bordering).
  • A direct solve removes solver error, not linearisation error: fast motion needs substeps, Newton iterations or geometric stiffness.
  • Large mass ratios make the system ill-conditioned: iterative solvers need more sweeps, direct solvers lose digits. Use double precision where you can and scale rows to comparable sizes.
