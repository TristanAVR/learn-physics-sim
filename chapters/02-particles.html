Particles and Newton

A dot, an arrow, and a rule for where they go next.

Almost everything on this site is made of particles. A rope is a row of them, a sheet of cloth is a grid of them, and a block of jelly or a heap of snow is a cloud of thousands. Later chapters will tie particles together with springs and constraints, or let them talk through a grid, but underneath, every one of those simulations repeats the same small loop: work out the forces on each particle, then move it a little.

This chapter builds that loop for one particle and then for many. You will throw a ball and compare it with the exact parabola from school physics, bounce it off the ground, spray a fountain of particles, and add air resistance until the falling speed stops growing. By the end you will be able to write a particle simulator from an empty file, predict what it should do before running it, and check that it does.

What a particle is

A particle is a point with a mass. It has no size, no shape and no orientation; it is somewhere, and it is moving. Two vectors capture that completely:

  • the position $\mathbf{x} = (x, y)$, where it is, in metres;
  • the velocity $\mathbf{v} = (v_x, v_y)$, how fast its position is changing, in metres per second.

Together they form the particle's state. If you know the state now and the forces acting on it, you can work out the state a moment later, and that is all a simulation does. The mass $m$, in kilograms, is a fixed property rather than part of the state. Bold letters are vectors; plain letters are numbers.

Throughout the site we use SI units: metres, kilograms and seconds, so forces come out in newtons ($1\ \text{N} = 1\ \text{kg m/s}^2$). The $y$ axis points up, so gravity pulls toward negative $y$. Sticking to real units costs nothing and pays off constantly: when a number in your simulation looks wrong, you can compare it with the world. A ball that takes ten seconds to fall one metre is a bug you can see without a debugger.

In code a particle is a plain object holding two vectors. The sandbox provides small helpers on {x, y} objects (V.v, V.add, V.scale, V.addScaled(a, b, s) for $a + s\,b$, V.len):

const mass = 0.5;                      // kg
const p = { x: V.v(0, 0), v: V.v(7.07, 7.07) };   // at the origin, moving up and to the right

Newton's second law

Isaac Newton's second law connects forces to motion. The net force $\mathbf{F}$ on a particle equals its mass times its acceleration $\mathbf{a}$, the rate at which its velocity changes:

$$\mathbf{F} = m\,\mathbf{a}, \qquad \mathbf{a} = \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}, \qquad \mathbf{v} = \frac{d\mathbf{x}}{dt}.$$

Read it as a chain. Forces set the acceleration ($\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{F}/m$); acceleration changes the velocity; velocity changes the position. A force does not move a particle directly. It bends its velocity, and the velocity moves it. That is why a thrown ball keeps rising for a while after gravity starts pulling it down.

The first force we need is gravity. Near the Earth's surface every object is pulled down by its weight,

$$\mathbf{F}_\text{gravity} = m\,\mathbf{g}, \qquad \mathbf{g} = (0, -9.81)\ \text{m/s}^2.$$

Divide by $m$ and the acceleration is $\mathbf{g}$ whatever the mass: in a vacuum, a feather and a hammer fall together. Mass will start to matter as soon as a second force joins in, because other forces do not scale with $m$.

The update loop

The equations above describe continuous change. A computer advances in jumps, by a fixed time step $h$, often 1/60 s or smaller. The simplest way to jump is to pretend that velocity and acceleration stay constant for the whole step:

$$\mathbf{x}_{n+1} = \mathbf{x}_n + h\,\mathbf{v}_n, \qquad \mathbf{v}_{n+1} = \mathbf{v}_n + h\,\mathbf{a}_n, \qquad \mathbf{a}_n = \frac{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x}_n, \mathbf{v}_n)}{m}.$$

The subscript $n$ counts steps, so $\mathbf{x}_n$ is the position at time $t_n = n h$. In code:

function integrate(p, h) {
  const a = V.scale(force(p), 1 / mass);   // Newton: a = F / m
  p.x = V.addScaled(p.x, p.v, h);          // position changes by velocity * time
  p.v = V.addScaled(p.v, a, h);            // velocity changes by acceleration * time
}

Call it once per step, forever, and you have a simulator. This scheme is called explicit Euler integration, and it is not the only choice: the order of those two lines, and which velocity you use to move, matters a great deal once springs appear. The next chapter, Time Integration, is entirely about that. For a ball in gravity it does well, and we can measure exactly how well, because for this problem we know the true answer.

A projectile and its exact path

Throw a particle from the origin with speed $v_0$ at angle $\theta$ above the horizontal. With gravity as the only force, the horizontal velocity never changes and the vertical velocity decreases steadily, so the exact motion is

$$x(t) = v_0 \cos\theta\; t, \qquad y(t) = v_0 \sin\theta\; t - \tfrac12 g\,t^2,$$

a parabola. Setting $y = 0$ gives the flight time and the landing distance (the range):

$$T = \frac{2 v_0 \sin\theta}{g}, \qquad R = v_0\cos\theta\; T = \frac{v_0^2 \sin 2\theta}{g}.$$

With the demo's defaults, $v_0 = 10$ m/s and $\theta = 45°$, that is $T = 1.4416$ s and $R = 10.194$ m, and the top of the arc is $v_0^2\sin^2\theta / (2g) = 2.548$ m high. The demo draws this parabola as a dashed curve and lets the simulated particle fly over it, leaving a trail of dots. When it lands, it reports where.

The simulated particle lands slightly beyond the exact point: about 1.4 cm at the default time step of 2 ms. Raise the time step to 16 ms and the gap grows to 12 cm; at 50 ms it is 35 cm. We can explain that exactly. Summing the update rule over $n$ steps gives the simulated height after $n$ steps:

$$y_n = v_0\sin\theta\; t_n - \tfrac12 g\,t_n^2 + \tfrac12 g\,h\,t_n .$$

The last term is the error. Because each step moves with the velocity from the start of the step, before gravity has slowed it, the particle always runs a little high, and it reaches the ground exactly one time step late. It therefore lands $v_0\cos\theta\; h$ too far: $7.07 \times 0.002 = 0.014$ m at 2 ms and $7.07 \times 0.05 = 0.35$ m at 50 ms, which is what the demo measures. The error shrinks in proportion to $h$; halve the step and you halve the error. Methods with this property are called first order.

Always keep an exact answer next to your simulation. A projectile, a falling body, a pendulum's period, a spring's frequency: whenever a closed-form solution exists, draw it or print it beside the simulated value. It turns "looks about right" into a number, and it is how every claim in this course is checked.

Bouncing off the ground

The ground is the plane $y = 0$. A point particle that ends a step below it has passed through, so we do two things: put it back on the surface, and turn its downward velocity into an upward one. Real balls do not bounce back with all their speed; some energy goes into sound, heat and wobble. The coefficient of restitution $e$ is the fraction of the impact speed that survives:

$$v_y^{\text{after}} = -e\; v_y^{\text{before}}, \qquad 0 \le e \le 1.$$ 
function collideGround(p) {
  if (p.x.y < 0) {
    p.x.y = 0;                                          // back onto the surface
    if (p.v.y < 0) p.v.y = -restitution.value * p.v.y;  // reflect, keeping the fraction e
  }
}

The check p.v.y < 0 matters: a particle that is below the ground but already moving up (because it bounced last step and has not yet climbed out) must not be reflected again, or it would be sent back down.

What does $e$ do to the energy? Just before an impact all of the ball's energy is kinetic, $\tfrac12 m v^2$. Just after, the speed is $e v$, so the kinetic energy is $e^2$ times as large. Each bounce keeps a fraction $e^2$ of the energy and loses $1 - e^2$. Since the ball then climbs until that kinetic energy has all become potential energy $m g y$, each peak is $e^2$ times the previous one. Drop a ball from 2 m with $e = 0.8$ and the peaks should be 1.28 m, 0.819 m, 0.524 m, each one 64 % of the last, with 36 % of the energy lost per bounce.

Choose "bouncing ball" in the demo. It marks each peak with a dashed line and prints the ratio of the last two. At the default 2 ms step the first peak is 1.291 m and the ratios come out at 0.646 to 0.657, within about 3 % of 0.64. Now set the time step to 16 ms: the ratios rise to 0.70 and above, and with $e = 1$ the ball bounces higher every time. That is the same "running a little high" error as before, which adds a sliver of energy every step. You will see it again, much more dramatically, in the next chapter.

A ball that never stops. With $e < 1$ the bounces get shorter and shorter: the times between them shrink geometrically by a factor $e$, so infinitely many bounces fit in finite time (about 5.75 s for a 2 m drop at $e = 0.8$). A simulation with a fixed step cannot resolve them all, and the ball ends up jittering on the floor. Real engines add a rule such as "below a small impact speed, set the velocity to zero". Contact gets a proper treatment in Contact and Friction.

Many particles

Nothing in the update refers to other particles, so a hundred particles are a hundred independent copies of the same loop:

for (const p of particles) {
  integrate(p, h);
  collideGround(p);
}

The "fountain" scene emits about a hundred particles a second from the origin, with slightly random speeds and directions, and keeps the newest 400. Each follows its own parabola and bounces on its own. It already looks like spray from a hose. Once particles start pushing on each other (springs in chapter 05, constraints in chapter 06, pressure in a fluid much later), the loop gets one more stage, "compute forces between particles", but its shape stays the same.

For a few hundred particles an array of objects is fine. For tens of thousands, as in the MPM chapters, you will store positions and velocities in flat typed arrays (xs[i], ys[i]) so the memory is contiguous and fast to loop over. The physics does not change.

Forces add up

When several forces act on a particle, Newton's law uses their vector sum:

$$\mathbf{F} = \sum_k \mathbf{F}_k = m\,\mathbf{g} + \mathbf{F}_\text{drag} + \dots$$

This is the main reason to write a separate force function: every new effect is one more term. The most useful second term is drag, the resistance of the air or liquid the particle moves through. It always points against the velocity, and two simple models cover most needs.

Linear drag is proportional to velocity:

$$\mathbf{F}_\text{drag} = -c\,\mathbf{v},$$

with a coefficient $c$ in N·s/m. It describes slow motion through thick fluids, such as a bead sinking in honey or a fine dust grain in air, and it is a common, cheap way to calm a simulation down.

Quadratic drag grows with the square of the speed:

$$\mathbf{F}_\text{drag} = -c_2\,|\mathbf{v}|\,\mathbf{v}, \qquad c_2 = \tfrac12\,\rho\,C_d\,A,$$

where $\rho$ is the fluid density (1.2 kg/m³ for air), $A$ the frontal area and $C_d$ a dimensionless drag coefficient (about 0.5 for a sphere). Written as $|\mathbf{v}|\,\mathbf{v}$, the force has magnitude $c_2 v^2$ and points against the motion. This is the right model for ordinary objects moving at ordinary speeds in air. For a tennis ball of radius 3.35 cm, $c_2 \approx 0.0011$ kg/m.

function force(p) {
  let f = V.v(0, -mass * gravity);                                  // weight
  if (dragModel.value === 'linear') {
    f = V.addScaled(f, p.v, -cLin.value);                           // -c v
  } else if (dragModel.value === 'quadratic') {
    f = V.addScaled(f, p.v, -cQuad.value * V.len(p.v));             // -c2 |v| v
  }
  return f;
}

Turn on linear drag in the projectile scene. The particle now falls short of the dashed parabola and comes down more steeply than it went up. The orange dashed curve is the exact solution with linear drag, which we can still write down: with $\tau = m / c$ and $v_t = m g / c$,

$$x(t) = v_{0x}\,\tau\,(1 - e^{-t/\tau}), \qquad y(t) = (v_{0y} + v_t)\,\tau\,(1 - e^{-t/\tau}) - v_t\,t.$$

The simulated trail lies on it: with $c = 0.5$ N·s/m the particle lands at 4.958 m, and the exact curve says 4.952 m. Quadratic drag couples the $x$ and $y$ motions (the drag on $v_x$ depends on $v_y$ through $|\mathbf{v}|$), and there is no closed form for the full flight. That is typical: the exact answers run out quickly, and the simulation keeps going.

Terminal velocity

Drop a particle from rest with drag switched on. At first it is slow, drag is negligible and it accelerates at $g$. As it speeds up, drag grows, until drag balances weight. At that point the net force is zero, the acceleration is zero, and the speed stops changing. That speed is the terminal velocity $v_t$. Set the forces equal and solve:

$$\text{linear: } c\,v_t = m g \;\Rightarrow\; v_t = \frac{m g}{c}, \qquad \text{quadratic: } c_2\,v_t^2 = m g \;\Rightarrow\; v_t = \sqrt{\frac{m g}{c_2}}.$$

Here mass matters: a heavier object with the same drag coefficient falls faster, because its weight needs more drag to balance. The tennis ball (57 g) reaches about 23 m/s.

We can also say how quickly the speed gets there. For linear drag the speed approaches $v_t$ exponentially,

$$v(t) = v_t\,\bigl(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\bigr), \qquad \tau = \frac{m}{c},$$

so after three time constants it is at 95 % of $v_t$. For quadratic drag, $v(t) = v_t \tanh(g t / v_t)$. The "terminal velocity" scene drops a 0.5 kg particle with the camera following it. With linear drag and $c = 0.5$ N·s/m, $v_t = 9.81$ m/s and $\tau = 1$ s; the simulation reads 9.32 m/s after 3 s (the formula says 9.322) and then settles on 9.8100. With quadratic drag and $c_2 = 0.05$ kg/m, $v_t = \sqrt{98.1} = 9.905$ m/s.

The simulated terminal speed matches the formula to every printed digit, even at a large time step. That is not a coincidence: at terminal velocity the acceleration is zero, so the update $\mathbf{v}_{n+1} = \mathbf{v}_n + h\,\mathbf{a}_n$ leaves the velocity alone. The steady state of the update is the steady state of the physics. Only the approach to it carries a time-step error.

Drag can blow up too. With linear drag alone, one step multiplies the velocity by $(1 - h c / m)$. If $h c / m$ exceeds 1 the velocity flips sign every step, and beyond 2 it grows without limit: the drag "overshoots". With $m = 0.5$ kg and $h = 0.05$ s that needs $c > 20$ N·s/m, far above the demo's slider, but very light particles with strong drag hit this limit easily. It is your first glimpse of a stability limit, the main topic of the next chapter.

A word about the time step

Every result above depends on $h$. Keep it fixed. If you pass the measured frame time into the update, a single slow frame becomes a giant step, and the error (or worse, an instability) depends on what else your computer was doing. The standard pattern is to run as many fixed steps as fit into the elapsed time. The sandbox lets you set $h$ and the number of steps per drawn frame, and the demo picks enough steps of the chosen size to keep pace with real time. Why fixed steps matter so much, and how to choose $h$, is the subject of Time Integration.

particles in the MPM papers

The MPM course notes and the APIC paper describe a material as a set of particles, each carrying a mass $m_p$, a position $\mathbf{x}_p$ and a velocity $\mathbf{v}_p$: exactly the state of this chapter, with the subscript $p$ for "particle". What they add, in their sections on transferring to and from the grid, is a way for particles to feel each other's forces through a background grid. Gravity still enters as $m\mathbf{g}$, added to the grid momentum each step, and the particle positions still advance as $\mathbf{x}_p \leftarrow \mathbf{x}_p + h\,\mathbf{v}_p$.

Summary

  • A particle's state is its position and velocity. Mass is a fixed property. Use SI units with $y$ up and $\mathbf{g} = (0, -9.81)$ m/s².
  • Newton's second law, $\mathbf{F} = m\mathbf{a}$, turns forces into acceleration; acceleration changes velocity, and velocity changes position.
  • The update loop advances both by a fixed time step $h$. The simplest version is first order: its error shrinks in proportion to $h$, and for a projectile it lands exactly one step late.
  • Ground contact: project back onto the surface and reflect the normal velocity with restitution $e$; each bounce keeps $e^2$ of the energy.
  • Forces add as vectors. Linear drag $-c\mathbf{v}$ and quadratic drag $-c_2|\mathbf{v}|\mathbf{v}$ give terminal velocities $mg/c$ and $\sqrt{mg/c_2}$.
  • Compare with a closed form whenever one exists.
