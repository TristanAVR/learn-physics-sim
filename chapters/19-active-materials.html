Muscles and Travelling Waves

Materials that push back on their own, and the one-line change that turns a constraint into a muscle.

Everything simulated so far has been passive. A spring stretched by a load pulls back; a balloon inflated by a pump pushes out; leave them alone and they settle. Some materials do not wait to be loaded. A muscle contracts because it is told to. An earthworm crawls by passing a wave of contraction along its body. A peristaltic pump, the kind that moves slurry or paint without letting it touch a valve, rolls a squeeze along a hose. Squeezing a toothpaste tube from the closed end is the same idea done by hand, and artificial muscles (pneumatic braided muscles, shape-memory wires, electroactive polymers) bring it to robots.

What these have in common is a source of force inside the material, switched by a control signal. In a simulator that turns out to be a small change: the constraint you already know, with its rest length or its force driven by an activation $a(t)$ between 0 (relaxed) and 1 (fully on). The interesting part is what follows from it: how a wave of activation moves things, how a body crawls with nothing but internal forces and friction, and how a stiff actuator can inject energy into a light node if you let it snap.

By the end of this chapter you will have written a muscle constraint, driven it with a travelling wave, predicted the transport speed of a peristaltic tube and the crawling speed of a worm from one-line formulas, and seen why real actuators need an activation time constant.

Two ways to make a material active

Take a single muscle fibre between particles $a$ and $b$, with rest length $L_0$ and current length $\ell = |x_b - x_a|$. There are two classic ways to make it contract.

Active strain. Change the shape the material wants to have. The activation shortens the rest length,

$$L(a) = L_0\,(1 - \varepsilon_\text{max}\, a), \qquad C = \ell - L(a),$$

where $\varepsilon_\text{max}$ is the largest strain the muscle can produce (0.3 means it can shorten by 30%). The constraint machinery then pulls the fibre to its new rest length, exactly as it would pull a stretched spring back to an unchanged one.

Active stress. Leave the rest length alone and add a contractile force along the fibre:

$$F = a\, F_\text{max}\, f_L(\ell),$$

where $F_\text{max}$ is the strongest pull and $f_L$ is a force-length factor between 0 and 1. Real muscle fibres pull hardest near their rest length and lose force as they shorten, because the molecular motors run out of overlap. We use the simplest version: $f_L$ falls linearly from 1 at $\ell = L_0$ to 0 at the shortest length $L_0(1 - \varepsilon_\text{max})$.

For a linear spring of stiffness $k$ the two are the same thing seen from two sides: shifting the rest length by $\delta$ adds a force $k\delta$. They differ in what you control. With a stiff constraint, active strain is a position servo: it reaches its target length whatever the load, as long as it can. Active stress is a force source: it pulls with a given force, and how far it moves depends on what it pulls against. Biological muscle is closer to the second (biomechanics models it with Hill-type force-length and force-velocity curves); simulation codes often prefer the first, because it fits the constraint solver and stays stable at any stiffness.

The muscle constraint

Active strain is one XPBD distance constraint with two changes. First, a muscle can pull but not push. When the fibre is shorter than its rest length it goes slack, so the constraint is an inequality, active only while $C > 0$, and its multiplier must keep the sign of tension. Second, a muscle has a strongest pull, $F_\text{max}$. In XPBD the force over a step is $F = |\lambda| / h^2$ (from the XPBD chapter), so capping the force is clamping the accumulated multiplier.

function solveMuscle(m) {
  const dx = P.x[m.b] - P.x[m.a], dy = P.y[m.b] - P.y[m.a], len = Math.hypot(dx, dy);
  const nx = dx / len, ny = dy / len;
  const rest = m.L0 * (1 - m.strainMax * m.act);            // active strain
  const C = len - rest;
  if (m.tensionOnly && C <= 0) return;                   // slack: a muscle cannot push
  const wa = P.w[m.a], wb = P.w[m.b], at = m.alpha / (h * h);
  const dl = (-C - at * m.lam) / (wa + wb + at);
  const lamMax = m.cap * h * h;                              // force cap: |lambda| / h^2 <= cap
  const lam = Math.max(-lamMax, Math.min(m.tensionOnly ? 0 : lamMax, m.lam + dl));
  const d = lam - m.lam;
  m.lam = lam;
  P.x[m.a] -= wa * nx * d; P.y[m.a] -= wa * ny * d;
  P.x[m.b] += wb * nx * d; P.y[m.b] += wb * ny * d;
}

With the gradient $\nabla_b C = n$ (the unit vector from $a$ to $b$), a stretched fibre ($C > 0$) gives a negative multiplier, which moves the two ends towards each other. So tension means $\lambda < 0$, and a tension-only muscle clamps $\lambda$ to $[-F_\text{max}h^2, 0]$. The compliance $\alpha$ is the passive stretchiness of the fibre and its tendon. The demo runs 20 substeps of $h = 1/1200$ s per frame with one pass each, as in the small steps chapter, so the multiplier restarts at zero every substep.

The demo below has three scenes. Start with muscle under load: one muscle ($L_0 = 30$ cm, $\varepsilon_\text{max} = 0.3$) hangs from a beam with a weight, switched on for two seconds and off for two.

Under load: servo or force source

With active strain, the muscle shortens by $\varepsilon_\text{max} L_0 = 9$ cm minus the small passive stretch $m g \alpha$ of its compliance ($\alpha = 10^{-4}$ m/N): 8.95 cm with a 0.5 kg weight, 8.90 cm with 1 kg, 8.85 cm with 1.5 kg. The load barely matters. That is the servo behaviour: the constraint produces whatever force it takes.

Until it can't. With the default cap of 20 N, a 2 kg weight (19.6 N) is lifted only slowly, because there is almost no spare force to accelerate it, and a 3 kg weight (29.4 N) cannot be held at all: the multiplier sits at its clamp, the muscle lengthens under the load, and the weight sinks to the floor. A capped active-strain muscle is a position servo with a force limit, which is a good model of most real actuators.

Switch the law to active stress. At full activation the weight hangs where the active force balances it, $F_\text{max} f_L(\ell) = m g$, so the shortening is

$$\Delta \ell = \varepsilon_\text{max} L_0 \left(1 - \frac{m g}{a F_\text{max}}\right).$$

With $F_\text{max} = 20$ N that predicts 8.56 cm for 0.1 kg, 6.79 cm for 0.5 kg, 4.59 cm for 1 kg and 2.38 cm for 1.5 kg, and the demo measures the same to the hundredth of a centimetre. Now the load matters a lot: heavier weights are lifted less, and at $m g = F_\text{max}$ not at all. That is how your arm feels.

Snapping: actuators that inject energy

Set the load to its lightest (10 g), the force cap to 1000 N and the activation time constant to 0. When the command switches on, the rest length jumps by 9 cm in a single substep. A stiff constraint tries to close that gap at once, and closing 9 cm in $h = 1/1200$ s means a velocity of $0.09 \times 1200 = 108$ m/s. The force cap limits it (the most the constraint can move a 10 g particle in one substep is $w F_\text{max} h^2 = 6.9$ cm), but the readout still shows a peak of about 44 m/s. That kinetic energy came from nowhere physical: it is the energy of a spring whose rest length was teleported.

Nothing in the constraint is wrong. The command is. A real muscle cannot switch on instantly; its activation rises over tens of milliseconds as calcium floods the fibres. Model that with first-order activation dynamics:

$$\frac{da}{dt} = \frac{u - a}{\tau}, \qquad a \leftarrow a + (u - a)\big(1 - e^{-h/\tau}\big)\ \text{each substep},$$

where $u$ is the command and $\tau$ the time constant. With $\tau = 50$ ms the same light weight peaks at about 1.9 m/s. This is an honest speed limit: it limits how fast the actuator can change, which is a property of the actuator, rather than clamping the velocity of whatever it happens to move.

Active stress on a light node is an explicit stiff force. The force-length factor makes the active force behave like a spring of stiffness $F_\text{max}/(\varepsilon_\text{max}L_0)$, here $1000/0.09 \approx 11{,}000$ N/m. On a 10 g node that is $\omega \approx 1050$ rad/s and $h\omega \approx 0.88$ per substep: still under the symplectic Euler limit of 2 from the time integration chapter, but only just, and the weight still flies at 24 m/s with $\tau = 50$ ms. Give actuated parts some mass, some damping and a ramped command, and check $h\omega$ for explicit actuator forces as you would for any stiff spring.

Travelling waves

One muscle lifts a weight. A row of muscles, switched on one after another, moves things along. The control signal becomes a function of position $s$ along the body and of time:

$$a(s, t) = f(s - c\,t).$$

Any function of $s - ct$ is a shape that slides along $s$ at speed $c$ without changing: the value at position $s$ at time $t$ is the value that was at $s - c\,\Delta t$ a time $\Delta t$ earlier. If $f$ repeats every wavelength $\lambda$, each point contracts once per period $\lambda / c$. The demo's $f$ is one smooth bump per wavelength,

$$f(\phi) = \left(\tfrac12\big(1 - \cos 2\pi\phi\big)\right)^3, \qquad \phi = \frac{s - ct}{\lambda} \bmod 1,$$

cubed to make the contraction narrower than the gap between contractions. Its average over a wavelength is $5/16$, a number that will matter for the worm.

function waveActivation(s, t) {
  let phase = ((s - waveSpeed * t) / wavelength) % 1;
  if (phase < 0) phase += 1;                  // % keeps the sign of its left operand
  const bump = 0.5 * (1 - Math.cos(2 * Math.PI * phase));
  return bump * bump * bump;
}

Peristalsis

Switch the demo to the peristaltic tube: two walls of 41 particles each, every wall node held near its place by a soft 50 N/m tether (the elasticity of the tube), and a tension-only ring muscle across the tube at each node pair, with a rest length equal to the diameter (20 cm), $\varepsilon_\text{max} = 0.8$, and a 10 N force cap. The muscle at position $s$ follows $a(s, t)$, so a narrow waist travels along the tube at $c$ and pushes the ball ahead of it.

In a real tube, the ring muscle is a hoop around the wall. Cut the tube lengthwise along a diameter and look at one half, of length $\Delta z$: the pressure $P$ inside pushes it outwards with a force $P \cdot 2r\,\Delta z$, and the hoop tension $T$ (force per unit length of tube) pulls it back at both cut edges, $2T\,\Delta z$. Balance gives Laplace's law for a cylinder:

$$P = \frac{T}{r}.$$

A hose of radius 5 mm squeezed by a hoop tension of 10 N/m holds a pressure of 2000 Pa; the same tension at radius 2.5 mm holds 4000 Pa. A contracting ring gets more effective as it closes, which is why a squeeze, once started, completes easily. (A balloon, the doubly curved case from the pressure chapter, has $P = 2T/r$.) In the 2D demo each ring is a single strut across the tube, standing in for the two halves of the hoop.

How fast do the contents move?

If the contraction closes the tube completely behind the ball, the ball cannot slip back through it, and the contraction moves at $c$. So the ball moves at $c$: an occlusive wave carries its contents at the wave speed, whatever the wavelength. Industrial peristaltic pumps rely on exactly this, with rollers pinching a hose shut, so they deliver a fixed volume per roller pass.

Occlusion is geometry. The ball (radius 6 cm) fits between the walls while the ring's length, measured between wall node centres, exceeds twice the sum of the ball radius and the wall node radius (1.5 cm). The ring's shortest length is $2R(1 - \varepsilon)$ with $R = 10$ cm, so the wave can grip the ball when

$$2R(1 - \varepsilon) < 2(r_\text{ball} + r_\text{wall}) \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \varepsilon > 1 - \frac{0.06 + 0.015}{0.1} = 0.25.$$

The demo measures the ball's speed over the last three seconds and divides it by $c$. With strength 0.8, measured over 15 seconds, the ratio is 1.00 within a few percent for wave speeds from 0.1 to 1 m/s and wavelengths from 0.3 to 1.2 m (the three-second readout wobbles more, because the ball's place on the slope of the contraction shifts a little). The threshold is sharp: at strength 0.25 and below the wave slides over the ball and the ratio is zero; from 0.27 up it is 1. A bed of 24 grains (radius 1.8 cm) needs a much tighter squeeze, $\varepsilon > 1 - (0.018 + 0.015)/0.1 = 0.67$: at strength 0.4 the grains stay put, at 0.8 they move at $c$, though now and then a grain is squeezed back through a contraction, the way fluid leaks past a pump roller that does not quite close the hose.

Crawling

A worm has no wheels and nothing to push against but the ground, and every force its muscles make is internal: they sum to zero. So how does it go anywhere? Through friction that depends on direction. An earthworm's segments carry tiny bristles (setae) that slide easily forwards and dig in backwards. With such a ratchet, any change of length moves the body forward: a segment that shortens pulls its rear part forward (the front part cannot slide back), and a segment that lengthens pushes its front part forward (the rear cannot slide back).

Each earthworm segment is a sealed bag of fluid with two muscle layers: circular muscles that squeeze it thin and long, and longitudinal muscles that pull it short and fat. The fluid's volume stays constant, so the two layers work against each other through it (a hydrostatic skeleton). In the demo's crawling worm each of ten 5 cm segments is one two-sided muscle whose length follows the activation, with $\varepsilon_\text{max} = 0.5 \times$ strength, and is drawn with constant area, so short segments look fat. The wave runs from head to tail, as in real earthworms.

The ground friction is the Coulomb friction from the contact chapter, with a coefficient that depends on which way the node is sliding:

function groundFriction(i, dn) {                // dn: how far the ground pushed node i up this substep
  const slide = P.x[i] - P.px[i];               // +x is forward, towards the head
  const mu = slide > 0 ? muForward : muBackward;
  const maxCancel = mu * dn;                    // Coulomb: at most mu times the normal correction
  if (Math.abs(slide) <= maxCancel) P.x[i] = P.px[i];
  else P.x[i] -= Math.sign(slide) * maxCancel;
}

Predicting the crawling speed

Here is a calculation you can do on paper, and it predicts the demo well. Measure position along the body from the head with a material coordinate $s$. A segment's strain is $-\varepsilon\, a(s - ct)$. Add up the length changes from the head to $s$ and differentiate in time, and the velocity of the body at $s$ comes out as

$$v(s) = V_0 - \varepsilon\, c\; a(s - ct),$$

where $V_0$ is the same for the whole body. Parts with low activation move forwards faster than parts with high activation. Which $V_0$? The worm moves slowly, so inertia is negligible and the friction forces must cancel. With equal weight per unit length, the forward-sliding fraction of the body times $\mu_f$ must equal the backward-sliding fraction times $\mu_b$. The body slides forward where $a < q = V_0/(\varepsilon c)$, so if $F(q)$ is the fraction of a wavelength where $a < q$,

$$\mu_f\, F(q) = \mu_b\,\big(1 - F(q)\big) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F(q) = \frac{\mu_b}{\mu_b + \mu_f}, \qquad \bar v = \varepsilon\,c\,\big(q - \langle a \rangle\big),$$

where $\langle a \rangle = 5/16$ is the mean activation. For the bump shape, $F(q) = \tfrac1\pi \arccos(1 - 2q^{1/3})$, which inverts to $q = \big(\tfrac12(1 - \cos \pi F)\big)^3$.

  • Perfect ratchet ($\mu_f \to 0$): $F \to 1$, $q \to 1$ and $\bar v = \tfrac{11}{16}\,\varepsilon c$. The fully contracted parts are the anchors, exactly what earthworms do: they plant their short, fat segments and move the long, thin ones. With $\varepsilon = 0.4$ and $c = 0.2$ m/s, that is 5.5 cm/s.
  • The demo's setae ($\mu_b = 1$, $\mu_f = 0.1$): $F = 0.909$, $q = 0.940$ and $\bar v = 0.628\,\varepsilon c = 5.02$ cm/s. The demo measures 5.09 cm/s.
  • Isotropic friction ($\mu_b = \mu_f$): $F = 1/2$, so $q$ is the median activation, $(1/2)^3 = 0.125$, below the mean, and $\bar v = -0.1875\,\varepsilon c = -1.5$ cm/s. The worm crawls backwards. The demo measures exactly $-1.50$ cm/s. The direction of travel is set by the friction, not by the wave.

These numbers use a wavelength of 0.5 m, so the body holds exactly one wave and $F$ really is the fraction of the body; with other wavelengths the fraction fluctuates and the agreement is within about 10%. With zero forward friction the worm coasts on its own momentum and goes faster than the prediction, because the quasi-static assumption fails. An inchworm (and the inchworm robots built from it) is the same calculation with only two feet: anchor the rear, extend, anchor the front, contract.

Hint: the sign of the clamp

A stretched fibre has $C > 0$ and produces $\Delta\lambda < 0$. Tension is negative $\lambda$, so a tension-only muscle keeps $\lambda$ in $[-F_\text{max}h^2, 0]$; a two-sided one (the worm's segments) in $[-F_\text{max}h^2, F_\text{max}h^2]$. Solution 
function activeStressForce(m) {
  const len = Math.hypot(P.x[m.b] - P.x[m.a], P.y[m.b] - P.y[m.a]);
  const shortest = m.L0 * (1 - m.strainMax);
  const fl = Math.max(0, Math.min(1, (len - shortest) / (m.L0 - shortest)));
  return m.act * m.cap * fl;
}

The shortenings are in the table above. With the snap, active strain peaks near 44 m/s and active stress near 90 m/s. A time constant of 50 ms brings active strain down to about 2 m/s because its rest length then moves slowly, but active stress still peaks at 24 m/s: its force reaches 1000 N within a few time constants whatever the node does, and 1000 N on 10 g is an acceleration of $10^5$ m/s².

Summary

  • An active material makes force on command. Drive a constraint with an activation $a \in [0, 1]$: active strain shortens its rest length, $L = L_0(1 - \varepsilon_\text{max} a)$; active stress adds a contractile force $a F_\text{max} f_L(\ell)$.
  • A muscle constraint is a tension-only XPBD distance constraint ($\lambda \le 0$) with a force cap ($|\lambda| \le F_\text{max}h^2$). Capped active strain is a position servo with a force limit; active stress is a force source.
  • Snapping an activation injects energy. Ramp it with activation dynamics ($\tau$ of tens of ms), a speed limit on the actuator rather than on the bodies.
  • A travelling wave $a(s, t) = f(s - ct)$ moves contents at the wave speed when it is occlusive. A ring's squeeze is governed by Laplace's law, $P = T/r$.
  • Crawling needs direction-dependent friction. The quasi-static speed is $\varepsilon c(q - \langle a\rangle)$, with $q$ fixed by $\mu_f F(q) = \mu_b(1 - F(q))$; isotropic friction can send a worm backwards.
