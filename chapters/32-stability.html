Hunting Explosions

A field guide to the ways a simulation blows up, and the tools that find which one it was.

Every simulator explodes. Usually at the worst moment: in front of someone, after an hour of running fine, only on one machine, only when the user grabs that one object. The scene flickers, a particle shoots off at a thousand metres per second, and a frame later every number on screen reads NaN. By then the evidence is gone. What you see is the last frame of a chain of events that started somewhere small, many frames earlier.

This chapter is about working backwards along that chain. It has two halves. The first is a catalogue of causes, each one something you have met in earlier chapters, now seen from the point of view of the person debugging it. The second is a toolkit: monitors that stop the simulation at the first bad frame and point at the first bad particle, a recorder that replays the failure exactly, and a fuzzer that finds failures you did not think to look for.

By the end you will have written the watchdog and the fuzzer for a deliberately fragile scene, diagnosed three planted explosions without being told their cause, and you will know when a velocity clamp is an honest design decision and when it is hiding a bug.

What an explosion is

An explosion is energy appearing from nowhere. A closed system with no motors and no hand dragging it can only keep its energy or lose it (to friction, damping, inelastic collisions and the numerical damping of the integrator). Whenever the total energy of such a system rises, the numbers, not the physics, are doing it.

It helps to recognise three signatures, because each points to a different family of causes.

  • Growth. The energy, or some error, is multiplied by a constant factor greater than 1 every step. On a log plot it is a straight line going up. This is an instability: some mode of the system, a pattern of motion, has a step matrix with an eigenvalue of magnitude above 1, exactly the analysis of the time integration chapter. It can start invisibly small and take seconds to show.
  • A jump. The energy rises a lot in a single frame, and then the system may calm down. Something injected energy once: a deep penetration resolved in one step, two bodies created overlapping, an actuator that snapped.
  • NaN. A number became "not a number", usually from $0/0$, $\infty - \infty$ or the square root of a negative. NaN is contagious: any arithmetic with it gives NaN, so within a frame or two every particle connected to the first one is NaN too.

The usual suspects

The demo for this chapter is a deliberately fragile scene, small enough to understand completely: a chain of ten 50 g beads pinned at one end with a 0.5 kg weight at the other (XPBD distance constraints, ten Gauss-Seidel iterations per substep), a box of four 0.2 kg corners joined by six explicit springs of 2000 N/m (forces integrated with symplectic Euler, the way many games add springs to a position-based engine), and three 0.3 kg balls. It runs four substeps per 1/60 s frame, so $h = 1/240$ s. Each cause below can be planted with the plant cause button.

1. The time step against the fastest mode

An explicit spring is stable only while $h\omega \le 2$, where $\omega$ is the fastest angular frequency in the system. For the box (four equal masses $m$ with sides and diagonals of stiffness $k$) the fastest mode has $\omega_\text{max} = \sqrt{4k/m}$, which is $200$ rad/s for the defaults, so $h\omega = 0.83$. Comfortable. The limit $h\omega = 2$ is reached at

$$k_\text{crit} = \frac{m}{h^2} = 0.2 \times 240^2 = 11{,}520\ \text{N/m}.$$

Stiff box springs multiplies the stiffness by ten, to 20,000 N/m and $h\omega = 2.64$. The step matrix of symplectic Euler then has an eigenvalue of $-4.73$: the error flips sign and grows almost fivefold every substep, about 500-fold every frame. The box disintegrates within the frame it was planted in.

2. Mass ratios

The fastest mode is set by the stiffest spring on the lightest particle. Tiny box mass makes one corner a thousand times lighter, and with the stiffness unchanged the estimate becomes $h\omega \approx 26$. Mass ratios cause explosions in explicit force codes for exactly the same reason as stiffness: both live inside $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$.

In position-based solvers a mass ratio does something quieter. Heavy weight makes the chain's weight 1000 times heavier than before, 10,000 times a bead. XPBD stays stable at any stiffness, but Gauss-Seidel converges slowly when a light bead sits between heavy neighbours (the substepping chapter), so with ten iterations the chain stretches: its links grow by more than 2 cm within a quarter of a second. No energy is created; the failure is a constraint error. Not every bug is an explosion, which is why the watchdog below checks constraint errors too.

3. Stiffness ratios

The same story with springs in place of masses: a stiff constraint next to a soft one makes the stiff one the fastest mode in an explicit code, and slows Gauss-Seidel convergence in an implicit one. And when two solvers are coupled, the stiffness ratio decides whether the coupling iteration converges at all (the coupling chapter).

4. Conflicting and redundant constraints

Constraints that cannot all hold at once, a rope pinned at two points farther apart than its length, or two constraints on the same pair asking for different distances, fight forever. Gauss-Seidel settles on whichever it solved last, the others stay violated, and positions jump between iterations and between frames. Because velocity is computed from position changes, that fighting turns into motion: energy from nowhere.

Redundant constraints (more than needed, but consistent) are harmless for Gauss-Seidel and dangerous for Jacobi. Jacobi computes every correction from the same old positions and adds them up. Take a chain with a constraint between each pair of neighbours and also between next-neighbours, and an error pattern $u_i = \cos(i\theta)$ along it. Each iteration multiplies that pattern by $1 - \tfrac12\big(4 - 2\cos\theta - 2\cos 2\theta\big)$, and at $\cos\theta = -1/4$ that is $-2.125$. Ten iterations per substep multiply it by about 1900. Jacobi with redundant links plants exactly this. The fix is the one from the solvers chapter: divide each particle's summed correction by its number of constraints, or under-relax.

5. Bad initial states

A position-based solver resolves an overlap by moving the bodies apart, and then computes velocity as distance over time. Two balls created 15 cm deep inside each other are pushed apart in one substep, which means a velocity of the overlap divided by $h$: tens of metres per second, from nothing. Overlapping balls does this. The fix belongs to whoever creates the state: spawn objects apart, or ramp the separation in over several frames.

6. Collisions injecting energy

The same mechanism mid-simulation. A ball that ends a step 30 cm below the ground (it was fast, or something teleported it) is pushed out by its penetration $d = 0.38$ m in one substep, and leaves with

$$v = \frac{d}{h} = 0.38 \times 240 = 91\ \text{m/s}, \qquad \tfrac12 m v^2 = \tfrac12 \times 0.3 \times 91.2^2 \approx 1248\ \text{J}.$$

Ball deep in the ground plants it, and the watchdog reports an energy jump of 1249 J: the closed form plus the ball's small change in potential energy. Fast objects need continuous collision detection (the collision chapter); deep overlaps need a separation speed limit, which is one of the honest clamps discussed at the end.

7. Division by small numbers

Every normalisation divides by a length, every position-based update divides by a sum of inverse masses, every pressure divides by a volume. When the divisor reaches zero you get infinity or NaN; when it gets small you get a huge but finite number, which is often worse because nothing flags it. Coincident box corners puts one corner exactly on another. The spring between them computes its direction as $(x_b - x_a)/|x_b - x_a| = 0/0$, which is NaN.

A safe normalise is a silent one. A helper that returns a zero vector for a zero-length input (the sandbox's V.norm does) stops the NaN, and the spring then exerts no force at all. That may be right, or it may let two corners stay merged forever. Guards are fine, but decide what the physics should do in that case, and count how often the guard fires.

8. NaN propagation

NaN has two properties that make it hard to trace. It spreads: one NaN position enters every constraint that touches it, and those constraints write NaN into every neighbour, so after a few iterations the whole connected body is NaN. And every comparison with NaN is false, so if (d < minDist) silently skips a collision, Math.max(NaN, 1) is NaN, and a loop that searches for the largest error with if (err > best) never picks the NaN one. By the time you notice, the particle where it started looks like all the others. The only reliable method is to check early and often, and record where the first one appeared.

The demo

Plant a cause and the watchdog stops the run at the first bad frame, circles the particle it blames and prints what it saw. The info box also shows three diagnostics: the box's estimated $h\omega$, the chain's mass ratio and its constraint count. Every frame's input (mouse position and button, and any planted causes) is recorded; replay recording resets and plays it back, and replay to the frame before the trip stops one frame early so you can press Step (or the . key) and watch the failure happen. Drag any body with the mouse; the watchdog ignores energy changes while you drag, because your hand is doing work.

Tool 1: monitors that stop at the first bad frame

A monitor is a function that looks at the state after every frame and decides whether it is plausible. Three cheap checks catch nearly everything in this chapter.

Non-finite numbers. After each phase of each substep (forces, integration, constraints), scan the particles for a position, velocity or force that is not finite. Record the first one with its frame, substep and phase, then stop recording: only the first matters. In the demo, coincident corners planted at frame 120 are reported as "NaN at frame 120, particle 11 (box), substep 0, after spring forces", which is precisely where the $0/0$ happened.

function findNonFinite(S, fx, fy) {
  for (let i = 0; i < S.n; i++) {
    if (!Number.isFinite(S.x[i]) || !Number.isFinite(S.y[i]) ||
        !Number.isFinite(S.vx[i]) || !Number.isFinite(S.vy[i])) return i;
    if (fx && (!Number.isFinite(fx[i]) || !Number.isFinite(fy[i]))) return i;
  }
  return -1;
}

Energy. Measure the total energy (kinetic, gravitational, elastic) after this frame's input has been applied, and again at the end of the frame. If it rose by more than a tolerance while nothing external did work, trip. The tolerance must be bigger than the honest wobble of your integrator: here 1 J plus 1% of the total, against a scene energy that starts at 24 J and settles near 14 J. And blame the particle whose speed rose the most, not the one whose energy rose the most: a light particle can be very fast while carrying little energy.

const tol = 1 + 0.01 * Math.abs(before.E);
if (!external && after.E - before.E > tol) {
  let worst = 0, gain = -Infinity;
  for (let i = 0; i < S.n; i++) {
    if (after.speed[i] - before.speed[i] > gain) { gain = after.speed[i] - before.speed[i]; worst = i; }
  }
  return { kind: 'energy jump', frame: S.frame - 1, particle: worst, detail: ... };
}

Constraint error. The largest violation of the constraints that should hold, here the chain's link lengths, against a tolerance (2 cm, a quarter of a link). This catches failures that create no energy, like the heavy weight.

Momentum and angular momentum are worth monitoring too, in scenes where nothing external acts: internal forces cannot change them, so any drift is a bug (typically a constraint that does not push equally on both ends). The demo's scene is pinned and sits on the ground, so it has no conserved momentum to check.

Monitors find the frame, not always the cause. A frame here contains four substeps. A fast instability has already spread through a whole body by the end of its first frame, so the blamed particle may be a neighbour of the real culprit. Plant tiny box mass: the light corner is particle 13, and the watchdog blames corner 12, which the light corner flung away. The report narrows the search to a body and a frame; the diagnostics, the replay and the drawing do the rest.

Tool 2: record, replay, reduce

A failure you cannot reproduce is a failure you cannot fix. The cure is to make the simulation a pure function of its inputs: a fixed time step (never the measured frame time), seeded random numbers, the same order of operations on every run, and no wall-clock time anywhere in the physics. Then record the inputs, one entry per frame:

input = { x: Sim.mouse.x, y: Sim.mouse.y, down: Sim.mouse.down, events: pending };
recording.push(input);
for (const e of input.events) applyEvent(world, e);   // planted causes are inputs too
stepScene(world, input);

To replay, reset the scene and feed it the recorded inputs instead of the live mouse. The demo stores a hash of the positions when the watchdog trips, and when the replay trips at the same frame it compares hashes: "replay reproduces it: yes, same state hash" means the replay is bit-for-bit the same run. Now you can stop one frame early and look.

Deterministic replay holds on one machine with one build. Different compilers, CPUs and math libraries may round differently, and a chaotic simulation amplifies the difference, which is why "it only explodes on that machine" is often a numerical sensitivity, not a different bug.

With a reproduction in hand, make it minimal. Remove bodies, constraints and features one at a time and replay; whatever you removed last before the failure disappeared is involved. A two-particle reproduction of a thousand-particle explosion usually explains itself.

Tool 3: draw what the solver sees

Most explosions are obvious once you can see the right quantity. Draw velocities as arrows (a particle about to explode often shakes visibly frames before), colour constraints by their error (the demo colours chain links orange above 5 mm and red above 2 cm), colour particles by mass or by the number of constraints touching them, and draw contact normals and penetration depths. Two minutes of drawing beats an hour of printing numbers.

Tool 4: randomised stress testing

You only test the inputs you think of. A fuzzer tests the ones you don't: it runs the simulation headless (no drawing, as fast as possible) many times, each with random settings and a random input program, applies the watchdog, and counts failures by kind. The demo's fuzzer picks the substep count from 1, 2, 4 and 8, the iteration count from 2 to 20, a box stiffness between 316 and 100,000 N/m, a weight between 1 and 1000 bead masses, and one random mouse drag per run, and runs each for 300 frames. 200 runs take about a second and a half.

The table it prints shows energy and NaN failures by substep count and stiffness. Read it against the prediction $k_\text{crit} = m/h^2 = 0.2\,(60\,n)^2$ for $n$ substeps: 720 N/m for one substep, 2880 for two, 11,520 for four, 46,080 for eight. With the demo's seed, every cell wholly above the boundary fails in every run, every cell wholly below it passes, and the cells the boundary cuts through fail partly. The fuzzer has measured the stability limit of the box. It also finds a second family of failures (stretch, from heavy weights with few iterations) that the stiffness analysis says nothing about.

Look closely at the edges, too. One run at 2840 N/m with two substeps fails although it sits just below the 2880 N/m boundary ($h\omega = 1.986$). The limit $h\omega = 2$ comes from a linear analysis of a free box; with ground contact, large rotations and a 10 kg weight swinging nearby, the real limit is a little lower. Stability limits are not knife edges, which is why a design rule should keep well inside them. And treat every surprise in a fuzz table as a question: it may be a real failure near a limit, or a false alarm from a monitor whose tolerance is too tight for some honest motion. The fuzzer is how you find both.

The workflow is a loop: fuzz, take the largest category of failures, replay one, find and fix its cause, and fuzz again with the same seeds so the comparison is fair. A fix that moves failures from one category to another is not a fix.

Fixing causes, not symptoms

The tempting fix for an explosion is a clamp: limit every velocity to 50 m/s, and nothing can fly off. It works, in the sense that the screen stops showing the problem. The stiff box still has an unstable mode; it now vibrates at the speed limit instead of flying apart, quietly turning the clamp into an energy source and the box into a buzzing mess. Clamps hide bugs from you and from your monitors.

Some limits are honest, though. A limit is honest when it models something real, or when it is a deliberate, documented design decision, and when you count how often it fires:

  • A separation speed limit for depenetration ("overlapping bodies separate at no more than 1 m/s") is a design choice: it accepts a few frames of visible overlap in exchange for never injecting energy. Many engines do this by moving positions apart without letting the correction count as velocity.
  • An actuator rate limit, like the activation time constant of the muscles chapter, models how fast a real motor or muscle can change its command.
  • A physical speed, like the terminal velocity of a falling leaf, belongs in the model as drag, not as a clamp.

A clamp on the result of the solver is a different thing: a statement that you do not know why it misbehaves. Find out.

Solution 
for (let f = 0; f < frames && kind === 'ok'; f++) {
  const input = program(f);
  const before = energy(S);
  stepScene(S, input);
  const rep = checkFrame(S, before, energy(S), S.grab >= 0);
  if (rep) kind = rep.kind;
}

The remaining failures are stretches, and they come from heavy weights with few iterations.

Summary

  • An explosion is energy from nowhere. Recognise its signature: steady growth (an unstable mode), a one-frame jump (an injection), or NaN (an invalid operation that then spreads).
  • The usual causes: $h\omega$ above the limit; mass and stiffness ratios; conflicting or redundant constraints (Jacobi without averaging); overlapping starts; deep penetrations resolved in one step ($v = d/h$); division by small numbers; NaN propagation.
  • Monitor non-finite numbers after every phase, energy against a tolerance (excluding external work), and constraint errors. Stop at the first bad frame and blame the particle whose speed rose most.
  • Make the simulation deterministic, record its inputs, replay to the frame before the failure, and reduce it to a minimal reproduction.
  • Fuzz with random settings and inputs, count failures by kind, fix the largest category, and re-run with the same seeds.
  • Fix causes. A clamp is honest only when it models something or is a documented design decision whose firing you count.
