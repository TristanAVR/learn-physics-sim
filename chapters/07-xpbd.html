XPBD: Compliance

One extra term per constraint, and stiffness stops depending on how hard the solver tries.

Position-based dynamics, from the last chapter, is wonderfully robust: it never explodes, and a rope made of distance constraints behaves like a rope. But you also met its awkward secret. The stiffness of a PBD constraint is not a property of the material. It is a property of the solver settings. Run twice as many iterations and the rope gets stiffer. Halve the time step and it gets stiffer again. Tune a cloth to look right at 60 Hz with ten iterations, and it looks wrong as soon as someone changes either number.

In 2016 Miles Macklin, Matthias Müller and Nuttapong Chentanez fixed this with a small change they called XPBD, extended position-based dynamics. Each constraint gets a compliance, the inverse of a physical stiffness, and the solver keeps one extra number per constraint during the step. The update is still a few lines of code, but now it is solving a well-defined physical problem: implicit Euler for a set of springs.

In this chapter you will derive XPBD from implicit Euler, implement it, and predict the stretch of a hanging chain to three significant figures. You will also learn to compute one number, the stiffness ratio $S$, that tells you in advance whether a constraint will converge in five iterations or five hundred.

The problem, measured

Here is the scene for the whole chapter. A cord of ten links, 1.5 m long in total, hangs from a fixed point. Each of the nine beads along it has a mass of 0.1 kg, and a 2 kg weight hangs at the bottom. The links are supposed to be rigid, so every centimetre of that stretch is solver error, and it halves each time you double the iterations. The time step matters too: with 20 iterations the stretch is 0.70 cm at $h$ = 8.3 ms, 2.81 cm at 16.7 ms and 11.25 cm at 33.3 ms, growing like $h^2$. If you wanted a cord that stretches by exactly 3 cm under this weight, you could tune PBD's stiffness parameter until it did, and the tuning would be valid for exactly one combination of iteration count and time step.

A spring is a soft constraint

In chapter 5 a constraint was a function $C(\mathbf{x})$ of the positions that should be zero. A distance constraint between particles $a$ and $b$ with rest length $L$ is

$$C(\mathbf{x}) = |\mathbf{x}_b - \mathbf{x}_a| - L,$$

so $C$ is the stretch of the link, in metres. A hard constraint insists on $C = 0$. A spring allows $C \ne 0$ but charges energy for it. Write that energy with the constraint function inside it:

$$U(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2\alpha}\,C(\mathbf{x})^2 .$$

Compare it with the familiar spring energy $\tfrac12 k\,C^2$: the two are the same if

$$\alpha = \frac{1}{k}.$$

The number $\alpha$ is the compliance: how far the constraint gives per unit of force. For a distance constraint it is in metres per newton. A compliance of zero is an infinitely stiff spring, which is a hard constraint. That is the first nice thing about writing springs this way: hard and soft constraints are the same object, with one number sliding between them, and the formulas never divide by zero as the constraint becomes rigid.

The force the spring applies to each particle is minus the gradient of the energy:

$$\mathbf{f}_i = -\nabla_i U = -\frac{1}{\alpha}\,C\,\nabla_i C .$$

For the distance constraint, $\nabla_b C = \mathbf{n}$ and $\nabla_a C = -\mathbf{n}$, where $\mathbf{n}$ is the unit vector from $a$ to $b$. So a stretched link ($C > 0$) pulls $b$ back towards $a$ with force $C/\alpha = kC$: Hooke's law, as it should be.

With many constraints, collect them in a vector $\mathbf{C} = (C_1, \dots, C_m)$ and put the compliances on the diagonal of a matrix $\boldsymbol\alpha$. The total energy is $U = \tfrac12 \mathbf{C}^T \boldsymbol\alpha^{-1} \mathbf{C}$, and its gradient is $\nabla U = \nabla\mathbf{C}^T \boldsymbol\alpha^{-1} \mathbf{C}$, where $\nabla\mathbf{C}$ is the $m \times 3N$ matrix whose row $j$ is the gradient of $C_j$ (chapter 5 called it the Jacobian $\mathbf{J}$).

Deriving XPBD from implicit Euler

Now take one implicit Euler step, from chapter 3, with these spring forces plus any external force $\mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}}$ such as gravity. Implicit Euler evaluates the spring force at the end of the step. Written in positions only, with $\mathbf{M}$ the diagonal mass matrix, it says

$$\mathbf{M}\,\frac{\mathbf{x}^{n+1} - 2\mathbf{x}^n + \mathbf{x}^{n-1}}{h^2} = -\nabla\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{x}^{n+1})^T \boldsymbol\alpha^{-1}\,\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{x}^{n+1}) + \mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}} .$$

Everything we know at the start of the step can be gathered into the predicted position, the place each particle would reach with no constraints at all:

$$\tilde{\mathbf{x}} = \mathbf{x}^n + h\,\mathbf{v}^n + h^2\,\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}} .$$

That is exactly the PBD prediction step. Multiplying the equation by $h^2$ and moving the known terms to one side gives

$$\mathbf{M}\,(\mathbf{x}^{n+1} - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}) = -\nabla\mathbf{C}^T\,\frac{\boldsymbol\alpha^{-1}}{h^2}\,\mathbf{C} .$$

The time step and the compliance appear only together, so give the combination a name, the time-scaled compliance:

$$\tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha} = \frac{\boldsymbol\alpha}{h^2}.$$

Next, introduce a Lagrange multiplier per constraint, defined as

$$\boldsymbol\lambda = -\tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha}^{-1}\,\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{x}) .$$

With it, the implicit Euler step splits into two equations that must both hold at the end of the step:

$$\mathbf{M}\,(\mathbf{x} - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}) - \nabla\mathbf{C}(\mathbf{x})^T\boldsymbol\lambda = \mathbf{0}, \qquad \mathbf{C}(\mathbf{x}) + \tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha}\,\boldsymbol\lambda = \mathbf{0}.$$

The first says: particles move away from their prediction only because constraint forces push them, and those forces point along the constraint gradients. The second says: each constraint is violated by exactly as much as its compliance allows for the force it carries. For $\alpha = 0$ the second equation becomes $C = 0$, and we are back to the hard constraints of chapter 5. This trick of dividing the energy's stiffness out into a multiplier is what keeps everything finite as $\alpha \to 0$.

These equations are nonlinear in $\mathbf{x}$. Solve them with Newton's method: start from $\mathbf{x} = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ and $\boldsymbol\lambda = \mathbf{0}$, linearise, and solve for corrections $\Delta\mathbf{x}$ and $\Delta\boldsymbol\lambda$:

$$\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{K} & -\nabla\mathbf{C}^T \\ \nabla\mathbf{C} & \tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha} \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \Delta\mathbf{x} \\ \Delta\boldsymbol\lambda \end{pmatrix} = -\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{g} \\ \mathbf{C} + \tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha}\boldsymbol\lambda \end{pmatrix},$$

where $\mathbf{g}$ is the left-hand side of the first equation and $\mathbf{K}$ is its derivative with respect to $\mathbf{x}$: the mass matrix plus a term involving the second derivatives of the constraints. XPBD makes two approximations. First, it drops the second-derivative term, so $\mathbf{K} \approx \mathbf{M}$. Second, it takes $\mathbf{g} = \mathbf{0}$. At the start of the step $\mathbf{g}$ really is zero ($\mathbf{x} = \tilde{\mathbf{x}}$, $\boldsymbol\lambda = 0$), and each update keeps it at zero exactly if the constraint gradients do not change within the step, and approximately when they change slowly. With both approximations, the first row gives

$$\Delta\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{M}^{-1}\nabla\mathbf{C}^T\,\Delta\boldsymbol\lambda ,$$

and substituting into the second row leaves a system for the multipliers alone:

$$\left(\nabla\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{M}^{-1}\nabla\mathbf{C}^T + \tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha}\right)\Delta\boldsymbol\lambda = -\mathbf{C} - \tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha}\,\boldsymbol\lambda .$$

This is a linear system with one unknown per constraint. Chapter 9 is about solving it properly. XPBD, like PBD, solves it with Gauss-Seidel: take one constraint at a time, solve its own row while pretending the others are fixed, apply the correction immediately, and move on. For constraint $j$ the row is a single equation in a single unknown:

$$\boxed{\;\Delta\lambda_j = \frac{-C_j - \tilde\alpha_j\,\lambda_j}{\sum_i w_i\,|\nabla_i C_j|^2 + \tilde\alpha_j}, \qquad \Delta\mathbf{x}_i = w_i\,\nabla_i C_j\,\Delta\lambda_j, \qquad \lambda_j \leftarrow \lambda_j + \Delta\lambda_j\;}$$

where $w_i = 1/m_i$ is the inverse mass of particle $i$ and the sum runs over the particles the constraint touches. Set $\tilde\alpha_j = 0$ and this is exactly the PBD projection from chapter 6. The whole difference between PBD and XPBD is the two $\tilde\alpha$ terms and the bookkeeping of $\lambda_j$.

For a distance constraint, $|\nabla_a C|^2 = |\nabla_b C|^2 = 1$, so the denominator is $w_a + w_b + \tilde\alpha$, and the code is short:

function solveLink(j, alphaTilde) {
  const a = j, b = j + 1;
  const dx = x[b] - x[a], dy = y[b] - y[a];
  const len = Math.hypot(dx, dy);
  const nx = dx / len, ny = dy / len;       // grad_b C = n, grad_a C = -n
  const C = len - restLength;
  const dLambda = (-C - alphaTilde * lambda[j]) / (w[a] + w[b] + alphaTilde);
  lambda[j] += dLambda;                     // accumulate over the iterations of this step
  x[a] -= w[a] * nx * dLambda; y[a] -= w[a] * ny * dLambda;
  x[b] += w[b] * nx * dLambda; y[b] += w[b] * ny * dLambda;
}

The surrounding loop is the PBD loop you already know, with one new line: the multipliers start at zero at the beginning of every step.

function step(h) {
  const alphaTilde = compliance / (h * h);
  predict(h);                               // x~ = x + h v + h^2 w f_ext, remembering the old x
  lambda.fill(0);                           // a fresh multiplier for this step
  for (let it = 0; it < iterations; it++)
    for (let j = 0; j < links; j++) solveLink(j, alphaTilde);
  updateVelocities(h);                      // v = (x - x_old) / h
}

Two easy mistakes. If you forget to reset $\lambda$ each step, it keeps growing from step to step and the constraint drifts. If you forget to accumulate it (using $\lambda_j = 0$ in every iteration), the $\tilde\alpha\lambda$ term vanishes and the compliance becomes iteration-dependent again: each iteration then relaxes the spring a little further, and the more iterations you run, the stiffer it gets. The multiplier is the total force this constraint has applied so far in the step, and the update needs it.

Why the stiffness no longer depends on the solver

Stop iterating only when the corrections are zero. Then every constraint satisfies $C_j + \tilde\alpha_j\lambda_j = 0$ and the positions satisfy $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x} - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}) = \nabla\mathbf{C}^T\boldsymbol\lambda$. Those are the implicit Euler equations we started from. They contain the time step, the masses and the compliances, but not the iteration count and not the order in which the constraints were visited. That is the first promise of XPBD: at convergence, the result does not depend on how you got there.

What about the time step? Look at a chain hanging at rest, so $\mathbf{x}^{n+1} = \mathbf{x}^n$ and $\mathbf{v}^n = 0$. Then $\tilde{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{x} = h^2\mathbf{M}^{-1}\mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}}$, and the first equation becomes

$$\nabla\mathbf{C}^T\,\frac{\boldsymbol\lambda}{h^2} = -\mathbf{f}_{\text{ext}} .$$

The constraint forces $\nabla\mathbf{C}^T\boldsymbol\lambda/h^2$ exactly cancel gravity. Call the force in link $j$ its tension $T_j = |\lambda_j|/h^2$. The second equation then says $C_j = \alpha_j T_j$: stretch equals compliance times force, Hooke's law, with no $h$ anywhere. The static shape is the true static shape, whatever the time step. (The motion still depends on $h$: implicit Euler adds numerical damping, as chapter 3 showed, so the weight's bouncing dies out faster with a large step. Only the stiffness has become physical.)

For our cord we can write the static stretch down. Number the links from the top, $j = 0, \dots, n-1$. Link $j$ holds up the weight $M$ and the $n-1-j$ beads of mass $m$ below it, so it carries

$$T_j = \bigl(M + (n-1-j)\,m\bigr)\,g, \qquad \Delta L = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} \alpha\,T_j = \alpha\,g\left(nM + \frac{n(n-1)}{2}\,m\right).$$

With $n = 10$, $M = 2$ kg, $m = 0.1$ kg and $\alpha = 10^{-3}$ m/N, that is $10^{-3} \times 9.81 \times (20 + 4.5) = 0.2403$ m, or 24.03 cm. Here is what the demo measures after the bouncing has died out:

static stretch, $h$ = 16.7 ms29.29 cm24.82 cm24.06 cm24.03 cm24.03 cm

And with 100 iterations at three time steps, 8.3, 16.7 and 33.3 ms, the stretch is 24.02, 24.03 and 24.04 cm. The small differences are the last of the bouncing, not a change in stiffness. PBD at the same three time steps stretched by 0.70, 2.81 and 11.25 cm.

XPBD is iteration-independent only once it has converged. With 5 iterations the cord above stretches 22 % too much. Before convergence, XPBD inherits PBD's problem: the leftover error looks like extra softness. The difference is that XPBD has a target to converge to, the physical stretch, and more iterations get you closer to it, never past it. In PBD, more iterations take you towards a rigid rod, whatever you intended.

Units of compliance

Because $U = C^2/(2\alpha)$ is an energy, the compliance always has units of $[C]^2$ per joule. That gives a different unit for each kind of constraint:

Constraint$C$ measuresCompliance $\alpha$From material properties
distance (link of length $L$)stretch, mm/N$\alpha = L/(EA)$, with $E$ Young's modulus and $A$ the cross-section
bending angleangle, radrad2/J, i.e. 1/(N m)$\alpha = L/(EI)$, with $EI$ the bending stiffness (chapter 11)
volumevolume change, m3m6/J, i.e. m3/Pa$\alpha = V/K$, with $K$ the bulk modulus and $V$ the rest volume

A worked example: a rubber cord 1 cm in diameter, with $E \approx 2$ MPa, has $EA = 2\times10^6 \times \pi (0.005)^2 = 157$ N. Cut into links of 15 cm, each link has $\alpha = 0.15/157 = 9.5\times10^{-4}$ m/N, close to the demo's default. A steel cable 2 mm across ($E = 200$ GPa) with the same links has $\alpha = 2.4\times10^{-7}$ m/N, four thousand times stiffer. Notice that the compliance of a link grows with its length: if you split a rope into twice as many links, each one is twice as stiff, and the rope as a whole keeps its stiffness. Setting the same $\alpha$ for every link regardless of resolution is a common way to make a rope that changes behaviour when you refine it.

Reading forces out of the solver

We already used it above: since $\mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x} - \tilde{\mathbf{x}})/h^2$ is mass times acceleration, the force constraint $j$ applied during the step is

$$\mathbf{f}_j = \frac{\nabla C_j^T\,\lambda_j}{h^2}, \qquad |\mathbf{f}_j| = \frac{|\lambda_j|}{h^2}\ \text{for a distance constraint}.$$

PBD gives you no such number. With XPBD you can read the tension in a rope, the force on a hinge or the pressure behind a contact, which is what you need to break a link that is overloaded, play a sound when something hits hard, or check your simulator against a hand calculation. The demo shows $|\lambda_0|/h^2$ for the top link next to the exact load, $(M + 9m)g = 28.45$ N.

Try it with few iterations. With 5 iterations the stretch is 22 % off, yet the top link force still reads 28.45 N. That is not a coincidence. At rest, the predict step moves every particle down by $h^2 g$, and the constraints must move them back, exactly, or the chain would be sinking. Since every correction is $w_i\nabla_i C_j\Delta\lambda_j$, the accumulated multipliers are pinned by momentum balance, converged or not. What convergence decides is whether $C_j = -\tilde\alpha_j\lambda_j$ holds, that is, whether the stretch matches the force. So an unconverged XPBD constraint carries the right force at the wrong stretch: it behaves like a softer spring.

How rigid does a constraint look? The stiffness ratio

Why does a stiffer cord need more iterations? Look at the denominator of the update. It has two parts: the "mobility" $\sum_i w_i|\nabla_i C|^2$, how easily the particles move, and the time-scaled compliance $\tilde\alpha$, how easily the constraint gives. Their ratio is the number that controls everything:

$$S = \frac{\sum_i w_i\,|\nabla_i C|^2}{\tilde\alpha} = \frac{h^2\sum_i w_i\,|\nabla_i C|^2}{\alpha}.$$

For one particle of mass $m$ on a spring to a fixed point, $S = h^2 k/m = (h\omega)^2$: the same $h\omega$ that decided stability in chapter 3. So $S$ is the stiffness of the constraint measured against the time step.

Rewrite the update in terms of $S$. At the start of a step ($\lambda = 0$),

$$\Delta\lambda = \frac{-C}{\tilde\alpha\,(1 + S)}, \qquad\text{and the constraint moves by}\qquad \Delta C = -\frac{S}{1+S}\,C .$$

When $S \ll 1$ the constraint is soft compared with the time step. Each update moves the particles only a fraction $S/(1+S)$ of the way to satisfying it, and, more importantly, it barely disturbs its neighbours: most of the "answer" is carried by the $\tilde\alpha\lambda$ term, which is local to the constraint. Gauss-Seidel converges in a handful of sweeps. When $S \gg 1$ the denominator is dominated by the mobility, the update is almost the PBD update, and the constraint behaves like a rigid one for the solver: each correction shoves its neighbours, which shove theirs, and the iteration needs as many sweeps as PBD would. (Chapter 9 makes "as many sweeps" precise: for a rigid chain of $n$ links it grows like $n^2$.)

For the demo cord, the middle links join two 0.1 kg beads, so $\sum w = 20$ kg-1, and $h^2 = 2.78\times 10^{-4}$ s2. Measuring the iterations needed to get within 1 % of the exact stretch:

The iteration count grows roughly in proportion to $S$. There is a simple reason. The leftover error of an unconverged solve behaves like PBD's, shrinking roughly like $1/N$ with $N$ iterations, while the stretch we are trying to resolve is proportional to $\alpha$, that is, to $1/S$. Keeping the error a fixed fraction of the stretch needs $N \propto S$. A steel cable link on the same beads has $S \approx 23{,}000$; no sensible iteration count reaches its true stretch at this time step.

That leaves two levers. You can reduce $S$ by making $h$ smaller, since $S \propto h^2$: halving the step cuts $S$ by four. That is the subject of the next chapter, and it turns out to be far better than adding iterations. Or you can stop using Gauss-Seidel for the hard part and solve the system directly, which is chapter 9.

Mass ratios hide in $S$. The mobility $\sum w$ is dominated by the lightest particle a constraint touches. A link between a 2 kg weight and a 0.1 kg bead has $\sum w = 10.5$ kg-1, set almost entirely by the bead. Light particles attached to heavy ones make constraints look rigid, which is why a heavy weight on a light chain is the classic hard case for iterative solvers.

Damping

Real springs lose energy. The XPBD paper adds damping through a dissipation potential, which amounts to a damping force $-\beta\,\nabla C^T\,\dot C$ along the constraint gradient, with $\dot C = \nabla C\,\mathbf{v}$ the rate at which the constraint is changing. For a distance constraint $\beta$ is a damping coefficient in N s/m, exactly the $c$ of a dashpot from chapter 4. Carrying it through the same derivation, with $\tilde\beta = h^2\beta$, gives a modified update:

$$\Delta\lambda_j = \frac{-C_j - \tilde\alpha_j\lambda_j - \gamma_j\,\nabla C_j\,(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^n)} {(1 + \gamma_j)\sum_i w_i|\nabla_i C_j|^2 + \tilde\alpha_j}, \qquad \gamma_j = \frac{\tilde\alpha_j\tilde\beta_j}{h} = \frac{\alpha_j\beta_j}{h}.$$

The new numerator term $\nabla C_j(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^n)$ is how much the constraint has changed during this step so far, which is $h$ times its rate of change: the velocity the damper resists. The factor $\gamma$ is dimensionless; $\alpha\beta = \beta/k$ is the damper's time constant, and $\gamma$ measures it in time steps. You will add this term in the last exercise.

One weight on one link is a damped oscillator with $k = 1/\alpha$, so the critical damping from chapter 4 is $\beta_{\text{crit}} = 2\sqrt{kM} = 2\sqrt{M/\alpha}$. For $M$ = 2 kg and $\alpha = 10^{-3}$ m/N that is 89.4 N s/m.

the XPBD paper in our notation

Macklin, Müller and Chentanez, XPBD: Position-Based Simulation of Compliant Constrained Dynamics (Motion in Games 2016), derive exactly what this chapter did: implicit Euler in positions, the multiplier $\boldsymbol\lambda = -\tilde{\boldsymbol\alpha}^{-1}\mathbf{C}$, the two equations they call $\mathbf{g}$ and $\mathbf{h}$, the Newton step and the two approximations $\mathbf{K} \approx \mathbf{M}$ and $\mathbf{g} \approx \mathbf{0}$. Their central per-constraint update is their equation (18), our boxed formula, and the damped version comes from their section on damping. The notation is the same as ours, which is why the site uses it: $\alpha$, $\tilde\alpha$, $\lambda$, $\mathbf{M}$, $\nabla C$. The paper's experiments show the same independence from iteration count and time step that you measured here, and it points out that $\lambda/h^2$ gives constraint force estimates. When you reach Position-Based MPM, you will find the same predict, project, update loop, applied to a continuum.

Summary

  • A spring is a soft constraint with energy $U = C^2/(2\alpha)$, where the compliance $\alpha = 1/k$ is in units of $[C]^2$ per joule (m/N for a distance). $\alpha = 0$ is a hard constraint.
  • XPBD is implicit Euler for that energy, solved with one Newton step per iteration and Gauss-Seidel over the constraints. Its update is $\Delta\lambda = (-C - \tilde\alpha\lambda)/(\sum w|\nabla C|^2 + \tilde\alpha)$ with $\tilde\alpha = \alpha/h^2$, and $\Delta\mathbf{x}_i = w_i\nabla_i C\,\Delta\lambda$.
  • The multiplier is reset each step and accumulated over the iterations. At convergence the result does not depend on the iteration count, and the static shape does not depend on the time step: stretch equals $\alpha$ times load.
  • $\lambda/h^2$ is the constraint's force, and it is right even before the stretch has converged.
  • The stiffness ratio $S = h^2\sum w|\nabla C|^2/\alpha$ predicts convergence: $S \ll 1$ converges in a few iterations, $S \gg 1$ behaves like PBD and needs roughly $S$ times more. Light particles and small compliances make $S$ large; small time steps make it small.
  • Damping adds $-\gamma\nabla C(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}^n)$ to the numerator and $(1+\gamma)$ to the mobility, with $\gamma = \alpha\beta/h$.

  1. What goes wrong if you do not accumulate $\lambda$ across iterations? And if you do not reset it each step?
  2. A 0.5 kg mass hangs from a single link of compliance $2\times10^{-4}$ m/N. What is its static stretch, and what is $S$ at $h$ = 1/60 s?
  3. Why is the force estimate $\lambda/h^2$ correct for a resting chain even when the stretch is not?
  4. You double the resolution of a rope, from 20 links to 40. What should happen to the compliance of each link to keep the rope's overall stretch the same?
  5. Name two ways to make a constraint with $S = 1000$ converge, and say which one the next chapter prefers.
