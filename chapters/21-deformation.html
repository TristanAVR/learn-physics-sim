The Deformation Gradient

One small matrix that tells every piece of a material how it has been stretched, sheared, turned or crushed.

So far every soft thing on this site has been built from parts: springs between particles, distance constraints, triangles of cloth. Each part has its own rest length or rest angle, and the material's behaviour is whatever the parts add up to. That works, but it ties the physics to the mesh. Cut a block of jelly into a different set of triangles and it becomes a slightly different jelly. And when the material flows, like dough being kneaded or sand pouring out of a bucket, there is no fixed mesh to hang the springs on at all.

Continuum mechanics takes the opposite view. It describes the material itself, as a smooth substance, and asks one local question at every point: how has the neighbourhood of this point been deformed? The answer is a 2×2 matrix in 2D (3×3 in 3D) called the deformation gradient, $\mathbf{F}$. Everything in the next eight chapters, from elastic stress to snow plasticity to the Material Point Method, is built on it.

By the end of this chapter you will be able to compute $\mathbf{F}$ for a triangle, read off its area change, split it into a rotation and a stretch, measure strain in a way that ignores rotation, and evolve $\mathbf{F}$ through time in a velocity field with the same one-line update that MPM codes use.

Matter as a continuum

A block of rubber is made of long tangled molecules, and sand is made of grains, but at the scale we care about neither fact matters. We adopt the continuum assumption: the material fills space smoothly, and quantities such as density, velocity and stress are smooth functions of position. A "point" of the material is then a tiny neighbourhood: small enough that the deformation inside it is uniform, large enough to contain many molecules or grains.

The payoff is that we can use calculus. The price is that we must be careful about which coordinates we use, because a material that moves and deforms has two natural ways of naming its points.

Two sets of coordinates

Paint a grid on a block of jelly while it sits at rest, and label each painted point by where it was: its material coordinates (or rest coordinates) $X$. These labels belong to the material. When you squash the jelly, the paint moves with it, so the point labelled $X$ keeps its label forever.

Where that point is now is its world coordinates $x$ (also called spatial or deformed coordinates). The motion of the whole material is a single function, the deformation map $\phi$:

$$x = \phi(X, t).$$

At $t = 0$ we usually choose $\phi(X, 0) = X$: the rest shape and the starting shape coincide. Two examples. If the block slides along a direction $n$ at speed $s$, then $\phi(X, t) = X + s\,t\,n$. If it moves rigidly, turning by a rotation matrix $\mathbf{R}$ and shifting by $b$, then $\phi(X, t) = \mathbf{R}X + b$. Neither motion deforms the jelly, and whatever measure of deformation we invent had better say so.

The velocity of a material point is how fast its world position changes while its label stays fixed: $V(X, t) = \partial\phi/\partial t$. Hold on to the phrase "label fixed"; it will matter when we meet the Eulerian view later in the chapter.

The deformation gradient

Pick a material point $X$ and a second point a tiny distance away, $X + dX$. After the motion they sit at $\phi(X)$ and $\phi(X + dX)$. A first-order Taylor expansion gives the new separation:

$$dx = \phi(X + dX) - \phi(X) \approx \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial X}\, dX = \mathbf{F}\, dX.$$

That matrix of partial derivatives is the deformation gradient:

$$\mathbf{F} = \frac{\partial x}{\partial X} = \begin{pmatrix} \partial x_1/\partial X_1 & \partial x_1/\partial X_2 \\ \partial x_2/\partial X_1 & \partial x_2/\partial X_2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad F_{ij} = \frac{\partial x_i}{\partial X_j}.$$

Read the equation $dx = \mathbf{F}\,dX$ as a recipe: give me a tiny arrow drawn in the rest material, and $\mathbf{F}$ tells you where that arrow is now. In particular, the first column of $\mathbf{F}$ is what happened to a tiny arrow that pointed along $X_1$ at rest, and the second column is what happened to one along $X_2$. Translation does not appear at all: $\mathbf{F}$ only knows about the shape of the neighbourhood, not where it is.

When $\phi$ is an affine map, $x = \mathbf{F}X + b$, the matrix is the same everywhere and the formula is exact for arrows of any length. In general $\mathbf{F}$ varies from point to point, and the affine map is only the best local approximation, like a tangent line to a curve. Here are four affine examples, all in the demo below. Each maps the unit square $[0, 1]^2$:

Motion$\mathbf{F}$What the square becomes
Stretch $x_1$ by 2$\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$a 2 × 1 rectangle
Shear by 0.5$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0.5 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$a parallelogram leaning right; the top edge slides 0.5
Rotate by 30°$\begin{pmatrix} 0.866 & -0.5 \\ 0.5 & 0.866 \end{pmatrix}$the same square, turned
Stretch 1.5 × 0.8, then rotate 30°$\begin{pmatrix} 1.299 & -0.400 \\ 0.750 & 0.693 \end{pmatrix}$a turned 1.5 × 0.8 rectangle

Look at the last one. Its entries give no obvious hint of either the stretch or the angle; untangling them is the job of the polar decomposition, coming shortly. For translation, $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{I}$; for a rigid motion, $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}$.

Play with the sliders first. The dashed circle is a small circle of material at rest; the solid ellipse is the same material after deformation. The orange and green arrows are the ellipse's axes: the principal stretches. Notice that rotating changes the picture but not the readouts of stretch and strain, and that pushing "stretch y" below zero turns everything purple. Both facts are explained below.

Area change and inversion: $J = \det \mathbf{F}$

The two columns of $\mathbf{F}$ are the images of two unit arrows. The parallelogram they span has area $\det \mathbf{F}$, while the unit square they came from had area 1. So the determinant is the local area ratio (volume ratio in 3D), and it gets its own letter:

$$J = \det \mathbf{F}, \qquad dv = J\, dV.$$

Here $dV$ is a tiny area in the rest material and $dv$ the area it occupies now. In the examples above: the stretch has $J = 2$, the shear and the rotation have $J = 1$ (shearing a square into a parallelogram keeps its area), and the combination has $J = 1.5 \times 0.8 = 1.2$. This is how MPM will track the volume of each particle: store its rest volume $V^0_p$ and compute the current volume as $J_p V^0_p$.

$J > 1$ means expansion, $J \lt 1$ compression. $J = 0$ means the neighbourhood has been flattened to a line, and $J \lt 0$ means it has been turned inside out: the material is inverted. A triangle with $J \lt 0$ has had one corner pushed through the opposite edge, so its corners now go round the other way. Real matter never does this, but simulations do it all the time when a large time step or a violent collision pushes points past each other, and in the next chapter you will see that material models differ sharply in how they cope.

$\mathbf{F}$ for a triangle

Meshes give us $\mathbf{F}$ directly. Take a triangle with rest corners $X_0, X_1, X_2$ and current corners $x_0, x_1, x_2$. Inside a linear triangle the deformation is affine, so one $\mathbf{F}$ maps every rest edge onto the current edge: $x_1 - x_0 = \mathbf{F}(X_1 - X_0)$ and $x_2 - x_0 = \mathbf{F}(X_2 - X_0)$. Stack the edge vectors as the columns of two matrices,

$$\mathbf{D}_m = \begin{pmatrix} X_1 - X_0 & X_2 - X_0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathbf{D}_s = \begin{pmatrix} x_1 - x_0 & x_2 - x_0 \end{pmatrix},$$

and both edge equations become one: $\mathbf{D}_s = \mathbf{F}\,\mathbf{D}_m$, so

$$\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{D}_s\, \mathbf{D}_m^{-1}.$$

The subscripts are the conventional ones from finite elements: $m$ for "material", $s$ for "spatial". $\mathbf{D}_m$ never changes, so a simulator inverts it once per triangle and stores it. A check you can do in your head: rest corners $(0,0), (2,0), (0,1)$ and current corners $(1,1), (1,3), (0,1)$ give $\mathbf{D}_m = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{D}_s = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 2 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, so $\mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$: a rotation by 90°, with $J = 1$. The triangle has turned and moved but not deformed. This formula is the heart of the finite element soft body in the next chapter.

Rotation is not deformation

We want a number that says "how deformed", and the obvious candidate, $\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{I}$, is wrong: for a rotation it is not zero, yet a rotated jelly is perfectly relaxed. What does not change under rotation is lengths and angles. The squared length of a deformed arrow is

$$|dx|^2 = dX^\mathsf{T} \mathbf{F}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{F}\, dX,$$

so all length and angle information sits in $\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{F}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{F}$ (the right Cauchy-Green tensor). If $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}$ then $\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{R}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$, and if we rotate a deformed body afterwards, $\mathbf{F} \to \mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}$, then $\mathbf{C}$ is unchanged. The Green strain measures the departure from rest:

$$\mathbf{E} = \tfrac12 \left(\mathbf{F}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{I}\right).$$

It is zero for any rotation, by construction. For the stretch by 2 it is $\mathbf{E} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.5 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$; the diagonal entry is $(2^2 - 1)/2$, not $2 - 1$, because Green strain is quadratic in the stretch. For the 0.5 shear it is $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0.25 \\ 0.25 & 0.125 \end{pmatrix}$: the off-diagonal entries record the change of angle between the rest axes.

The small-strain shortcut fails under rotation. Engineering texts often use the linearised strain $\boldsymbol\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mathbf{F} + \mathbf{F}^\mathsf{T}) - \mathbf{I}$, which is accurate when the deformation and the rotation are small. For a pure rotation by $\theta$ it gives $\boldsymbol\varepsilon = (\cos\theta - 1)\,\mathbf{I}$, which for 30° is $-0.134\,\mathbf{I}$: the formula claims a rigidly turned block has been compressed by 13 % in every direction. A simulator built on it creates forces out of nothing whenever something spins. You will watch this happen in the next chapter.

Polar decomposition and the SVD

Green strain throws the rotation away. Often we want both halves: how much the neighbourhood turned, and how it was stretched. Every $\mathbf{F}$ can be written as a stretch followed by a rotation,

$$\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{S},$$

with $\mathbf{R}$ a rotation and $\mathbf{S}$ symmetric. This is the polar decomposition. $\mathbf{S}$ is a pure stretch along two perpendicular directions, and $\mathbf{R}$ then turns the result; $\mathbf{R}$ is also the rotation closest to $\mathbf{F}$, which is why the corotated models of the next chapter use it to measure "the part of $\mathbf{F}$ that is not rotation".

In 2D the decomposition has a closed form. A rotation is one angle, $\mathbf{R}(\theta) = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta \\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}$, and $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{R}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{F}$ must be symmetric. With $\mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}$,

$$\mathbf{R}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix} a\cos\theta + c\sin\theta & b\cos\theta + d\sin\theta \\ c\cos\theta - a\sin\theta & d\cos\theta - b\sin\theta \end{pmatrix}.$$

Setting the two off-diagonal entries equal gives $(a + d)\sin\theta = (c - b)\cos\theta$, so

$$\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(c - b,\; a + d), \qquad \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{R}(\theta)^\mathsf{T}\,\mathbf{F}.$$

Of the two angles that satisfy the equation (they differ by 180°), $\operatorname{atan2}$ picks the one that makes $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{S} = \sqrt{(a+d)^2 + (c-b)^2}$ non-negative. No division, no special cases: when $a + d$ and $c - b$ are both zero, atan2 returns 0 and $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{F}$. For the 0.5 shear, $\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(-0.5, 2) = -14.0°$: a shear is a stretch plus a small rotation, which you can see in the demo as the ellipse's tilt.

Now diagonalise the symmetric $\mathbf{S}$. Its eigenvectors are the two perpendicular directions of pure stretch, and its eigenvalues $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2$ are the principal stretches. For $\mathbf{S} = \begin{pmatrix} p & q \\ q & r \end{pmatrix}$:

$$\sigma_{1,2} = \frac{p + r}{2} \pm \sqrt{\left(\frac{p - r}{2}\right)^2 + q^2}, \qquad \mathbf{V} = \mathbf{R}(\varphi),\quad \varphi = \tfrac12 \operatorname{atan2}(2q,\; p - r).$$

So $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T}$ with $\boldsymbol\Sigma = \operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1, \sigma_2)$, and substituting gives the singular value decomposition

$$\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T}, \qquad \mathbf{U} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{V}, \qquad \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T}, \qquad \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T}.$$

Read it right to left as three steps: $\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T}$ turns the material so its principal directions line up with the axes, $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ stretches along the axes, $\mathbf{U}$ turns the result into place. A circle of material becomes an ellipse with semi-axes $\sigma_1$ and $\sigma_2$ along the columns of $\mathbf{U}$, which is exactly what the demo draws. Two more facts fall out: $J = \sigma_1\sigma_2$, and the Green strain has eigenvalues $(\sigma_i^2 - 1)/2$. For the 0.5 shear, $\sigma_1 = 1.281$ and $\sigma_2 = 0.781$, and their product is 1.

function polar(F) {                                  // F = [a, b, c, d]
  const theta = Math.atan2(F[2] - F[1], F[0] + F[3]);
  const R = rotation(theta);
  return { R: R, S: mul(transpose(R), F), theta: theta };
}

function svd(F) {                                    // F = U diag(s1, s2) V^T
  const pd = polar(F);
  const e = symmetricEigen(pd.S);                    // S = V diag(l1, l2) V^T
  return { s1: e.l1, s2: e.l2, U: mul(pd.R, rotation(e.phi)), V: rotation(e.phi) };
}

Signed singular values. The textbook SVD makes both singular values non-negative and lets $\mathbf{U}$ or $\mathbf{V}$ be a reflection. Simulation codes prefer the convention above, where $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{V}$ are always rotations and an inverted element shows up as $\sigma_2 \lt 0$. Because $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{S} \ge 0$, the negative one is always the smaller in magnitude. The MPM course notes call this the "polar SVD", and it is what lets material models detect and push back against inversion. Try stretch y = $-0.5$ in the demo: the readout shows principal stretches 1 and $-0.5$.

Push forward and pull back

Because every material point has both a label $X$ and a position $x$, any quantity can be written as a function of either. Suppose you painted a temperature onto the jelly at rest: a function $G(X)$. Asking "what is the temperature at the world position $x$?" means finding which material point is there and reading its label, $g(x) = G(\phi^{-1}(x))$. That is a push forward: moving a function from the rest shape to the current shape. Going the other way, $G(X) = g(\phi(X))$, is a pull back. Nothing deep happens; it is a change of variables, and it needs $\phi$ to be invertible, which is the mathematical form of "two pieces of material never occupy the same place".

Vectors and areas push forward too, but they pick up factors of $\mathbf{F}$. A material arrow $dX$ pushes forward to $\mathbf{F}\,dX$, as we saw. An area element $dV$ pushes forward to $J\,dV$, so an integral over the current shape can be computed over the rest shape: $\int g\, dx = \int G\, J\, dX$. A surface element with normal $N$ and area $dS$ becomes one with normal $n$ and area $ds$, related by $n\, ds = J\,\mathbf{F}^{-\mathsf{T}} N\, dS$ (Nanson's formula). You will not need that last one to write code, but it is how the next chapter converts between the two kinds of stress.

Lagrangian and Eulerian views

There are two ways to watch a river. You can drop a cork in and follow it, recording its speed as it drifts: that is the Lagrangian view, where quantities are functions of the material label $X$. Or you can stand on a bridge and record the speed of whatever water happens to pass beneath you: the Eulerian view, where quantities are functions of a fixed world position $x$. Particles are Lagrangian; a fixed grid is Eulerian. Hybrid methods like MPM carry the material on Lagrangian particles and compute forces on an Eulerian grid, so you will constantly translate between the two.

The Eulerian velocity field is the push forward of the Lagrangian velocity: $v(x, t) = V(\phi^{-1}(x, t), t)$. Now ask how fast some quantity $f$ changes for the cork. Even if the field is steady at every fixed point, the cork moves through it, so

$$\frac{Df}{Dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + v \cdot \nabla f.$$

This is the material derivative: the rate of change following the material. The first term is the change at a fixed point; the second is the change caused by moving to a point where $f$ is different. An example with numbers: a pipe whose water temperature rises steadily along its length by 5 °C per metre, unchanging in time. At a fixed point, $\partial T/\partial t = 0$. A blob of water moving along the pipe at 2 m/s warms at $DT/Dt = 0 + 2 \times 5 = 10$ °C per second. Acceleration is the material derivative of velocity, $a = Dv/Dt = \partial v/\partial t + (\nabla v)\, v$, which is why a steady flow can still accelerate the water in it. Particle methods get this term for free, because they literally follow the cork.

How $\mathbf{F}$ changes in time

The last piece is the one MPM uses every step. Define the velocity gradient $\nabla v$, the Eulerian twin of $\mathbf{F}$:

$$\nabla v = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = \begin{pmatrix} \partial v_1/\partial x_1 & \partial v_1/\partial x_2 \\ \partial v_2/\partial x_1 & \partial v_2/\partial x_2 \end{pmatrix}.$$

It says how the velocity differs between nearby points: two points $dx$ apart separate at the rate $(\nabla v)\, dx$. Differentiate $\mathbf{F} = \partial\phi/\partial X$ in time with the label fixed, swap the order of the derivatives, and use the chain rule through $x = \phi(X, t)$:

$$\frac{\partial \mathbf{F}}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial}{\partial X}\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial V}{\partial X} = \frac{\partial v}{\partial x}\frac{\partial x}{\partial X} = (\nabla v)\,\mathbf{F}.$$

In words: the deformation of a neighbourhood grows at the rate set by the velocity differences across it, applied to the deformation it already has. Discretise with one explicit Euler step of size $h$, as in the Time Integration chapter, and you get the update that every particle in an MPM code performs:

$$\mathbf{F}_{n+1} = (\mathbf{I} + h\,\nabla v)\,\mathbf{F}_n.$$

In MPM, $\nabla v$ comes from the grid, as a weighted sum of grid velocities, $\nabla v_p = \sum_i v_i (\nabla w_{ip})^\mathsf{T}$; you will build that in the Material Point Method chapter. In this chapter the demo knows the velocity field in closed form, so it can evaluate $\nabla v$ exactly. Four fields to try, with rate $\omega$ (1/s):

  • Rigid rotation, $v = \omega(-x_2, x_1)$, $\nabla v = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\omega \\ \omega & 0 \end{pmatrix}$: should leave $J = 1$ and zero strain.
  • Shear flow, $v = (\omega x_2, 0)$, $\nabla v = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \omega \\ 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$: the exact answer is $\mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \omega t \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$, area preserved.
  • Expansion, $v = \omega x$, $\nabla v = \omega\mathbf{I}$: exactly $J = e^{2\omega t}$.
  • Vortex, $v = \frac{\omega}{1 + r^2/r_0^2}(-x_2, x_1)$: spins fast in the middle and slowly outside. Its divergence $\operatorname{tr}\nabla v$ is zero, so area is preserved, but the differential rotation shears the square into a spiral and the strain grows without limit.

The demo moves each point and its $\mathbf{F}$ with the same explicit step, both using the velocity at the old position:

for (let k = 0; k < count; k++) {
  const L = velocityGradient(posX[k], posY[k]);
  velocity(posX[k], posY[k], vel);
  integrateF(pointF, k, L, h);       // F <- (I + h L) F
  posX[k] += h * vel.x;
  posY[k] += h * vel.y;
}

Watch the triangles in the vortex. After a turn or two, neighbouring triangles alternate between blue and red, even though the points between them stay pale. A triangle's straight edges cannot follow material that is being curved and sheared within the size of one triangle, so its corner-based $\mathbf{F}$ goes wrong, while each point's own integrated $\mathbf{F}$ stays close to the truth. That is a first hint of why MPM keeps $\mathbf{F}$ on particles instead of on a mesh.

The shear flow comes out exact: $\nabla v$ is constant and $\det(\mathbf{I} + h\nabla v) = 1$, so $J$ stays 1 to the last digit and $\mathbf{F}_n = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & n h \omega \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}$. The rigid rotation is the interesting case, and it is your third exercise.

The update and the motion agree. For a linear velocity field the position step is $x_{n+1} = (\mathbf{I} + h\nabla v)\, x_n$, so $\mathbf{I} + h\nabla v$ is exactly the Jacobian of one step of the point motion, and the product of these matrices is exactly the deformation gradient of the discrete motion. Whatever error the time integration makes, $\mathbf{F}$ makes the same one, consistently. The colour of the points (their own $J$) and the colour of the triangles ($J$ from their corners) therefore agree, which is a good test of your code.

function triangleF(X0, X1, X2, x0, x1, x2) {
  const Dm = [X1.x - X0.x, X2.x - X0.x,
              X1.y - X0.y, X2.y - X0.y];
  const Ds = [x1.x - x0.x, x2.x - x0.x,
              x1.y - x0.y, x2.y - x0.y];
  return mul(Ds, inverse(Dm));
}

integrate F in a rotating flow

Switch the starter to "flow field" with "rigid rotation". The points circle the origin but their colours never change, because integrateF is empty. Implement $\mathbf{F} \leftarrow (\mathbf{I} + h\nabla v)\,\mathbf{F}$.

  1. A rigid rotation should keep $J = 1$ and the strain at zero. Before running it, compute $\det(\mathbf{I} + h\nabla v)$ for the rotation field, and predict $J$ after one full revolution at $\omega$ = 1 rad/s and $h$ = 16 ms. Write the number down, then measure it (the readout counts steps).
  2. What is the Green strain then? Explain why it is not zero even though every point is moving on a circle.
  3. Set $h$ to 300 ms. What happens, and which chapter have you seen this behaviour in before?
  4. Try the vortex. Its $J$ stays near 1 while the strain grows; why?
Hint: the prediction

$\mathbf{I} + h\nabla v = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -h\omega \\ h\omega & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ has determinant $1 + h^2\omega^2$, and $J$ multiplies by it every step. One revolution takes $2\pi/\omega$ seconds.

Solution 
function integrateF(Fs, k, L, h) {
  const o = 4 * k;
  const a = Fs[o], b = Fs[o + 1], c = Fs[o + 2], d = Fs[o + 3];
  const m00 = 1 + h * L[0], m01 = h * L[1], m10 = h * L[2], m11 = 1 + h * L[3];
  Fs[o] = m00 * a + m01 * c;
  Fs[o + 1] = m00 * b + m01 * d;
  Fs[o + 2] = m10 * a + m11 * c;
  Fs[o + 3] = m10 * b + m11 * d;
}

One revolution is $2\pi / 0.016 \approx 393$ steps, so $J = (1 + 0.016^2)^{393} \approx 1.106$: the square has gained 11 % in area. $\mathbf{I} + h\nabla v$ is a rotation scaled by $\sqrt{1 + h^2\omega^2}$, so after $n$ steps $\mathbf{F} = \sqrt{J}\,\mathbf{R}$ and the Green strain is $\tfrac12(J - 1)\,\mathbf{I}$, with norm about 0.075. The points really are spiralling outward: explicit Euler moves each point along the tangent, which always leaves the circle, and $\mathbf{F}$ faithfully records that expansion. This is the same growth factor $1 + h^2\omega^2$ that made explicit Euler gain energy on a spring in chapter 4. At $h$ = 300 ms the factor is 1.09 per step and $J$ passes 6 within one revolution. At $h$ = 1 ms the error after one revolution is only 0.6 %: the total drift is $n h^2\omega^2 = 2\pi h \omega$, first order in $h$. The vortex's $J$ stays near 1 because its divergence is zero (apart from the same explicit drift, strongest in the fast-spinning centre), but neighbouring points circle at different rates, which is shear, and shear accumulates without bound.

In a real MPM simulation this drift does not run away, because the material's elastic forces push back against any area change. It is still there, and it is one reason MPM codes sometimes show slow volume gain in fast-spinning objects.

Summary

  • A deforming material has material coordinates $X$ (labels) and world coordinates $x = \phi(X, t)$.
  • The deformation gradient $\mathbf{F} = \partial x/\partial X$ maps tiny rest arrows to their current shape, $dx = \mathbf{F}\,dX$. Translation leaves it at $\mathbf{I}$; a rigid rotation makes it $\mathbf{R}$.
  • $J = \det\mathbf{F}$ is the local area (volume) ratio; $J \lt 0$ means the material is inverted.
  • For a linear triangle, $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{D}_s\mathbf{D}_m^{-1}$ from its edge vectors.
  • Green strain $\mathbf{E} = \tfrac12(\mathbf{F}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{I})$ vanishes for any rotation; the linearised strain does not.
  • Polar decomposition $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}$ with $\theta = \operatorname{atan2}(c - b, a + d)$ in 2D, and the signed SVD $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T}$ whose singular values are the principal stretches.
  • The material derivative $Df/Dt = \partial f/\partial t + v\cdot\nabla f$ follows the material through an Eulerian field.
  • $\dot{\mathbf{F}} = (\nabla v)\mathbf{F}$, discretised as $\mathbf{F}_{n+1} = (\mathbf{I} + h\nabla v)\mathbf{F}_n$, is the MPM update. In a rigid rotation it multiplies $J$ by $1 + h^2\omega^2$ per step.

  1. Why is $\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{I}$ a bad measure of deformation, and what is a good one?
  2. A triangle has rest corners $(0,0), (1,0), (0,1)$ and current corners $(0,0), (3,0), (1,0.5)$. What are $\mathbf{F}$ and $J$, and has the triangle been inverted?
  3. In the polar decomposition, which factor would you use to rotate a texture painted on the material, and which to measure how much it is stretched?
