Constraints and Multipliers

When a spring can never be stiff enough, stop using a spring.

Some connections should not stretch at all. A pendulum's rod, the links of a chain, the threads of a rope under load: in the real world they stretch by fractions of a millimetre, and a simulation that lets them stretch by centimetres looks rubbery and wrong. The obvious fix is a stiffer spring, and the last two chapters told you how that ends. Stiffness raises the frequency $\omega$, and once $h\omega$ passes 2 the simulation explodes.

This chapter takes a different route. Instead of a force that pushes back when the length is wrong, we state the rule the motion must obey, "the distance stays $L$", and enforce it directly. That rule is a constraint. You will learn how to write constraints as equations, why the force that enforces them points where it does and does no work, what the mysterious multiplier $\lambda$ means physically, and the simplest way to enforce a constraint in code: by moving positions. By the end you will have a pendulum whose rod length is exact to the last bit at any time step, a double pendulum, and a rope that can go slack.

Why very stiff springs fail

Model a 1 kg pendulum bob on a 1 m rod as a spring of stiffness $k$. Released from 60°, the bob swings through the bottom with a rod tension of about 19.6 N (we will derive that number shortly). The spring stretches by tension divided by stiffness, so at $k = 10^4$ N/m the rod is about 2 mm too long at the bottom, and it bounces along its length as it swings.

To bring the error down to 0.1 mm we need $k \approx 19.6 / 0.0001 \approx 196{,}000$ N/m. The radial wobble of the bob then has $\omega = \sqrt{k/m} \approx 443$ rad/s, and symplectic Euler is stable only while $h\omega \le 2$, so $h \le 4.5$ ms. At a game's 16 ms the limit is $k \le 4m/h^2 = 15{,}625$ N/m. Every tenfold reduction in stretch costs a factor $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.2$ in time step, and an infinitely stiff rod would need an infinitely small step. The spring is fighting the wrong battle: it tries to make a force large enough, when what we actually want is a geometric fact.

Constraints as equations

A constraint is a function of the positions that must be zero (or non-negative). For the pendulum, with the bob at $\mathbf{x}$ and the pivot at $\mathbf{p}$,

$$C(\mathbf{x}) = |\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}| - L = 0.$$

This is a bilateral or equality constraint: the bob may be neither closer nor further than $L$. Other everyday constraints are inequalities, called unilateral:

  • the ground: $C(\mathbf{x}) = y \ge 0$, the particle may be anywhere above the floor but not below it;
  • a rope: $C(\mathbf{x}) = L - |\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}| \ge 0$, the end may come closer to the anchor (the rope goes slack) but not further.

The gradient $\nabla C$ is the vector of partial derivatives of $C$ with respect to the coordinates of the particle. It points in the direction in which $C$ increases fastest, and it is perpendicular to the set of positions where $C = 0$. For the distance constraint,

$$\nabla C = \frac{\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}}{|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{p}|} = \mathbf{n},$$

the unit vector from the pivot to the bob. Moving along $\mathbf{n}$ changes the length; moving perpendicular to it (around the circle) does not. For the ground, $\nabla C = (0, 1)$. When a constraint involves several particles, each particle gets its own gradient: for a distance constraint between $a$ and $b$, $\nabla_a C = \mathbf{n}$ and $\nabla_b C = -\mathbf{n}$, with $\mathbf{n}$ now pointing from $b$ to $a$.

The constraint force does no work

Suppose the bob is moving and obeys the constraint at every instant. Then $C$ stays zero, so its rate of change is zero. By the chain rule,

$$\frac{dC}{dt} = \nabla C \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0.$$

Every velocity the constraint allows is perpendicular to $\nabla C$. Now ask what force the rod must exert. We want a force that changes nothing the constraint allows and only stops what it forbids, so it should point along $\nabla C$:

$$\mathbf{F}_c = \lambda\,\nabla C.$$

The scalar $\lambda$ is called the Lagrange multiplier. Its power, the rate at which it does work, is $\mathbf{F}_c \cdot \mathbf{v} = \lambda\,\nabla C \cdot \mathbf{v} = 0$. An ideal constraint never adds or removes energy; it only redirects motion. That is the property a perfect rod, a frictionless floor or a frictionless hinge has, and it is why constraint forces are written this way in every textbook and paper you will read.

The direction is fixed by the geometry. The size, $\lambda$, is whatever it takes to keep $C = 0$, and that is the physically interesting part: for a rod, $|\lambda|$ is the tension. Here $\nabla C$ points outward, the rod pulls the bob inward, so $\lambda = -T < 0$. Newton's law along the rod gives the tension directly. The bob moves on a circle, so it needs a net inward (centripetal) force $m v^2/L$; the rod supplies that plus whatever balances the outward component of gravity, $m g\cos\theta$ at angle $\theta$ from the bottom:

$$T = m g\cos\theta + \frac{m v^2}{L}, \qquad T_\text{bottom} = m g + \frac{m v^2}{L}.$$

Released from rest at $\theta_0$, energy conservation gives $v^2 = 2gL(1 - \cos\theta_0)$ at the bottom, so $T_\text{bottom} = m g\,(3 - 2\cos\theta_0)$. From 60° that is $2mg = 19.62$ N, twice the bob's weight; from the horizontal it is $3mg$.

Run the demo at its default 16 ms step and $k = 10^4$ N/m. The red bob's rod error swings by up to 2.8 mm. Push the stiffness above about 15,600 N/m and the spring explodes, as predicted. The blue rod's error reads zero to every digit the readout shows (it is about $4 \times 10^{-16}$ m, the rounding error of a double), at any time step. That is the method we turn to now.

Enforcing a constraint by projecting positions

Here is a recipe that needs no stiffness at all. In each step:

  1. Predict. Move the particle as if the constraint did not exist: $\mathbf{v} \leftarrow \mathbf{v} + h\,\mathbf{g}$, then $\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{x} + h\,\mathbf{v}$. The bob flies off along its tangent and ends up slightly outside the circle.
  2. Project. Move $\mathbf{x}^*$ the shortest way back onto the constraint, along $\nabla C$.
  3. Update the velocity from the change in position: $\mathbf{v} = (\mathbf{x}^* - \mathbf{x})/h$, then $\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x}^*$.

Step 3 is the clever one. The projection in step 2 moved the particle; recomputing the velocity from the total displacement makes the velocity consistent with that move, which removes the part of the velocity that pointed off the constraint. The particle's state is now on the circle and moving along it.

How far to move in step 2, and how to share the move between two particles? Look for the smallest correction $\Delta\mathbf{x}_i$ that fixes $C$. To first order, $C(\mathbf{x} + \Delta\mathbf{x}) \approx C + \sum_i \nabla_i C \cdot \Delta\mathbf{x}_i$, and we want that to be zero. Moving along each particle's gradient, and moving lighter particles more, means

$$\Delta\mathbf{x}_i = w_i\,\nabla_i C\;\lambda, \qquad \lambda = -\frac{C}{\sum_j w_j\,|\nabla_j C|^2},$$

where $w_i = 1/m_i$ is the inverse mass. (Substitute the first formula into the linearised condition and solve for $\lambda$; you get the second.) For a distance constraint both gradients have length 1, so $\lambda = -C/(w_a + w_b)$, and the two particles move in opposite directions by amounts in the ratio of their inverse masses. That keeps their centre of mass where it was: like a spring, a constraint is internal and cannot push the system along. A fixed pivot has $w = 0$ and does not move at all; the whole correction goes to the bob.

// Enforce |xa - xb| = L. Returns lambda (kg m); lambda / h^2 is the force during this step.
function projectDistance(c) {
  const a = c.a, b = c.b;
  const d = V.sub(a.x, b.x);
  const len = V.len(d);
  const C = len - c.L;
  const wsum = a.w + b.w;
  if (wsum === 0 || len < 1e-12) return 0;
  const n = V.scale(d, 1 / len);              // grad C for a; -n for b
  const lambda = -C / wsum;
  a.x = V.addScaled(a.x, n,  a.w * lambda);
  b.x = V.addScaled(b.x, n, -b.w * lambda);
  return lambda;
}

For one constraint this lands exactly on $C = 0$ (the distance function is linear along $\mathbf{n}$), which is why the blue rod is exact. Notice what is missing: a stiffness. Nothing in the projection gets more dangerous as the rod gets "stiffer", because the rod is perfectly rigid by construction.

Use the predicted positions, not the old ones. All the projections must act on $\mathbf{x}^*$, and the velocity must be computed from the old position you saved before predicting. A common bug is to project the old positions, or to overwrite the saved position before computing $\mathbf{v}$; the pendulum then either ignores the constraint or stops dead.

Reading the force back out

The projection never computed a force, but it implies one. During a step, a constant force $\mathbf{F}$ would move a particle by $\tfrac12 h^2 \mathbf{F}/m$ more than its velocity alone, and in our update (velocity first, then position) by exactly $h^2 \mathbf{F}/m$. So the correction $\Delta\mathbf{x} = w\,\nabla C\,\lambda$ corresponds to a force

$$\mathbf{F}_c = \frac{\lambda}{h^2}\,\nabla C.$$

Compare with $\mathbf{F}_c = \lambda\,\nabla C$ above: the projection's $\lambda$ is the force multiplier times $h^2$. It has units of kg·m, and dividing by $h^2$ gives newtons. (The XPBD chapter keeps exactly this position-level $\lambda$ and builds on it.) The demo prints $-\lambda/h^2$ for the blue rod and, at each pass through the bottom, $mg + mv^2/L$ from the bob's current speed. At $h = 1$ ms the first pass reads 19.540 N for both. Even at 16 ms, where the step is crude, they agree to within 0.2 %: the projection is computing the tension, it just never needed to name it.

What projection costs: energy

Let the demo run. The red spring pendulum keeps swinging. The blue one, with its perfect rod, slowly dies down: at 16 ms it loses about 21 % of its energy in the first second, and after 10 s it swings to 22° instead of 60°. At 4 ms it loses 6 % in the first second, at 1 ms under 2 %. The loss per second is roughly proportional to $h$, so it vanishes as the step shrinks, but at large steps it is strong.

Where does it go? The predicted position is on the tangent line, slightly outside the circle, and the projection pulls it back along the radius. The resulting chord is shorter than the distance the bob "wanted" to travel, and the velocity recomputed from it is a little smaller each step. This numerical damping is a feature of every position-projection method. It makes them stable (energy can only leak out, never build up), and it is why they became popular in games, but it also means a position-based pendulum does not swing for long at 60 Hz. Two chapters ahead you will see the cure: many small steps instead of one big one (Small Steps), which drives the loss down without making anything stiff.

The period of a pendulum

For small swings, $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ and the pendulum is a harmonic oscillator with period

$$T_0 = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{L}{g}} = 2.006\ \text{s} \quad\text{for } L = 1\ \text{m}.$$

That formula is only the first term. The exact period for a release angle $\theta_0$ is $T = 4\sqrt{L/g}\;K\!\bigl(\sin(\theta_0/2)\bigr)$, where $K$ is the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, and its series starts

$$T = T_0\left(1 + \frac{\theta_0^2}{16} + \frac{11\,\theta_0^4}{3072} + \dots\right).$$

(A fast way to compute it exactly is $T = T_0 / \operatorname{AGM}(1, \cos(\theta_0/2))$, with the arithmetic-geometric mean; the demo does that.) At 10° the correction is 0.19 %; at 60° it is 7.3 %, giving $T = 2.153$ s; at 90° it is 18 %; near the top, at 170°, the period is 2.44 times $T_0$. Big swings are slow.

The demo times each pass through the bottom. The spring pendulum reads 2.155 s at 60° (its rod is slightly longer on average, so it is slightly slow). The projected pendulum's first period at $h = 1$ ms is 2.146 s, then the periods shorten as its amplitude decays, exactly as the large-angle formula says they should.

Unilateral constraints: ropes and floors

An inequality constraint $C \ge 0$ is enforced only when it is violated. If the rope's end is closer to the anchor than $L$, the rope is slack and does nothing; if it is further, project it back exactly as for a rod. In code it is one line at the top of projectDistance:

if (c.unilateral && C <= 0) return 0;   // shorter than L: slack, no force

Unilateral constraints can only pull (a rope) or only push (a floor), never both, so the multiplier has a sign restriction: the rope's tension is never negative. You have met one already. The ground collision of chapter 02, "if $y < 0$, set $y = 0$", is a projection onto $C = y \ge 0$.

Choose "rope vs rod" in the demo. Two bobs start at the bottom with the same sideways speed $v_0 = 5.86$ m/s, so $v_0^2 = 3.5\,gL$: enough to rise past the horizontal but not enough to loop. The rod (blue) can push as well as pull; its bob climbs to $\cos\theta = 1 - v_0^2/(2gL)$, that is 138.6°, and swings back. The rope (green) can only pull. Its tension $m g\cos\theta + m v^2/L$ reaches zero where $v^2 = -gL\cos\theta$; combining that with energy conservation gives

$$\cos\theta_\text{slack} = \frac{2gL - v_0^2}{3gL} = -\tfrac12, \qquad \theta_\text{slack} = 120°.$$

Beyond that angle the green bob leaves the circle and falls freely along a parabola inside it, until the rope snaps taut again. The demo reports the angle at which the rope first went slack. At $h = 1$ ms it reads 118.9° (and the rod reaches 136.1°); at 16 ms the numerical damping has already slowed the bobs, and the rope goes slack at 107°. When the rope snaps taut, the projection removes the bob's outward velocity entirely: a perfectly inelastic jerk, which is indeed how a rope behaves.

More than one constraint

Hang a second bob from the first and you have a double pendulum, two constraints sharing a particle. Project the first link and you move bob 1, which breaks the second link; project the second and you move bob 1 again, which breaks the first. A single pass no longer lands on $C = 0$ for both.

The simplest remedy is to loop: project every constraint in turn, and repeat. Each pass shrinks the errors, and after a handful of passes they are negligible. The demo uses 20 passes per step, which brings both link errors below $10^{-5}$ mm. This "keep cycling through the constraints" loop is a method with a name, Gauss-Seidel iteration, and the next chapter, Position-Based Dynamics, builds a complete simulator around it, along with its most important weakness.

The double pendulum is also famously chaotic: two releases that differ by a hair diverge completely within seconds. Do not expect your trajectory to match the reference solution's, or even your own after a small change; compare the link errors and the energy instead.

positions first

The recipe of this chapter, predict, project positions, derive velocities from displacements, is the skeleton of position-based dynamics, and it is also the skeleton of the PB-MPM paper. That paper's central quantity is each particle's displacement over the step, $\mathbf{d}_p = h\,\mathbf{v}_p$, which it iteratively corrects (through a grid) until the material's constraints are satisfied, and only then turns back into a velocity. The authors present their method as a position-based alternative to explicit and implicit MPM, for exactly the reason this chapter opened with: it does not have to resolve the stiffest wobble with a tiny time step. You will build it in Position-Based MPM.

Summary

  • A stiff spring trades error for time step: halving the stretch requires a stiffer spring and a smaller step, with no end.
  • A constraint is a function $C(\mathbf{x})$ that must be zero (bilateral) or non-negative (unilateral: floors, ropes). Its gradient is perpendicular to the allowed motions.
  • The ideal constraint force is $\lambda\nabla C$. It does no work, and $|\lambda|$ is the physical force: the tension in a rod.
  • Projection enforces a constraint without stiffness: predict, move positions along $\nabla C$ weighted by inverse mass, $\lambda = -C/\sum w_j|\nabla_j C|^2$, then set $\mathbf{v} = \Delta\mathbf{x}/h$. Fixed points have $w = 0$.
  • The projection's $\lambda$ divided by $h^2$ is the constraint force. Projection is exact in length but damps energy, with a loss that shrinks with $h$.
  • Several coupled constraints need repeated passes: the subject of the next chapter.
