Cable-Driven Bending

Pull a string on one side of a flexible spine and it curls. How far, how hard, and why never quite as far as you hoped.

A marionette's arm, a robotic tentacle, the neck of a puppet dinosaur and the flexible snake-like arms used in continuum robotics all work the same way. A flexible backbone carries a stack of small guides, like vertebrae, and a cable (a tendon) runs through holes in the guides a short distance to one side of the centreline. Pull the cable at the base and the backbone curls towards it. Put a second cable on the opposite side and you can curl it both ways from one knob, which is how many robot arms, animatronic puppets and toys are built.

The geometry is simple enough to fit on a napkin, and it gives a beautifully clean prediction: the bend angle is the cable pull divided by the cable's offset. Real devices miss that prediction, and the reasons are the interesting part: slack in the cables, friction in the sheath that brings them to the knob, and the fact that cables stretch.

In this chapter you will derive the cable-driven bending law, turn a tendon into a one-sided XPBD constraint on the tentacle from chapter 13, and then add slack, sheath friction and cable stretch, predicting each effect before you measure it.

Pull a cable, bend a spine

Take a section of backbone of length $L$ that bends into a circular arc through an angle $\theta$. Arcs of constant curvature are the standard model for these devices (Webster and Jones, Design and Kinematic Modeling of Constant Curvature Continuum Robots: A Review, 2010), and we will see in a moment why they are a good one. The centreline is an arc of radius $R = L/\theta$.

The cable runs through guides at a fixed offset $r$ from the centreline. On the inside of the bend it follows an arc of the same angle but radius $R - r$, so its length inside the section is

$$\ell_{\text{inside}} = (R - r)\,\theta = L - r\theta.$$

The cable got shorter by exactly $r\theta$, independently of the length of the section and of how the curvature is distributed (sum $r\,d\theta$ over any shape and you get $r$ times the total angle). Turn it around: if you pull the cable in by $u$ at the base, the section bends by

$$\theta = \frac{u}{r}.$$

With guides at $r = 15$ mm, a quarter turn ($\theta = 90^\circ = 1.571$ rad) needs $u = 23.6$ mm of cable. The cable on the opposite side, at offset $-r$, gets longer by the same $r\theta$. That is convenient: wrap the two cables in opposite directions around one pulley of radius $r_p$, and turning the knob by an angle $\phi$ pulls one in by $u = r_p\phi$ while letting the other out by the same amount. This is an antagonistic pair, and it gives

$$\theta = \frac{r_p}{r}\,\phi.$$

With $r_p = r$, the tip copies the knob: turn the knob 90° and the tentacle curls 90°. A smaller pulley trades range for precision.

Forces: a moment and no net push

Now the statics. With tension $T$ in the cable, cut the section anywhere and look at the part beyond the cut. The cable crosses the cut at distance $r$ from the centreline, pulling back with force $T$; the backbone crosses the cut at the centreline, pushing with force $T$ to balance it (it is in compression). The two forces are equal and opposite, offset by $r$: a pure bending moment

$$M = T\,r,$$

the same at every cut. A constant moment means, by $M = EI\kappa$ from chapter 12, a constant curvature: that is why a cable-driven section bends into a circular arc, as long as gravity and contact forces are small. The angle follows:

$$\theta = \kappa L = \frac{T r L}{EI}.$$

For the demo's tentacle ($L = 0.4$ m, $EI = 0.05$ N m2, $r = 15$ mm), a 90° bend takes $T = EI\theta/(rL) = 13$ N.

Notice what does not happen: the tentacle is not pulled towards the knob. The cable pulls its far end towards the base with force $T$, and the same cable pulls the base end (where it enters the sheath) away with the same $T$; the sheath carries that force back to the knob in compression. Cable and sheath form a closed loop of internal forces, exactly like a bicycle brake cable. Squeeze the lever and the brake closes, but the handlebar is not pulled towards the wheel, because the housing pushes back as hard as the inner cable pulls. This is a Bowden cable, and it is why the knob can sit anywhere you like, at the end of a long, winding sheath.

A tendon as a constraint

Now let's put cables into the tentacle of chapter 13: rigid segments with positions and angles, joined by rigid stretch-shear joints and compliant bend joints ($\alpha = l/EI$), base clamped. Give every segment $k$ a guide at offset $r$ along its normal, $\mathbf g_k = \mathbf x_k + r\,\mathbf n_k$ with $\mathbf n_k = (-\sin\theta_k, \cos\theta_k)$ for the left cable ($-r$ for the right). Guide 0 sits on the clamped base, where the cable leaves its sheath. The cable runs straight from guide to guide, so its length is the polyline length

$$\ell(\mathbf x, \boldsymbol\theta) = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} |\mathbf g_{k+1} - \mathbf g_k|.$$

The knob sets how much cable is available between the base and the tip: the straight length $\ell_0 = Nl$, minus the pull $u$. The constraint is

$$C = \ell - (\ell_0 - u) \;\le\; 0.$$

It is an inequality. A cable can be shorter than the available length (it is slack and does nothing), but it cannot be longer (it is taut and pulls). Cables pull; they never push. In XPBD that is one extra line: skip the constraint while $C \le 0$ and no tension has built up, and clamp the accumulated multiplier so that it never changes sign. With our sign convention a pulling cable has $\lambda < 0$ (it moves the guides in the direction that shortens it), so we clamp $\lambda \le 0$:

$$\lambda \leftarrow \min(0,\ \lambda + \Delta\lambda).$$

The gradient of a polyline's length with respect to one of its vertices is the difference of two unit vectors: the unit vector of the piece arriving at the vertex minus the unit vector of the piece leaving it. For an interior guide those nearly cancel and leave a small sideways pull towards the inside of the bend; for the last guide only the arriving vector is left, pointing back along the cable. Each guide is a point on a rigid segment at offset $\mathbf r_k = \mathbf g_k - \mathbf x_k$, so, as in chapter 13, it moves through both translation and rotation, and the constraint's generalised inverse mass sums over every guide it touches:

$$W = \sum_{k=1}^{N} \left( w_k\,|\nabla_k \ell|^2 + \frac{(\mathbf r_k \times \nabla_k \ell)^2}{I_k} \right), \qquad \Delta\lambda = \frac{-C - \tilde\alpha\lambda}{W + \tilde\alpha}.$$ 
// gradient of the polyline length: (unit vector arriving) - (unit vector leaving)
for (let k = 0; k < N; k++) {
  const dx = guideX[k + 1] - guideX[k], dy = guideY[k + 1] - guideY[k], d = Math.hypot(dx, dy);
  gradX[k + 1] += dx / d; gradY[k + 1] += dy / d;
  gradX[k] -= dx / d; gradY[k] -= dy / d;
}
// ... sum W over the guides, then:
let dlambda = (-C - alphaTilde * lambda) / (W + alphaTilde);
const newLambda = Math.min(0, lambda + dlambda);   // a cable can pull but not push
dlambda = newLambda - lambda;
lambda = newLambda;

This one constraint couples every segment of the tentacle, which is a little unusual: so far all our constraints touched two or three bodies. It costs $O(N)$ to evaluate, and it is what makes the tentacle bend as a whole instead of joint by joint. The force on the base (guide 0, infinite mass) is simply absorbed by the clamp, which is our simulation's version of the sheath pushing back.

Why the tip falls a little short

Even with no slack, no friction and a nearly rigid cable, the demo's tip reaches 87.2° when the knob says 90°. Two reasons, and both are worth knowing.

First, the guides are discrete. Between two guides the cable is a straight chord, not an arc. For two segments meeting at a joint with relative angle $\varphi$, the cable between their guides has length $l\cos(\varphi/2) - 2r\sin(\varphi/2)$ instead of the ideal $l - r\varphi$; it cuts the corner by an extra $l(1 - \cos(\varphi/2)) \approx l\varphi^2/8$. So the pulled cable shortens slightly more per radian than $r$ and the tentacle needs to bend less to use up the pull. Spread over $N$ joints, the relative shortfall is about $L\theta/(8N^2 r)$, which is 2 % for a 90° bend with 16 segments, and it shrinks quickly with more guides.

Second, the cable stretches. That is the subject of the last part of this chapter, and here it costs under 1 %. An exact solution of the discrete model (minimising its energy with both effects) gives 87.6°; the simulation, with its finite substeps, reaches 87.2°.

Slack: the dead band

Real cables are never cut to exactly the right length, and they bed in and stretch with use. Suppose each cable has an extra length $s$ of slack. Pulling the knob first takes up the slack and does nothing to the tentacle; only when $u > s$ does the cable go taut and start bending it. The tip angle becomes

$$\theta = \frac{\max(0,\ u - s)}{r},$$

and in knob angle the dead band is

$$\phi_{\text{dead}} = \frac{s}{r_p}.$$

With 4 mm of slack and a 10 mm pulley that is 0.4 rad, or 22.9°, on either side of zero: you can wiggle the knob through almost 46° without the tentacle noticing. In the demo the graph shows it as a flat step through the middle. Because each cable of the pair has its own slack, reversing direction always costs the full dead band, which is why cable-driven devices feel "loose" around the neutral position, and why the opposite cable goes visibly slack (dashed) whenever one side pulls.

The usual fix is pre-tension: tighten both cables against each other until neither is slack at neutral. The dead band disappears, at the price of a permanent compression of the backbone and, as we are about to see, a lot more friction.

Sheath friction: the capstan equation

The cable reaches the base through a sheath, and a sheath is rarely straight. Wherever it curves, the taut cable presses against its inside wall, and friction resists the cable sliding through. How much force is lost?

Look at a short piece of cable wrapped around a curve through a small angle $d\beta$, with tension $T$ on one side and $T + dT$ on the other. The two tensions are not quite parallel, so they add up to a force of about $T\,d\beta$ pressing the cable into the wall. Friction can then hold back at most $\mu\,T\,d\beta$, where $\mu$ is the friction coefficient between cable and sheath. While the cable slides, $dT = \mu\,T\,d\beta$, and integrating over the whole wrap angle $\beta$ (the total turning of the sheath, in radians) gives the capstan equation:

$$T_{\text{knob}} = T_{\text{base}}\; e^{\mu\beta}.$$

The force you must apply grows exponentially with the total wrap, not with the length of the sheath. With $\mu = 0.2$ and one full loop of sheath ($\beta = 2\pi$), the knob needs $e^{0.2 \times 2\pi} = 3.51$ times the force that reaches the tentacle; with two loops and $\mu = 0.3$ it needs 43 times as much. This is the same law that lets a sailor hold a heavy boat with a few turns of rope around a post. In the demo, the slider curl the base sets $\beta$, and the readout force at knob shows $T\,e^{\mu\beta}$.

Curling the tentacle itself adds wrap too: inside the body the cable turns through the bend angle $\theta$ against its guides, so the tension that arrives at the tip is lower again, by about $e^{-\mu_g\theta}$ for a guide friction $\mu_g$. The demo's guides are frictionless, but in a real device this is why a tentacle that is already curled is harder to curl further.

Force is not the only loss: travel

If the cable were perfectly rigid, friction would cost force but not motion: the knob would still move the cable end by $u$, only harder. Real cables stretch, and the tension inside the sheath is highest near the knob. Let the sheath contain a length $L_s$ of cable with axial stiffness $EA$, and spread the wrap evenly along it, so that the tension rises from $T_{\text{base}}$ at the base to $T_{\text{base}}\,e^{\mu\beta}$ at the knob. Adding up the stretch $T/(EA)$ along the sheath gives

$$\Delta\ell_{\text{sheath}} = \frac{T_{\text{base}}\,L_s}{EA}\cdot\frac{e^{\mu\beta} - 1}{\mu\beta}.$$

The second factor is 1 without friction and grows exponentially with it. For the tentacle, this stretch looks like a spring in series with the cable: a compliance

$$\alpha_{\text{cable}} = \frac{\ell_0 + L_s\, g(\mu\beta)}{EA}, \qquad g(a) = \frac{e^a - 1}{a},$$

counting the cable inside the body ($\ell_0$) and inside the sheath. That is exactly the $\alpha$ of the tendon constraint, so XPBD handles cable stretch and the loading part of sheath friction with no new machinery.

How much travel does it cost? The pull now has to cover the bend and the stretch: $u - s = r\theta + \alpha_{\text{cable}} T$, and the bend needs $T = EI\theta/(L r)$. Solving for $\theta$:

$$\theta = \frac{u - s}{r}\cdot\frac{1}{1 + \alpha_{\text{cable}}\,EI / (L r^2)}.$$

Read it as two springs in series, measured in cable length: the backbone, whose compliance seen from the cable is $Lr^2/EI$ (metres of cable per newton), and the cable, $\alpha_{\text{cable}}$. The pull divides between them in proportion. A stiff backbone, a small offset $r$, a long or thin cable and lots of sheath friction all make the cable's share larger, and the tip falls further behind the knob.

For the demo ($L_s = 1$ m of sheath), take a cable with $EA = 10^4$ N and use the bending length up to the last guide, $L_b = L - l/2 = 0.3875$ m, for $L$. The backbone's cable-space compliance is $0.3875 \times 0.015^2 / 0.05 = 1.74 \times 10^{-3}$ m/N. With no friction, $\alpha_{\text{cable}} = 1.4/10^4$ and the formula gives 83.3° for a 90° knob. With $\mu = 0.2$ and one loop, $g = 2.00$, $\alpha_{\text{cable}} = 2.4\times 10^{-4}$ m/N and the tip reaches 79.1°, while the knob needs 3.5 times the tension. With $\mu = 0.3$ and two loops, $g = 11.2$ and the tip manages only 54°. The simulation gives 81.8°, 78.0° and 54.0° (the small extra shortfall at the smaller angles is the chord effect from before).

Friction has a direction, and this model only knows one. We treated the sheath as a series spring whose stiffness depends on friction, which is right while you pull. When you let go, friction reverses: the tension near the knob drops first while the cable near the base stays stuck, and the tentacle lags behind the knob in the other direction. The tip angle then depends on whether you got there by pulling or by releasing: hysteresis. Modelling that needs the sheath to be simulated as cable segments with friction against it (chapter 16's friction, along a curve), or a state-dependent model that remembers the last direction of motion.

Solution

Compare with solution.js:

function cableLength(side) {
  computeGuides(side);
  let len = 0;
  for (let k = 0; k < N; k++) len += Math.hypot(guideX[k + 1] - guideX[k], guideY[k + 1] - guideY[k]);
  return len;
}

function solveCable(side, h) {
  const key = side > 0 ? 'left' : 'right';
  const C = cableLength(side) - allowedLength(side);
  if (C <= 0 && lambdaCable[key] === 0) return;
  gradX.fill(0); gradY.fill(0);
  for (let k = 0; k < N; k++) {
    const dx = guideX[k + 1] - guideX[k], dy = guideY[k + 1] - guideY[k];
    const d = Math.hypot(dx, dy);
    if (d < 1e-12) continue;
    gradX[k + 1] += dx / d; gradY[k + 1] += dy / d;
    gradX[k] -= dx / d; gradY[k] -= dy / d;
  }
  let W = 0;
  for (let k = 1; k <= N; k++) {
    const rx = guideX[k] - x[k], ry = guideY[k] - y[k];
    const rg = rx * gradY[k] - ry * gradX[k];
    W += w[k] * (gradX[k] * gradX[k] + gradY[k] * gradY[k]) + wI[k] * rg * rg;
  }
  const alphaTilde = cableCompliance() / (h * h);
  let dlambda = (-C - alphaTilde * lambdaCable[key]) / (W + alphaTilde);
  const newLambda = Math.min(0, lambdaCable[key] + dlambda);
  dlambda = newLambda - lambdaCable[key];
  lambdaCable[key] = newLambda;
  for (let k = 1; k <= N; k++) {
    const rx = guideX[k] - x[k], ry = guideY[k] - y[k];
    const rg = rx * gradY[k] - ry * gradX[k];
    x[k] += w[k] * gradX[k] * dlambda;
    y[k] += w[k] * gradY[k] * dlambda;
    th[k] += wI[k] * rg * dlambda;
  }
}

Summary

  • A cable at offset $r$ shortens by $r\theta$ when its section bends by $\theta$, so a pull $u$ bends it by $u/r$; a pulley of radius $r_p$ gives $\theta = (r_p/r)\,\phi$.
  • Tension $T$ produces a uniform moment $Tr$, so an unloaded section bends into an arc with $\theta = TrL/EI$. Cable and sheath form a closed loop of internal forces: no net pull on the body.
  • In simulation a tendon is a one-sided XPBD constraint on the length of the polyline through its guides: skip it when slack, clamp the accumulated multiplier to $\lambda \le 0$. One constraint couples every segment.
  • Slack $s$ gives a dead band of $s/r_p$ in knob angle.
  • Sheath friction multiplies the knob force by $e^{\mu\beta}$ (capstan equation). With a cable that stretches, it also costs travel: a series compliance $(\ell_0 + L_s g(\mu\beta))/EA$, sharing the pull with the backbone's $Lr^2/EI$.
  • Discrete guides make the cable cut corners, a few-percent effect that shrinks as $1/N^2$.
