Particles Meet Grids: PIC and FLIP

Two ways to send velocities through a grid and back, one that forgets too much and one that remembers too much.

Particles are wonderful at carrying material around. Each one keeps its mass, its colour and its history, and moving it is one line of code: $x \leftarrow x + h\,v$. Nothing smears and nothing is lost. But particles are awkward at everything that involves neighbours. Which particles are close to this one? What is the velocity gradient here? Where is the wall? Each question needs a neighbour search and a guess about how to weigh the answers.

A fixed grid is the opposite. Neighbours are the next index over, derivatives are differences, and a wall is a row of nodes whose velocity you clamp. But move material across a grid and it blurs, because every step averages values between cells. Hybrid particle-grid methods use both: particles carry the material, and every step they copy their mass and momentum onto a grid, let the grid compute the physics, and read the result back. The grid is scratch paper, wiped at the start of each step. The Material Point Method is one of these methods; so is the FLIP fluid solver used for most film water.

The weak point is the copying. Each transfer is an interpolation, and interpolation loses information. This chapter builds the two classic transfers, PIC and FLIP, and measures exactly what each one loses. By the end you will have working particle-to-grid and grid-to-particle code that the next five chapters build on, and you will know precisely which problem APIC was invented to solve.

One step of a hybrid method

Every hybrid step has the same four stages. Particles are numbered $p$ and grid nodes $i$, as in the papers. A particle has position $x_p$, velocity $v_p$ and mass $m_p$; a grid node sits at a fixed position $x_i$ and stores a mass $m_i$ and a velocity $v_i$ that are rebuilt every step.

  1. Particles to grid (P2G). Each particle spreads its mass and momentum over the nearby nodes.
  2. Grid update. Divide momentum by mass to get node velocities, then apply forces (gravity, pressure, and in MPM the elastic stress) and boundary conditions.
  3. Grid to particles (G2P). Each particle reads a new velocity from the nearby nodes.
  4. Advection. Each particle moves with its new velocity.

The grid stages are where the physics lives, and later chapters fill them in. This chapter keeps the physics deliberately thin, so that whatever goes wrong is the transfers' fault.

Interpolation weights

"Nearby nodes" and "spreads" need a precise meaning. We put a small bump-shaped function on each grid node, and a particle interacts with a node in proportion to the height of that node's bump at the particle's position. That height is the weight $w_{ip}$. In 2D the bump is a product of two 1D functions, one per axis:

$$w_{ip} = N\!\left(\frac{x_p - x_i}{\Delta x}\right) N\!\left(\frac{y_p - y_i}{\Delta x}\right),$$

where $\Delta x$ is the grid spacing and $N$ is a 1D kernel measured in cells. The simplest is the linear or tent kernel, $N(u) = 1 - |u|$ for $|u| \lt 1$ and 0 otherwise: each particle touches the 2 nearest nodes per axis, 4 in 2D. MPM prefers the quadratic B-spline:

$$N(u) = \begin{cases} \tfrac34 - u^2 & |u| \lt \tfrac12 \\[2pt] \tfrac12\left(\tfrac32 - |u|\right)^2 & \tfrac12 \le |u| \lt \tfrac32 \\[2pt] 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

It touches 3 nodes per axis, a 3×3 stencil of 9 nodes in 2D (27 in 3D). In code we never evaluate the piecewise formula directly. Instead, find the first node of the stencil and the particle's offset from it, in cells:

$$\text{base} = \left\lfloor \frac{x_p}{\Delta x} - \frac12 \right\rfloor, \qquad f = \frac{x_p}{\Delta x} - \text{base} \;\in\; [\tfrac12, \tfrac32).$$

The particle is then $f$, $f - 1$ and $f - 2$ cells from nodes base, base + 1 and base + 2, and the three weights are

$$w_0 = \tfrac12\left(\tfrac32 - f\right)^2, \qquad w_1 = \tfrac34 - (f - 1)^2, \qquad w_2 = \tfrac12\left(f - \tfrac12\right)^2.$$ 
// fx = x / dx - base, in [0.5, 1.5)
function quadraticWeights(fx, w) {
  w[0] = 0.5 * (1.5 - fx) * (1.5 - fx);
  w[1] = 0.75 - (fx - 1) * (fx - 1);
  w[2] = 0.5 * (fx - 0.5) * (fx - 0.5);
}

A worked example: a particle at $x_p/\Delta x = 10.3$ has base $= \lfloor 9.8 \rfloor = 9$ and $f = 1.3$, so its weights on nodes 9, 10 and 11 are 0.02, 0.66 and 0.32. Node 10 is the nearest (0.3 cells away) and gets the most. Three properties of these weights carry the rest of the chapter:

  • Partition of unity: $\sum_i w_{ip} = 1$ for every particle position (here $0.02 + 0.66 + 0.32 = 1$). Whatever a particle spreads, it spreads all of it.
  • Linear reproduction: $\sum_i w_{ip}\, x_i = x_p$. The weighted average of the node positions is the particle position ($9 \times 0.02 + 10 \times 0.66 + 11 \times 0.32 = 10.3$). Consequently any field that is linear in space is interpolated exactly.
  • Smoothness: the quadratic B-spline has a continuous first derivative (it is $C^1$); the tent does not.

MPM also needs the weight gradients $\nabla w_{ip}$, to compute stress forces and velocity gradients. Differentiate each factor with respect to the particle position: in cells, the three derivatives are $f - \tfrac32$, $-2(f - 1)$ and $f - \tfrac12$, each divided by $\Delta x$ to make them per metre, and

$$\nabla w_{ip} = \frac{1}{\Delta x}\begin{pmatrix} N'(u_x)\, N(u_y) \\ N(u_x)\, N'(u_y) \end{pmatrix}.$$

Because the weights always sum to 1, the gradients always sum to zero. The tent kernel's gradient jumps from $+1$ to $-1$ as a particle crosses a node, so its forces jump too; in MPM that causes the cell-crossing instability, which is why the quadratic (or cubic) B-spline is the standard choice. The transfers in this chapter do not need gradients yet, but your MPM code in chapter 25 will.

Nodes at corners or at centres. This site puts node $i$ at $x_i = i\,\Delta x$, as the MPM course notes do. The open-source PB-MPM code places its nodes at cell centres, $(i + \tfrac12)\Delta x$; its weight formula looks different by a half-cell shift, but the weights are the same functions. When you read other code, first find out where it thinks the nodes are.

Particles to grid

Mass first. Each particle gives node $i$ the fraction $w_{ip}$ of its mass:

$$m_i = \sum_p w_{ip}\, m_p.$$

By the partition of unity, $\sum_i m_i = \sum_p m_p$: no mass is created or lost. Momentum is spread the same way, and the node velocity is momentum divided by mass:

$$(m v)_i = \sum_p w_{ip}\, m_p v_p, \qquad v_i = \frac{(m v)_i}{m_i} \quad (m_i > 0).$$

Momentum is conserved for the same reason as mass. Nodes that no particle touches have $m_i = 0$ and are simply skipped: they are not part of the material this step. In code, P2G is a loop over particles that scatters into the 3×3 stencil:

for (let p = 0; p < count; p++) {
  const gx = px[p] * invDx, gy = py[p] * invDx;
  const bx = Math.floor(gx - 0.5), by = Math.floor(gy - 0.5);
  quadraticWeights(gx - bx, wx);
  quadraticWeights(gy - by, wy);
  for (let b = 0; b < 3; b++) for (let a = 0; a < 3; a++) {
    const w = wx[a] * wy[b], node = (bx + a) + (by + b) * nodesX;
    gm[node]  += w * pm[p];
    gvx[node] += w * pm[p] * pvx[p];       // momentum for now
    gvy[node] += w * pm[p] * pvy[p];
  }
}
for (let i = 0; i < nodeCount; i++) if (gm[i] > 0) { gvx[i] /= gm[i]; gvy[i] /= gm[i]; }

All data lives in flat Float64Arrays: particle $p$'s position is px[p], py[p], and node $(i, j)$ is stored at index i + j * nodesX. This layout ports directly to C++, C# or a GPU buffer.

The grid update

On the grid, forces are easy. A node with mass $m_i$ and force $f_i$ updates its velocity with a symplectic Euler step, $\tilde v_i = v_i + h\, f_i / m_i$; the tilde marks the velocity after the grid update, as in the APIC paper. Gravity is $f_i = m_i g$. Boundaries are just as easy: for the nodes within two cells of a wall, remove any velocity component pointing into the wall. Because every particle will read its velocity from these nodes, no particle needs to know where the wall is.

The demo uses two force fields. The rotating disk is held together by a pull toward the centre, $\tilde v_i = v_i - h\,\omega^2 (x_i - c)$. A disk spinning rigidly at $\omega$ needs exactly this centripetal acceleration, and a real disk would get it from its internal stress; supplying it from outside keeps the disk spinning rigidly forever in the exact solution, with no torque, so any loss of angular momentum is caused by the transfers. The blob scene uses a simple pressure: a node packed denser than the rest density pushes outward, $p_i = K\max(0, \rho_i/\rho_0 - 1)$ with $\rho_i = m_i/\Delta x^2$, and the force is the pressure gradient by central differences. It is a stand-in for the real material forces of chapter 25.

Grid to particles: PIC

The original Particle-In-Cell method (PIC, Harlow 1964) reads the new particle velocity straight off the grid, with the same weights:

$$v_p \leftarrow \sum_i w_{ip}\, \tilde v_i.$$

It conserves linear momentum too: $\sum_p m_p v_p = \sum_i \tilde v_i \sum_p w_{ip} m_p = \sum_i m_i \tilde v_i$. Then every particle moves, $x_p \leftarrow x_p + h\,v_p$, and the step is done. Try it in the demo with "PIC" and the rotating disk.

PIC forgets

The PIC disk slows down. Nothing in the physics slows it: the exact solution spins forever. Yet the orange spoke lags, the plot of angular momentum $L$ falls, and after one revolution (252 steps) only 28 % of it is left. This is PIC's famous dissipation, and it makes PIC fluids look like honey and PIC sand look wet.

Where does the angular momentum go? Define it for the particles and for the grid (APIC paper, eq. 3):

$$L_P = \sum_p x_p \times m_p v_p, \qquad L_G = \sum_i x_i \times m_i v_i.$$

In 2D the cross product of two vectors is the number $a \times b = a_x b_y - a_y b_x$. P2G conserves angular momentum: substitute the definition of $m_i v_i$ and use linear reproduction,

$$L_G = \sum_p \Big(\sum_i w_{ip} x_i\Big) \times m_p v_p = \sum_p x_p \times m_p v_p = L_P.$$

G2P does not. Think of what a particle receives: nine different node velocities, describing how the velocity varies across its neighbourhood, including how that neighbourhood spins. It keeps only their average. For a rigid rotation the spin it throws away is easy to compute. The particle's stencil holds the angular momentum $\sum_i w_{ip}(x_i - x_p) \times m_p \tilde v_i$ relative to the particle, and with $\tilde v_i = \omega \times (x_i - c)$ this is $m_p\,\omega \sum_i w_{ip}|x_i - x_p|^2 = m_p\,\omega\,\Delta x^2/2$ for the quadratic kernel (you will prove the $\Delta x^2/2$ in the next chapter). Summed over a disk of mass $M$ and radius $R$, whose angular momentum is $M R^2 \omega / 2$, the fraction lost per step is

$$\frac{M\omega\Delta x^2/2}{M R^2\omega/2} = \left(\frac{\Delta x}{R}\right)^2.$$

For the demo, $\Delta x = 1/64$ m and $R$ = 0.25 m, which predicts 0.39 % per step. The demo measures 0.38 % on the first step. Over 60 steps this compounds to a loss of 20 %, which is what the plot shows after one second.

PIC is a filter. The APIC paper's way of saying this: in PIC, all information passes through the grid. P2G followed by G2P averages each particle's velocity with its neighbours over a radius of about one and a half cells. That is a low-pass filter, and like any filter it removes detail: the part of the velocity that varies within a stencil, which includes rotation and shear. The upside is that it also removes noise, which is why PIC is very stable.

FLIP remembers

The Fluid-Implicit-Particle method (FLIP, Brackbill and Ruppel 1986) changes one line. Instead of replacing the particle's velocity with the grid's, it adds the change the grid made:

$$v_p \leftarrow v_p + \sum_i w_{ip}\left(\tilde v_i - v_i\right).$$

The grid velocity before the update, $v_i$, must be kept for this; the code copies it into gvxOld, gvyOld right after P2G. Intuitively, the grid only contributes what it computed (the effect of forces), and the particle keeps everything else it knew. With no forces at all, FLIP leaves particle velocities untouched.

Run the disk with FLIP: the angular momentum stays at 1.00000 indefinitely. That is not luck. The pull toward the centre is linear in position, so the grid's velocity change $-h\omega^2(x_i - c)$ is a linear field, and linear reproduction hands each particle exactly $-h\omega^2(x_p - c)$: every particle follows its exact circular orbit, and the grid never touches anything else.

The price: noise, and the ringing instability

Count degrees of freedom. The disk has 3,228 particles, each with a 2D velocity, sitting on about 800 grid nodes, also with a 2D velocity each. P2G squeezes about 6,500 numbers into about 1,600. Whatever velocity patterns the particles hold, the grid can only see a quarter of them; the rest are averaged away. A simple example is two particles in the same spot with opposite velocities: the grid sees zero momentum there. Patterns like this, which P2G maps to zero, are the transfer's null space.

PIC wipes the null space at every G2P, because it rebuilds particle velocities entirely from the grid. FLIP never does: a null-space pattern produces no grid velocity, so no grid force acts on it, so FLIP keeps it forever. Meanwhile the particles move with those velocities, so the pattern becomes position noise: particles bunch into clumps and leave gaps, with no restoring force. This is the ringing instability (Brackbill 1988). It exists in PIC too, because particles moving through the grid recreate it each step, but in PIC it is filtered at once.

Try it. Press "add particle noise", which adds a random 0.3 m/s velocity to every particle. With PIC, one step later 98 % of the added kinetic energy has gone: the grid simply could not see it. With FLIP all of it remains. Then switch the colouring to "velocity the grid cannot see", which shows $|v_p - \sum_i w_{ip}\tilde v_i|$, the difference between each particle's velocity and what the grid would have given it.

Now switch to "blob into wall": a blob flies into the right wall at 2 m/s and the pressure makes it bounce. With PIC it hits the wall, flattens into thin streaks of particles along it and drifts back slowly, having lost three quarters of its kinetic energy within half a second. With FLIP it rebounds with more than twice as much energy, but the hidden velocities reach several metres per second, and within a second the blob has burst into a spray of particles that fills the box. Neither looks like a real splash. (This blob has no surface tension and no elasticity, so even a perfect transfer would let it spread out; the difference between the transfers is in how it spreads.)

FLIP/PIC blends

The practical compromise, popularised by Zhu and Bridson (2005), mixes the two:

$$v_p \leftarrow \alpha\left(v_p + \sum_i w_{ip}(\tilde v_i - v_i)\right) + (1 - \alpha)\sum_i w_{ip}\,\tilde v_i,$$

with $\alpha$ usually between 0.95 and 0.99. The small PIC fraction filters some of the null-space noise each step, at the cost of a little dissipation: in the disk, the blend with $\alpha$ = 0.95 loses 0.019 % per step, almost exactly 5 % of PIC's 0.38 %. It is an engineering trade, and the right $\alpha$ depends on the scene, which is unsatisfying. You choose between a lively simulation with noise and a calm one that looks too viscous. The next chapter shows there is a third option that keeps PIC's filtering without its dissipation.

Solution

The per-step loss is set by the stencil size relative to the body, $(\Delta x/R)^2$, so it falls by a factor of 4 each time the grid is refined: the first-step losses we measured were 5.3 %, 1.46 %, 0.378 % and 0.096 % (predictions 6.25 %, 1.56 %, 0.39 %, 0.098 %; the coarsest grid deviates because only eight cells span the radius). Particles per cell do not enter: after 60 steps on the 64 grid, $L/L_0$ was 0.802, 0.800 and 0.800 for 1, 4 and 9 particles per cell. Refining the grid is therefore an expensive cure: in 2D, halving $\Delta x$ quadruples the nodes and the particles. Note also that the loss is per step, so smaller time steps make it worse per second.

APIC, sections 1 to 5.1

The first half of Jiang et al.'s APIC paper is this chapter.

  • Section 1 sets out the dilemma: PIC is stable but dissipative, FLIP is lively but noisy and sometimes unstable, blends need per-scene tuning. It names the ringing instability, caused by having more particles than grid nodes, and argues that it is worse in FLIP because PIC's transfer is "a true filtering" of it.
  • Section 3 and Figure 7 give the dataflow view: in PIC all information goes through the grid; FLIP adds a direct particle-to-particle path, which the paper calls unsafe. The key observation is that a particle receives data from many nodes but keeps a single constant velocity.
  • Section 4 is the notation. Superscript $n$ means "at the start of step $n$": $x_p^n$, $v_p^n$, and $v_i^n$ is the grid velocity right after P2G. The velocity after the grid update is $\tilde v_i^{n+1}$, our $\tilde v_i$. Weights are $w_{ip}^n = N(x_p^n - x_i)$, with the cell size folded into $N$.
  • Section 5.1: eq. (1) is our P2G (mass and momentum), eq. (2) is PIC's G2P, and eq. (3) defines the particle and grid angular momenta $L_P$ and $L_G$. The paper states that P2G conserves angular momentum and G2P does not; you have now measured it. FLIP's update is not numbered in the paper; in its notation it is $v_p^{n+1} = v_p^n + \sum_i w_{ip}^n(\tilde v_i^{n+1} - v_i^n)$.

Two practical remarks from section 10.5 apply to your code already. Step 2 says to skip nodes whose mass is exactly zero, without a small threshold, because a threshold quietly breaks momentum conservation; our gm[i] > 0 test does this. And step 8 notes that with FLIP the particle position should strictly be advected with the grid, $x_p = \sum_i w_{ip} x_i^{n+1}$, while with PIC and APIC that is the same as $x_p + h v_p$.

Summary

  • Hybrid methods carry material on particles and compute physics on a grid that is rebuilt every step: P2G, grid update, G2P, advect.
  • Quadratic B-spline weights: base $= \lfloor x/\Delta x - \tfrac12\rfloor$, $f = x/\Delta x$ - base, weights $\tfrac12(\tfrac32 - f)^2$, $\tfrac34 - (f-1)^2$, $\tfrac12(f - \tfrac12)^2$. They sum to 1, reproduce linear fields, and are $C^1$.
  • P2G conserves mass, momentum and angular momentum. Empty nodes are skipped.
  • PIC ($v_p = \sum w_{ip}\tilde v_i$) is a filter: stable, but it throws away each particle's local spin and shear, losing about $(\Delta x/R)^2$ of a rotating body's angular momentum per step.
  • FLIP ($v_p \mathrel{+}= \sum w_{ip}(\tilde v_i - v_i)$) loses almost nothing, but keeps velocity patterns the grid cannot see, which become noise and the ringing instability.
  • Blends trade one problem for the other with a tuning knob.
