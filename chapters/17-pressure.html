Pressure and Inflated Bodies

Fill a loop of particles with gas, and it becomes a balloon, a beach ball or a tyre.

A beach ball is almost nothing: a thin plastic skin that, empty, folds flat into a pocket. Filled with air it becomes a firm ball that bounces, rolls and can be squeezed and spring back. All of that firmness comes from the gas inside pushing outwards, everywhere, all the time, and from the skin holding it in. Air mattresses, bouncy castles, car tyres, inflatable boats and party balloons all work the same way.

In this chapter you will build one: a closed loop of particles (the skin), held together by the distance constraints you know, and filled with a gas. You will model the gas two ways, as an ideal gas whose pressure rises when it is squeezed, and as a single area constraint that keeps the inside at a fixed size, and you will find that the second one's Lagrange multiplier is the pressure. Along the way you will derive and check Laplace's law, the relation between pressure, size and wall tension that explains why big balloons need strong skins.

Pressure is a force per area, along the normal

A gas at pressure $P$ pushes on every bit of the surface that contains it, with a force proportional to the area of that bit and directed along its outward normal. In 3D, $P$ is a force per area, in pascals (N/m2). Atmospheric pressure is about 101 kPa; a car tyre runs at about 220 kPa above that; a party balloon is only a few kPa above the air around it.

We will work in 2D, so the "surface" is a closed outline and its "area" is a length of wall. Think of the 2D balloon as a cross-section of a long sausage-shaped balloon, a slice 1 m deep. Then pressure in our simulation is a force per length of wall, in N/m (numerically, pascals times one metre of depth), the enclosed area $A$ is in m2, and the tension in the wall is a force, in newtons. Everything in this chapter carries over to 3D by replacing edges with triangles and area with volume.

The force on one edge

Number the $N$ particles of the outline counter-clockwise. The edge from particle $i$ to particle $j = i + 1$ has the vector $\mathbf{e} = \mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_i = (e_x, e_y)$ and length $L = |\mathbf{e}|$. For a counter-clockwise outline the outward normal is the edge direction turned clockwise, $\mathbf{n} = (e_y, -e_x)/L$. The pressure force on the edge is

$$\mathbf{F} = P\,L\,\mathbf{n} = P\,(e_y,\, -e_x).$$

The length cancels: the force is the pressure times the edge vector rotated by a right angle. Split it equally between the edge's two particles (the pressure is uniform along the edge, so each end carries half), and add it to their velocities like any other force:

for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) {
  const j = (i + 1) % N;
  const ex = x[j] - x[i], ey = y[j] - y[i];
  const fx = 0.5 * P * ey, fy = -0.5 * P * ex;    // half of P * L * n for each end
  vx[i] += h * w[i] * fx;  vy[i] += h * w[i] * fy;
  vx[j] += h * w[j] * fx;  vy[j] += h * w[j] * fy;
}

If your outline happens to be clockwise, this pushes inwards and the balloon implodes. Check the sign of the area (below) once at the start.

Where the pressure comes from: the ideal gas

For a fixed amount of gas at a fixed temperature, the ideal gas law $PV = nRT$ says that pressure times volume stays constant ($n$ is the amount of gas in moles, $R$ the gas constant, $T$ the temperature). In 2D the volume is the enclosed area, so

$$P\,A = \text{const} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad P = \frac{P_0 A_0}{A}.$$

We fix the constant from the initial state, pressure $P_0$ at area $A_0$, and recompute $P$ from the current area every substep. Squeeze the balloon to 90 % of its area and the pressure rises by 11 %; let it expand and the pressure falls. That feedback is the springiness of every inflatable.

The area of a polygon comes from the shoelace formula, so called because of the criss-cross pattern of the products:

$$A = \frac12\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}\big(x_i\,y_{i+1} - x_{i+1}\,y_i\big), \qquad \text{indices modulo } N.$$

It is positive for a counter-clockwise outline and negative for a clockwise one. Each term is twice the signed area of the triangle formed by the origin and one edge; the triangles outside the shape cancel against each other, whatever the shape and wherever the origin is.

Two simplifications to know about. First, we treat $P$ as the pressure difference across the wall and let it obey $PA = \text{const}$, as if the balloon sat in a vacuum. A real balloon's absolute pressure obeys the gas law, and the air outside pushes back with atmospheric pressure, so the difference changes by a larger fraction when squeezed: a balloon at 105 kPa absolute, squeezed to 90 % of its volume, goes to 116.7 kPa, so the difference grows from 4 to 15.7 kPa. Model this by using $P_{\text{abs}} = nRT/A$ and applying $P_{\text{abs}} - P_{\text{atm}}$ to the wall. Second, fast squeezing heats a gas; $PV^\gamma = \text{const}$ with $\gamma = 1.4$ for air (the adiabatic law) is the better model for a ball hitting the ground.

Laplace's law: pressure, radius and tension

Cut a round balloon of radius $r$ in half along a diameter and look at the forces on one half. The gas pushes on it along every bit of the curved wall, but by symmetry only the component perpendicular to the cut survives, and it adds up to the pressure times the length of the cut, $P \cdot 2r$. The wall, cut at its two ends, pulls back with its tension $T$ at each end, $2T$. In equilibrium the two balance:

$$2T = 2rP \quad\Longrightarrow\quad T = P\,r.$$

This is Laplace's law for a cylinder (our 2D balloon is the cross-section of one). For a sphere, the same cut gives $\pi r^2 P$ balanced by $2\pi r T$, so $T = Pr/2$: a sphere holds twice the pressure of a cylinder of the same radius and wall. The law says something that surprises people: at the same pressure, a bigger balloon needs a proportionally stronger wall. That is why large gas tanks need thick walls, and why, in a long balloon being blown up, the part that is already widest carries the highest wall tension.

Laplace's law holds locally too: wherever the wall curves with radius $R$, the tension is $T = P R$. And our polygon obeys a discrete version exactly. Each particle receives half the pressure force of each of its two edges, and each edge's normal is tilted by $\pi/N$ from the particle's radial direction, so the outward push on a particle is $P\,L\cos(\pi/N)$. The two wall segments pull it inward with $2T\sin(\pi/N)$. With $L = 2r\sin(\pi/N)$:

$$T = P\,r\cos(\pi/N),$$

that is, $P$ times the polygon's inradius. For $N = 60$, $\cos(\pi/60) = 0.9986$.

The demo measures $T$ directly, as each wall constraint's XPBD multiplier divided by $h^2$, averaged over the 60 segments. Turn gravity off and compare it with "P x mean radius": the ratio is 0.9986, in both gas models. With gravity on, it drops by a little under 1 %, and that is real too: where the balloon sits on the ground, the pressure pushes on the ground directly instead of on the wall's curvature, which carries the balloon's weight without wall tension.

The area constraint

An ideal gas is one way to keep a balloon inflated. Another is to declare that its area must not change, the right model for a water balloon or anything filled with a nearly incompressible fluid. That is a single constraint on all $N$ particles at once:

$$C(\mathbf{x}_0, \dots, \mathbf{x}_{N-1}) = A - A_0.$$

Its gradient

The XPBD update needs $\nabla_i C$, the gradient with respect to each particle. Only two terms of the shoelace sum contain $x_i$: the term $x_i y_{i+1}$ and, from the previous edge, $-x_i y_{i-1}$. Differentiating:

$$\frac{\partial A}{\partial x_i} = \tfrac12\,(y_{i+1} - y_{i-1}), \qquad \frac{\partial A}{\partial y_i} = \tfrac12\,(x_{i-1} - x_{i+1}).$$

Now look at what this vector is. $\tfrac12(y_{i+1} - y_{i-1},\, x_{i-1} - x_{i+1})$ is the chord from particle $i - 1$ to particle $i + 1$, turned clockwise and halved. Written edge by edge, it is half the sum of the two adjacent edges' "length times outward normal":

$$\nabla_i A = \tfrac12\big(L_{i-1}\mathbf{n}_{i-1} + L_i\mathbf{n}_i\big).$$

This makes sense geometrically (moving particle $i$ outward by $\delta$ along an edge's normal sweeps out a sliver of area $\tfrac12 L\delta$ from each of its two edges), and it has a remarkable consequence. Compare with the pressure force on particle $i$: half of $P L_{i-1}\mathbf{n}_{i-1}$ plus half of $P L_i\mathbf{n}_i$. It is exactly

$$\mathbf{F}_i = P\,\nabla_i A.$$

The pressure force is the pressure times the gradient of the area. And in XPBD, a constraint with gradient $\nabla C$ and multiplier $\lambda$ applies the force $\nabla C\,\lambda/h^2$. So when the area constraint holds a balloon inflated, its multiplier divided by $h^2$ is the pressure of the fluid inside: nothing else could produce forces of that shape. Pressure, in the language of the constraints chapter, is the Lagrange multiplier of incompressibility. This idea returns in every fluid solver you will meet.

The update

With the gradient in hand, the XPBD step is the familiar one, with a sum over all particles in the denominator:

$$\Delta\lambda = \frac{-C - \tilde\alpha\lambda}{\sum_i w_i|\nabla_i A|^2 + \tilde\alpha}, \qquad \Delta\mathbf{x}_i = w_i\,\nabla_i A\,\Delta\lambda, \qquad P = \lambda / h^2.$$ 
const C = polygonArea() - area0;
let denom = alphaTilde;
for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) {
  const next = (i + 1) % N, prev = (i + N - 1) % N;
  gradX[i] = 0.5 * (y[next] - y[prev]);
  gradY[i] = 0.5 * (x[prev] - x[next]);
  denom += w[i] * (gradX[i] * gradX[i] + gradY[i] * gradY[i]);
}
const dlambda = (-C - alphaTilde * lambda) / denom;
lambda += dlambda;
for (let i = 0; i < N; i++) { x[i] += w[i] * gradX[i] * dlambda; y[i] += w[i] * gradY[i] * dlambda; }

The compliance $\alpha$ (in m3/N in our 2D units) turns the constraint into a linear spring on the area: in equilibrium $\lambda/h^2 = -C/\alpha$, so the pressure rises by $1/\alpha$ for every square metre of squeeze, a bulk modulus $K = A_0/\alpha$. At the demo's default $\alpha = 10^{-9}$ and $A_0 = 0.282$ m2, $K = 2.8\times10^{8}$ N/m: effectively incompressible.

A stiff ring needs care. The wall and the gas push against each other with large, nearly balanced forces, and the multipliers must be rebuilt from zero every substep. The demo needs 20 passes over the wall and area constraints per substep (and 20 substeps) for the measured tension and pressure to converge. And the order matters: sweeping round the ring in one direction drags particles along the sweep a little every pass, and a pre-stressed ring slowly starts to spin, adding a centrifugal tension that spoils Laplace's law by several percent. Solving the even segments first and then the odd ones removes the bias. Convergence is slowest for small balloons with stiff walls: at the stiffest wall the demo allows and the smallest radius, the area constraint's pressure reads about 10 % low, while still balancing the measured tension exactly.

One more trap: the plunger in the demo moves at most 0.5 m/s. A plunger that jumps would push the top of the wall straight through the bottom (nothing stops the wall passing through itself), turning the balloon inside out. Its shoelace area is then negative, the ideal gas pressure $nRT/A$ turns negative, and the gas pulls the balloon in for good.

Squeezing: compressible against incompressible

Now squeeze. Lower the plunger from above the balloon to 0.45 m, three quarters of the balloon's 0.6 m height, with gravity off, and let it settle. The two models respond completely differently.

area $A/A_0$pressure $P$wall tension $T$
before the squeeze (both)1.000500 N/m150 N
ideal gas0.918544 N/m122 N
area constraint0.9971046 N/m235 N

The ideal gas gives way: the area drops by 8 % and the pressure rises by just enough to keep $PA$ fixed ($544 \times 0.918 = 500$). The incompressible balloon gives up only 0.3 % of its area (the plunger contact is solved after the area constraint, so it wins by a little), so it must change shape at nearly constant area, which means a longer perimeter (a circle is the shape with the shortest perimeter for its area), a more stretched wall, and a pressure that doubles.

Both obey Laplace's law with the local radius. Squeezed between two flat plates, the free sides of the balloon become half-circles spanning the gap, of radius 0.225 m. The readout "T / P" gives 0.2249 m for the ideal gas and 0.2251 m for the area constraint. Notice what this means for the ideal gas: squeezing it raised the pressure but lowered the wall tension, because the sides became more tightly curved.

The force on the plunger can be predicted the same way as the half-balloon cut above, this time cutting horizontally through the balloon's widest point. Above the cut, the gas pushes up with $P$ times the width $w$ of the balloon, the wall pulls down with $T$ on each side (it is vertical there), and the plunger pushes down with $F$:

$$F = P\,w - 2T,$$

which equals $P$ times the length of wall flattened against the plunger. The readouts give 121.1 N against 121.6 N for the ideal gas and 283.6 N against 284.6 N for the area constraint. With gravity on, the plunger force is less by roughly the weight of the upper half of the wall. A car tyre carries its load the same way: the load equals the pressure times the contact patch. A 1,500 kg car on four tyres at 220 kPa above atmospheric needs a patch of 1500 × 9.81 / 4 / 220,000 = 0.0167 m2, about 167 cm2, per tyre.

A soft ball

Press "drop from 1 m". The balloon falls, flattens against the ground, the pressure jumps (to about 1,400 N/m for the ideal gas, nearly three times its resting value) and the ball bounces back to roughly 90 % of the height it fell from, a little more with the area constraint. Nothing in the code describes a bounce: the kinetic energy goes into compressing the gas and stretching the wall, and comes back out. The only losses are the contacts, which remove the normal velocity of the few particles that hit the ground. Adding damping to the wall (or the adiabatic gas law with a little heat loss) would make the ball deader, like a slightly soft football.

pressure from the volume ratio in MPM

In MPM, every particle carries its own deformation gradient $\mathbf{F}_p$, and $J_p = \det\mathbf{F}_p$ is the ratio of its current volume to its initial volume $V_p^0$, the per-particle version of our $A/A_0$. An energy density that depends only on $J$ gives a stress that is a pure pressure, rising as $J$ falls below 1: our ideal gas made local, and the usual way MPM codes model weakly compressible fluids (the course notes' constitutive sections give you the tools to derive it). The PB-MPM paper takes the other route of this chapter for its liquids: instead of a pressure law, it nudges each particle's $J$ back towards 1 with a position-based correction, an area constraint per particle instead of per balloon. When you read their liquid update, look for the correction that is a multiple of the identity matrix, a purely hydrostatic push: that multiple plays the role of our multiplier, the pressure.

Summary

  • Pressure pushes along the outward normal with a force proportional to area; on a 2D edge, $\mathbf{F} = PL\mathbf{n} = P(e_y, -e_x)$, half to each end.
  • An ideal gas at fixed temperature keeps $PV$ (in 2D $PA$) constant, so pressure rises as the body is squeezed. The shoelace formula gives the area.
  • Laplace's law: wall tension $T = Pr$ for a cylinder (our 2D balloon), $Pr/2$ for a sphere, and $T = PR$ with the local radius of curvature anywhere. Bigger balloons need stronger walls.
  • The area constraint $C = A - A_0$ has gradient $\nabla_i A = \tfrac12(L_{i-1}\mathbf{n}_{i-1} + L_i\mathbf{n}_i)$, the same shape as the pressure force, so its multiplier divided by $h^2$ is the pressure.
  • Squeezed, an ideal gas gives up area; an incompressible fill changes shape instead, and its pressure rises much more.
  • The force on a flat plate pressing an inflated body is $P$ times the contact length: the tyre rule.
