Cosserat Rods

Give every piece of the rod an orientation, and twist, torque and attachments come for free.

The particle rod of the last chapter knows where its centreline is and nothing else. That is enough to bend it, but ask it a simple question, "which way is the top of the rod facing here?", and it has no answer. Paint a stripe along a real garden hose and twist one end: the stripe winds around the hose, and the hose itself has not moved. A centreline cannot see that. Neither can it tell you which way a gripper bolted to the tip is facing, or how much a sliding contact is trying to spin the rod about its own axis.

The fix is old. In 1909 the brothers Eugène and François Cosserat described a rod as a curve plus a frame of three perpendicular unit vectors attached to the material at every point. The frame turns as the rod bends and twists, and how fast it turns along the rod is the strain the rod resists. Simulation papers picked this up a century later: represent the rod as a chain of small rigid bodies, each with a position and an orientation, and connect neighbours with constraints.

In this chapter you will build that in 2D, where each segment has a position and one angle, and see exactly how the 3D version works with the quaternions of chapter 11. By the end you can write the two constraints of a Cosserat rod, compute their compliances from $EI$, $GJ$ and $EA$, give a rod a rest shape, and explain what an orientation buys you over a bare centreline.

A curve with a frame

Describe the rod by its centreline $\mathbf r(s)$, a point in space for each arc length $s$ along the rest rod, and by three unit vectors $\mathbf d_1(s), \mathbf d_2(s), \mathbf d_3(s)$, the directors. They form a right-handed orthonormal frame glued to the cross-section: $\mathbf d_3$ is the rod's own "forward" direction, perpendicular to the cross-section, and $\mathbf d_1, \mathbf d_2$ lie in the cross-section, like the stripe on the hose and a second stripe a quarter turn around. Equivalently, there is a rotation, written as a unit quaternion $q(s)$, that takes the world axes onto the directors: $\mathbf d_k = q\, \mathbf e_k\, \bar q$, with $\bar q$ the conjugate of $q$.

For an undeformed straight rod, $\mathbf d_3$ points along the centreline and the frame does not turn as you walk along it. Everything that can go wrong with a rod is a departure from that, and it comes in two kinds.

Stretch and shear. Compare the centreline's tangent $\mathbf r'(s) = d\mathbf r/ds$ with the director $\mathbf d_3$:

$$\boldsymbol\Gamma = \mathbf r' - \mathbf d_3.$$

Written in the material frame, the component of $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ along $\mathbf d_3$ is the stretch (the centreline got longer or shorter) and the two components along $\mathbf d_1, \mathbf d_2$ are shear (the cross-section is no longer perpendicular to the centreline). For a rod at rest, $\boldsymbol\Gamma = 0$.

Bend and twist. Walk along the rod and the frame turns. Just as an angular velocity $\boldsymbol\omega$ describes how a rigid body's axes turn in time, $\dot{\mathbf d}_k = \boldsymbol\omega \times \mathbf d_k$, the Darboux vector $\boldsymbol\Omega$ describes how the directors turn in space:

$$\mathbf d_k' = \boldsymbol\Omega \times \mathbf d_k, \qquad k = 1, 2, 3.$$

$\boldsymbol\Omega$ is a rotation rate per metre (1/m). Its components in the material frame have names: the components along $\mathbf d_1$ and $\mathbf d_2$ are the two curvatures $\kappa_1, \kappa_2$ (the frame tips over: bending), and the component along $\mathbf d_3$ is the twist $\tau$ (the frame spins about the rod's axis). In quaternion form, $\boldsymbol\Omega$ in material coordinates is the vector part of $2\,\bar q\, q'$, the spatial analogue of the familiar $\boldsymbol\omega = 2\,\bar q\,\dot q$ from chapter 11.

A rod need not be straight at rest. A hook, a coil spring or a pre-bent wire has a rest Darboux vector $\bar{\boldsymbol\Omega}(s)$, and the strain is the difference $\boldsymbol\Omega - \bar{\boldsymbol\Omega}$.

The stored energy is the natural generalisation of the last chapter, with one stiffness per strain component:

$$E = \frac12 \int_0^L \Big( GA\,(\Gamma_1^2 + \Gamma_2^2) + EA\,\Gamma_3^2 + EI_1 (\kappa_1 - \bar\kappa_1)^2 + EI_2 (\kappa_2 - \bar\kappa_2)^2 + GJ (\tau - \bar\tau)^2 \Big)\, ds.$$

$GA$ is the shear stiffness, $EA$ the axial stiffness, $EI_1, EI_2$ the bending stiffnesses about the two cross-section axes (equal for a round rod), and $GJ$ the torsional stiffness. For the 5 mm steel rod of the last chapter, $EA = 3.9 \times 10^6$ N and $GA = 1.5 \times 10^6$ N, against $EI = 6.1$ N m2 and $GJ = 4.7$ N m2. The first two are so large that, for a thin rod, stretch and shear are nearly constraints; the last three are the rod's character.

Rigid segments with orientations

To simulate it, chop the rod into $N$ short rigid segments of length $l = L/N$. Each segment $i$ is a small rigid body with a centre $\mathbf x_i$, an orientation $q_i$, a mass $m_i$ and an inertia. Its directors are the rotated axes of $q_i$. Two papers set out this approach for position-based solvers:

  • Kugelstadt and Schömer 2016, Position and Orientation Based Cosserat Rods (SCA), put particles at the segment ends and one quaternion per segment, and wrote a stretch-shear constraint coupling the two end particles with the segment's quaternion, and a bend-twist constraint between neighbouring quaternions.
  • Deul, Kugelstadt, Weiler and Bender 2018, Direct Position-Based Solver for Stiff Rods (Computer Graphics Forum), made each segment a full rigid body and joined neighbours with zero-stretch joints plus bend-twist constraints, then solved the whole chain of joints directly (more on that below).

We follow the second layout, because it reuses the rigid bodies of chapter 11 unchanged. In 2D a segment's orientation is a single angle $\theta_i$, its director is $\mathbf d_i = (\cos\theta_i, \sin\theta_i)$, and its moment of inertia about its centre is that of a thin bar, $I_i = m_i l^2/12$. The state of the rod is $(x_i, y_i, \theta_i)$ for every segment: three numbers each, against two for a particle.

Play with it first. The orange tick on each segment is its normal director, so you can see orientations directly. Drag any segment, grab one near its end to twist it, raise the rest curvature to turn the rod into a hook or a coil, and tick whip to shake the base.

The stretch-shear constraint

In the continuous rod, $\boldsymbol\Gamma = 0$ says the centreline runs along $\mathbf d_3$. For rigid segments that means: the end of segment $i$ must meet the start of segment $i+1$. Segment $i$ ends at $\mathbf x_i + \tfrac l2 \mathbf d_i$ and segment $j = i+1$ starts at $\mathbf x_j - \tfrac l2 \mathbf d_j$, so

$$\mathbf C_{ij} = \left(\mathbf x_j - \tfrac l2 \mathbf d_j\right) - \left(\mathbf x_i + \tfrac l2 \mathbf d_i\right) = (\mathbf x_j - \mathbf x_i) - l\, \frac{\mathbf d_i + \mathbf d_j}{2}.$$

The second form shows it is the discrete $\boldsymbol\Gamma$ times $l$: the vector between neighbouring centres, compared with $l$ times the average director. This is the place where position and orientation are coupled. If a segment rotates while its neighbours stay put, its end points move and $\mathbf C$ grows; so the constraint can be satisfied by translating segments, by rotating them, or by both, and how it shares the work out depends on how hard each is to move.

The generalised inverse mass

That share is the generalised inverse mass of chapter 11. Here is the derivation in 2D, so you can do it yourself in the exercise. Take one attachment point $\mathbf p = \mathbf x + \mathbf r$ on a body, where $\mathbf r$ is the offset from the centre ($\tfrac l2 \mathbf d_i$ for the end of segment $i$). Push the body with a positional impulse $\mathbf p_\text{imp} = \Delta\lambda\, \mathbf n$ along a unit direction $\mathbf n$, applied at that point. The centre moves by $\Delta \mathbf x = \Delta\lambda\, \mathbf n / m$ and the body turns by $\Delta\theta = \Delta\lambda\,(\mathbf r \times \mathbf n)/I$, with the 2D cross product $\mathbf r \times \mathbf n = r_x n_y - r_y n_x$. A small rotation moves the point by $\Delta\theta\, \mathbf r^\perp$, and the part of that along $\mathbf n$ is $\Delta\theta\,(\mathbf r \times \mathbf n)$. So the point moves along $\mathbf n$ by

$$\Delta\mathbf p \cdot \mathbf n = \Delta\lambda \left( \frac1m + \frac{(\mathbf r \times \mathbf n)^2}{I} \right) = \Delta\lambda\, w, \qquad w = \frac1m + \frac{(\mathbf r \times \mathbf n)^2}{I}.$$

$w$ plays the part of $1/m$ for a particle: how far the attachment point gives per unit of impulse. A push straight through the centre ($\mathbf r \times \mathbf n = 0$) only translates; a push at right angles to the arm also turns the body, which makes the point easier to move.

Now the constraint. Measure the gap between the two attachment points, $C = |\mathbf C_{ij}|$, with unit direction $\mathbf n = \mathbf C_{ij}/C$. Exactly as for a distance constraint with zero rest length, with $w$ in place of the inverse masses:

$$\Delta\lambda = \frac{-C - \tilde\alpha\lambda}{w_i + w_j + \tilde\alpha}, \qquad \mathbf x_j \mathrel{+}= \frac{\Delta\lambda\,\mathbf n}{m_j}, \quad \theta_j \mathrel{+}= \frac{\mathbf r_j \times \Delta\lambda\mathbf n}{I_j},$$

and the opposite impulse on segment $i$. Thin rods are so stiff in stretch and shear that the demo makes this joint rigid, $\alpha = 0$, as Deul et al. do. If you want them soft, split the gap into two rows, along the average director and across it, with compliances $l/EA$ and $l/GA$: the same per-length rule as bending in the last chapter, because the joint stands for a length $l$ of rod.

const rax = 0.5 * l * Math.cos(th[i]), ray = 0.5 * l * Math.sin(th[i]);    // end of i
const rbx = -0.5 * l * Math.cos(th[j]), rby = -0.5 * l * Math.sin(th[j]);  // start of j
const dx = (x[j] + rbx) - (x[i] + rax), dy = (y[j] + rby) - (y[i] + ray);
const C = Math.hypot(dx, dy), nx = dx / C, ny = dy / C;
const rna = rax * ny - ray * nx, rnb = rbx * ny - rby * nx;
const wa = w[i] + wI[i] * rna * rna, wb = w[j] + wI[j] * rnb * rnb;   // generalised inverse masses

The bend constraint

In 2D the frame can only turn in the plane, so the Darboux vector has one component, the curvature, and between two segments it becomes the relative angle. The joint between $i$ and $j$ stands for a length $l$ of rod (half of each segment), so with rest curvature $\bar\kappa$:

$$C = (\theta_j - \theta_i) - \bar\kappa\, l, \qquad \alpha = \frac{l}{EI}, \qquad \nabla_{\theta_i} C = -1,\ \nabla_{\theta_j} C = 1.$$

The generalised inverse mass of a pure rotation constraint is $1/I_i + 1/I_j$, and the update is the scalar XPBD step. Notice two differences from the particle rod. There is no atan2: the angles are state, so we subtract them directly, and a coiled rod whose angle runs past $2\pi$ needs no special care. And the bend constraint touches no positions at all. It only turns segments; the stretch-shear joints turn those rotations into a curved centreline.

The clamp is a kinematic segment 0 (infinite mass and inertia). As in chapter 12 its half contributes no flexibility, so the first joint stands for only half a segment: $\alpha_0 = (l/2)/EI$, and its rest angle is $\bar\kappa\, l/2$. With that, the discrete rod has the same joint layout as the particle rod, and the same convergence: under self-weight its small-deflection tip is $wL^4/(8EI)\,(1 + 1/N^2)$.

Rest curvature

Set $\bar\kappa$ above zero and the rod's rest shape becomes a circular arc of radius $1/\bar\kappa$, the dashed grey line in the demo. With gravity off, every segment of the demo's 16-segment rod settles at $\theta_i = \bar\kappa\,(i - \tfrac12)\, l$: at $\bar\kappa = 6$ m-1 the last segment points at $6 \times (1 - 1/32)$ rad $= 333.0^\circ$, nearly a full turn. Gravity then pulls the coil open, more for a soft rod than for a stiff one. Change $\bar\kappa$ while the demo runs and the rod curls or uncurls in place, because rest shape is just a parameter of the constraint.

In 3D the rest Darboux vector has three components, and constant values give a helix. A rest curvature $\bar\kappa$ about $\mathbf d_1$ together with a rest twist $\bar\tau$ about $\mathbf d_3$ produces a helix of radius $\bar\kappa/(\bar\kappa^2 + \bar\tau^2)$ and pitch $2\pi\bar\tau/(\bar\kappa^2 + \bar\tau^2)$. A coil spring 10 mm in radius with 5 mm between turns needs $\bar\kappa = 99.4$ m-1 and $\bar\tau = 7.9$ m-1.

Bend and twist in 3D

In 3D the orientation of segment $i$ is a unit quaternion $q_i$. The rotation that takes frame $i$ to frame $j$ is the relative quaternion

$$\Delta q = \bar q_i\, q_j,$$

because $q_i\, \Delta q = q_j$. This is the discrete Darboux vector's raw material: over a length $l$ the frame turned by $\Delta q$, so the average Darboux vector is the rotation vector of $\Delta q$ divided by $l$. If $\Delta q = (\cos\tfrac\phi2,\ \sin\tfrac\phi2\,\mathbf a)$ (a turn by angle $\phi$ about unit axis $\mathbf a$), then

$$\boldsymbol\Omega_{ij} = \frac{\phi\,\mathbf a}{l} \approx \frac{2}{l}\, \operatorname{Im}(\Delta q) \quad\text{for small turns.}$$

Kugelstadt and Schömer use the approximation on the right. The bend-twist constraint has three rows, the three components of

$$\mathbf C = \boldsymbol\Omega_{ij} - \bar{\boldsymbol\Omega}_{ij},$$

each with its own compliance: $l/EI_1$ and $l/EI_2$ for the two bending rows, $l/GJ$ for the twist row. One detail from chapter 11: $q$ and $-q$ are the same rotation, so before comparing with the rest value, flip the sign of $\Delta q$ if its scalar part is negative, so that it describes the short way round.

Two properties make this the right strain, and both are worth understanding.

It is objective. A strain must not change when you pick up the whole rod and rotate it rigidly, or a rod would resist being carried around. Rotate everything by $R$: $q_i \to R q_i$ and $q_j \to R q_j$, and

$$\overline{(R q_i)}\,(R q_j) = \bar q_i\, \bar R R\, q_j = \bar q_i\, q_j.$$

The relative rotation is unchanged, so the strain is too. A strain built from world-space quantities, such as $\mathbf d_{3,j} - \mathbf d_{3,i}$, would change under rotation and would need to be expressed in some frame first.

It is expressed in the mid frame. The components of $\boldsymbol\Omega$ only mean "bend about $\mathbf d_1$" or "twist about $\mathbf d_3$" in a particular frame, and between two segments there are two candidates, frame $i$ and frame $j$. The joint sits between them, so the honest choice is the frame halfway between, $q_i\,(\Delta q)^{1/2}$. Many plausible strain measures give different numbers in frame $i$ and frame $j$ (try projecting $\mathbf d_{3,j} - \mathbf d_{3,i}$ onto each), and a constraint built on one of them is lopsided: its twist row measures twist about $\mathbf d_{3,i}$ rather than about the rod's axis at the joint, and the result depends on which end you numbered from. The rotation vector of $\Delta q$ avoids the problem. A rotation leaves its own axis unchanged, so $\mathbf a$ has the same components in frame $i$, in frame $j$ and in the mid frame: reading off $\operatorname{Im}(\bar q_i q_j)$ (whose direction is $\mathbf a$) gives the strain in the mid frame for free, and rows of different stiffness ($EI$ against $GJ$) act symmetrically on the two segments. That matters for rods with $EI_1 \ne EI_2$ (a ribbon, a flat cable) and rods where $GJ$ and $EI$ differ a lot.

The update is the angular version of what you have done in 2D. For a row along unit axis $\mathbf a$ (in world space), the generalised inverse mass is $\mathbf a^T \mathbf I_i^{-1} \mathbf a + \mathbf a^T \mathbf I_j^{-1}\mathbf a$ with the world-space inverse inertia tensors, the angular correction of segment $j$ is $\Delta\boldsymbol\theta_j = \mathbf I_j^{-1}\mathbf a\,\Delta\lambda$ (and the opposite for $i$), and each quaternion is updated with $q \leftarrow \operatorname{normalise}\big(q + \tfrac12 (0, \Delta\boldsymbol\theta)\, q\big)$, as in chapter 11.

What orientations buy you

All of this costs a quaternion per segment and three extra constraint rows per joint. In return:

  • Twist exists. A centreline-only rod cannot store twist, so it cannot form the loops of a twisted hose, cannot transmit torque along its length (turn the handle of a flexible shaft and nothing happens at the far end), and cannot show the twist-bend instability of the last chapter.
  • Contacts can apply torque about the axis. When a rotating rod slides against a wall, friction acts at its surface, a radius away from the centreline. That force has a moment about $\mathbf d_3$ that spins the rod. With an orientation per segment, the contact is a point on a rigid body, exactly as in chapter 11, and the torque is automatic.
  • Things can be attached to the frame. A gripper at the tip, a sensor, a fin, the next link of a robot, a second rod welded on at an angle: anything that must point where the rod points is a rigid attachment to one segment's frame. With a centreline only, "where the rod points" is ambiguous about the axis.
  • Rigid-body machinery reuses directly. Joints, contacts, drag and coupling to other rigid bodies are all the same constraint between two points on two bodies. The mouse drag in the demo is the stretch-shear joint with the world on one side.
  • Rest shapes are local. A pre-curved, pre-twisted rod is a rest Darboux vector per joint, independent of where the rod is in space.

Light rotations make stiff joints harder to converge. A thin segment's moment of inertia, $ml^2/12$, is small, so a bend joint acting only on rotations oscillates fast: $\omega = \sqrt{(1/I_i + 1/I_j)/\alpha} = \sqrt{24\,EI/(\mu l^4)}$, twice as fast as the particle joint of chapter 12. The demo picks its substeps to keep $h\,\omega \le 0.5$ for the stiffest joint, which is 133 substeps per frame at its defaults (16 segments, $EI = 0.5$ N m2). Some simulators inflate the rotational inertia to make this cheaper; it does not change the rest shape, but it slows down every rotational motion, so use it knowingly.

This cost is the problem Deul et al. set out to remove. Their observation is that a rod's joints form a chain (or, for branching rods, a tree), so the linear system of all joint constraints together is block-tridiagonal and can be solved exactly in time proportional to the number of joints, with the tree solve of chapter 10. One direct solve per iteration propagates a correction through the whole rod at once, instead of one joint per Gauss-Seidel sweep, and stiff rods with hundreds of segments converge in a few iterations. If you build a production rod solver, that is the version to build.

Discrete elastic rods, briefly

Cosserat rods are not the only way to give a rod twist. Discrete Elastic Rods (Bergou, Wardetzky, Robinson, Audoly and Grinspun, SIGGRAPH 2008) keep the centreline as the main unknown: particles at the nodes, plus a single twist angle per edge. The frame is not stored but rebuilt each step by parallel transport along the centreline (a frame moved along a curve without any spin about the tangent, the Bishop frame), and the twist angle says how far the material frame has turned relative to it. The curvature at a node is the discrete curvature binormal $2\,\mathbf e_{i-1}\times\mathbf e_i / (|\mathbf e_{i-1}||\mathbf e_i| + \mathbf e_{i-1}\cdot\mathbf e_i)$, built from the two edge vectors.

The trade-offs are roughly these. Discrete elastic rods have fewer unknowns (4 per node against 7 per segment), no shear at all by construction (the frame is always aligned with the edge), and a careful treatment of geometry that makes them a favourite for hair and for accurate engineering simulation, usually with implicit, force-based integrators. Cosserat rods in a position-based solver keep a full rigid body per segment, allow (very stiff) shear, and slot into the same constraint machinery as everything else in this course, which makes coupling, contact and attachments simple.

Solution

Compare with solution.js:

function solveStretchShear(i, h) {
  const j = i + 1;
  const rax = 0.5 * l * Math.cos(th[i]), ray = 0.5 * l * Math.sin(th[i]);
  const rbx = -0.5 * l * Math.cos(th[j]), rby = -0.5 * l * Math.sin(th[j]);
  const dx = (x[j] + rbx) - (x[i] + rax), dy = (y[j] + rby) - (y[i] + ray);
  const C = Math.hypot(dx, dy);
  if (C < 1e-12) return;
  const nx = dx / C, ny = dy / C;
  const rna = rax * ny - ray * nx, rnb = rbx * ny - rby * nx;
  const wa = w[i] + wI[i] * rna * rna, wb = w[j] + wI[j] * rnb * rnb;
  if (wa + wb === 0) return;
  const dlambda = -C / (wa + wb);
  lambdaJoint[i] += dlambda;
  const pxImp = dlambda * nx, pyImp = dlambda * ny;
  x[j] += w[j] * pxImp; y[j] += w[j] * pyImp; th[j] += wI[j] * (rbx * pyImp - rby * pxImp);
  x[i] -= w[i] * pxImp; y[i] -= w[i] * pyImp; th[i] -= wI[i] * (rax * pyImp - ray * pxImp);
}

Solution 
function solveBend(i, h) {
  const j = i + 1;
  const len = jointLength(i);
  const C = th[j] - th[i] - restCurvature.value * len;
  const alphaTilde = (len / bendStiffness.value) / (h * h);
  const wsum = wI[i] + wI[j];
  const dlambda = (-C - alphaTilde * lambdaBend[i]) / (wsum + alphaTilde);
  lambdaBend[i] += dlambda;
  th[i] -= wI[i] * dlambda;
  th[j] += wI[j] * dlambda;
}

Summary

  • A Cosserat rod is a centreline $\mathbf r(s)$ plus a material frame of directors $\mathbf d_1, \mathbf d_2, \mathbf d_3$, equivalently a quaternion $q(s)$.
  • Two strains: stretch-shear $\boldsymbol\Gamma = \mathbf r' - \mathbf d_3$ and bend-twist, the Darboux vector $\boldsymbol\Omega$ with $\mathbf d_k' = \boldsymbol\Omega\times\mathbf d_k$, compared with a rest value. The energy weights them by $GA$, $EA$, $EI_1$, $EI_2$ and $GJ$.
  • Discretised as rigid segments: a stretch-shear joint (end of one meets start of the next) that couples positions and orientations through the generalised inverse mass $1/m + (\mathbf r \times \mathbf n)^2/I$, and a bend-twist constraint on the relative rotation, with compliance (length represented)/(stiffness) per row.
  • In 3D the strain is the rotation vector of $\bar q_i q_j$ over $l$: objective because a common rotation cancels, and expressed in the mid frame because a rotation leaves its own axis alone.
  • Orientations give twist, torque transmission, contact torques about the axis, frame attachments and local rest shapes. Their cost is extra state and fast rotational modes, which direct (tree) solvers handle best.
