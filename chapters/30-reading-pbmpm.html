Reading: PB-MPM (2024)

A two-page talk and its source code, read side by side, line by line.

The Position Based MPM paper is two pages long. It has two algorithms, no numbered equations, and one paragraph of results. Every sentence carries weight, and several important details are only in the open-source code that accompanies it. That makes it a different kind of reading exercise from the APIC paper or the MPM course: here the skill is reconstructing a method from a terse description, checking it against an implementation, and noticing where the two disagree.

This chapter does that for you, and shows you how to do it yourself. We go through the talk in its own order, restate each line of Algorithms 1 and 2 as mathematics, reconstruct what the code actually does, and derive the parts the talk only asserts: why the liquid correction has the form it has, why the elastic target is a rotation, why the volume target divides by $\sqrt{\det\mathbf{F}}$ in 2D, and why the solver converges slowly. You have already built the method in chapter 27; this is about reading it.

the paper and the code

Chris Lewin. A Position Based Material Point Method. SIGGRAPH 2024 Talks, 2 pages. DOI 10.1145/3641233.3664323. The author is at SEED, Electronic Arts. Open-source 2D WebGPU code, BSD-3 licence: github.com/electronicarts/pbmpm. This chapter refers to the version released with the talk; file names below are from it.

What the paper is, and where it sits

The lineage runs from Particle-In-Cell (Harlow, 1964) through FLIP (Brackbill and Ruppel, 1986) to MPM for solids (Sulsky and co-authors, 1994-95), into graphics with FLIP liquids (Zhu and Bridson, 2005) and MPM snow (Stomakhin et al., 2013), then APIC transfers (2015), the MPM course (2016) and MLS-MPM (2018). All of these integrate with forces: explicitly, with a time step limited by stiffness, or implicitly, with Newton's method and its machinery. In parallel, real-time physics had gone a different way: Position-Based Dynamics (Müller et al., 2007) and XPBD (Macklin et al., 2016) replace forces by constraints that are projected iteratively, giving up accuracy for robustness.

PB-MPM puts the two lines together. The problem, in plain words: explicit MPM explodes unless the time step is tiny, and implicit MPM is a lot of work to implement and has its own failure modes. Real-time applications need a simulator that never explodes, whatever the user does, at a fixed cost per frame. PB-MPM keeps the MPM grid and transfers and replaces the force computation by a per-particle constraint projection, iterated through the grid like a PBD solver.

Abstract and section 1: Introduction

The abstract makes three claims; underline them, because the rest of the paper is evidence for them: the method is a "semi-implicit compliant constraint formulation" of MPM; it is stable at any time step; and it is as easy to implement as an explicit integrator. The introduction then sets up the problem in three paragraphs:

  1. Explicit MPM is easy but needs small steps; implicit MPM allows large steps but is complex, and has pitfalls such as keeping the stiffness matrix positive definite (the paper cites Smith et al. 2019; chapter 29, section 11.5, explains the issue).
  2. Real-time use needs stability under any input, including impossible situations such as a liquid crushed to zero volume between animated walls, and speed: many times faster than real time.
  3. PBD meets those needs by treating soft forces as relaxed rigid constraints, solved by local iterations rather than one global linearisation. The price is that apparent stiffness depends on how well the solver converges.

Then the thesis: an iterative constraint formulation of MPM, unconditionally stable, with explicit-like results at lower cost.

Read "semi-implicit" carefully. The constraints are evaluated on the deformation gradient the particle will have at the end of the step, which is the implicit part. But no system is assembled or linearised: each constraint is projected locally, and the projections are reconciled by iterating. That is the PBD sense of implicit, not the Newton sense of chapter 26.

Check your understanding

1. What does PBD give up in exchange for stability?

Control over stiffness. The stiffness you observe is whatever the solver reaches in the iterations it is given, so it changes with the time step and iteration count. XPBD restores a physical compliance for converged solves, but an unconverged solve still looks softer than intended.

Section 2: Details, and Algorithm 1

Section 2 says the method is "very similar to explicit MPM" and points to the MPM course for standard techniques. The idea in one sentence, as in Lagrangian PBD: iteratively improve a candidate displacement by solving local constraints and combining the results. Algorithm 1 shows the structure. Here it is as printed, in words:

UpdatePBMPM(P):
  for iteration = 1 .. iterationCount:
    P = SolveConstraints(P)        (red: new compared with explicit MPM)
    G = ParticleToGrid(P)
    G = GridUpdate(G)
    P = GridToParticle(P, G)
  P = IntegrateParticles(P)

Only the iteration loop and the constraint solve are marked as new. What the algorithm does not show is what the particles and grid store. The code fills that in.

Algorithm 1 reconstructed from the code

Each particle carries a position $\mathbf{x}_p$, a displacement $\mathbf{d}_p$ (where it will move this step), a deformation displacement $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p$ (how its neighbourhood will deform this step), a deformation gradient $\mathbf{F}_p$ (or, for liquids, just $J_p$), mass and material. One frame in sim.js is a loop over substeps, each of size $h$:

For each substep (emit new particles, then):

  1. For each iteration $k = 1, \dots, K$:
    1. Solve constraints (particleUpdatePBMPM): every particle, independently, changes its own $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p$ so that its constraint would be satisfied at the end of the step (Algorithm 2).
    2. Clear the grid (gridZero). Algorithm 1 leaves this out.
    3. P2G (particleToGrid), an APIC transfer of displacements: $$m_i = \sum_pw_{ip}m_p, \qquad m_i\mathbf{d}_i = \sum_pw_{ip}m_p\big(\mathbf{d}_p + \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)\big).$$
    4. Grid update (gridUpdate): $\mathbf{d}_i = (m\mathbf{d})_i/m_i$ (zero if $m_i$ is tiny), then collisions: if a node's displaced position $\mathbf{x}_i + \mathbf{d}_i$ would be inside a collider, remove the penetrating normal component of $\mathbf{d}_i$; nodes that would leave the domain get $\mathbf{d}_i = \mathbf{0}$. No forces, no gravity: the grid only averages and collides.
    5. G2P (gridToParticle), the APIC gather: $$\mathbf{d}_p = \sum_iw_{ip}\mathbf{d}_i, \qquad \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p = \frac{4}{\Delta x^2}\sum_iw_{ip}\mathbf{d}_i(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)^T.$$
  2. Integrate (particleIntegrate), once per substep:
    • liquids: $J_p \leftarrow (1 + \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p)J_p$, clamped below at 0.1;
    • other materials: $\mathbf{F}_p \leftarrow (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p)\mathbf{F}_p$, then singular values clamped to $[0.2, 10^4]$, then plasticity for sand and viscoplastic materials;
    • $\mathbf{x}_p \leftarrow \mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{d}_p$;
    • gravity for the next step: $\mathbf{d}_p \leftarrow \mathbf{d}_p + h^2\mathbf{g}$; mouse forces; push out of colliders; clamp to the domain.

Three things become clear only from this reconstruction.

It is APIC with displacements. Divide the P2G and G2P formulas by $h$: with $\mathbf{d} = h\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} = h\mathbf{C}$, they are the APIC transfers of chapter 28, equations (8) to (10), with the quadratic-spline $\mathbf{D}_p^{-1} = 4/\Delta x^2$. The update $\mathbf{F} \leftarrow (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}})\mathbf{F} = (\mathbf{I} + h\mathbf{C})\mathbf{F}$ is the MLS-MPM deformation update (chapter 29). So the talk's sentence "we used MLS-MPM with quadratic B-spline weighting" means exactly this.

The initial guess is inertia. Nothing resets $\mathbf{d}_p$ or $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p$ between substeps. Each substep starts from the previous one's final displacement plus gravity, $\mathbf{d}_p + h^2\mathbf{g}$, which is $h(\mathbf{v}_p + h\mathbf{g})$: the position the particle would reach with no internal forces. That is PBD's predicted position $\mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{x} + h\mathbf{v} + h^2\mathbf{g}$, written as a displacement. Without constraints the loop is symplectic Euler.

The last word belongs to the grid. Each iteration ends with G2P, so the $\mathbf{d}_p$ and $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p$ that are integrated are always grid-consistent. A particle's own projected $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p$ never moves it directly; it only acts through the grid. That is APIC's "true filter" property (chapter 28, section 3), and it is one reason the method does not develop FLIP-like noise.

Check your understanding

1. With iterationCount = 1, how many grid transfers does one PB-MPM substep do, compared with an explicit MPM step?

One P2G and one G2P, the same as explicit MPM. The constraint solve replaces explicit MPM's stress computation, at similar per-particle cost (one SVD for elastic particles). That is the basis of the talk's claim that one iteration costs roughly one explicit step.

2. If the constraint solve did nothing, what would PB-MPM simulate?

Free particles under gravity, with APIC transfers in between: a pressureless, stress-free material, like dust. The transfers would preserve affine motion, and collisions would still act through the grid.

Algorithm 2: the constraint solve, liquids

As printed:

if p.isLiquid:
  c       = tr(D)
  D_hydro = I (L - 1 - c)
  D_visc  = -(D - tr(D) I)
  D       = D + alpha_hydro D_hydro + alpha_visc D_visc

And as the code does it (particleUpdatePBMPM.wgsl), in 2D ($d = 2$):

$$\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} \leftarrow \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} - \alpha_{visc}\,\tfrac12\big(\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} + \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}\,T}\big),$$ $$a = \frac1d\Big(\frac1J - 1 - \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}\Big), \qquad \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} \leftarrow \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} + \alpha_{hydro}\,a\,\mathbf{I}.$$

Deriving the hydrostatic term

The talk says only that the hydrostatic impulse drives the tracked density towards 1. Here is where the formula comes from. A liquid particle tracks only its volume ratio $J$, and the integrator updates it with

$$J^{n+1} = (1 + \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}})\,J^n.$$

This is the first-order version of $\det(\mathbf{F}^{n+1}) = \det(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}})\det\mathbf{F}^n$: in 2D, exactly, $\det(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D}) = 1 + \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D} + \det\mathbf{D}$, and the dropped $\det\mathbf{D}$ is second order in the step ($\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} = h\nabla\mathbf{v}$). It is also the discrete form of $\dot J = J\,\nabla\cdot\mathbf{v}$, the course's equation (99).

Incompressibility says the particle should end the step at rest volume, $J^{n+1} = 1$. The correction is restricted to a pure pressure, a multiple of the identity $a\mathbf{I}$, because pressure acts equally in all directions. So require

$$\big(1 + \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} + a\mathbf{I})\big)J = 1 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad 1 + \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} + d\,a = \frac1J,$$

and solve: $a = \frac1d\big(\frac1J - 1 - \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}\big)$. That is the code's 0.5*(1.0/liquidDensity - tr(D) - 1.0) in 2D. With $L = 1/J$ (a density ratio) and $c = \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D}$, the paper's line 5 is the same expression without the $1/d$.

Two observations make this a good constraint:

  • It is linear in $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}$, because the integrator uses $1 + \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D}$ rather than the exact determinant. So the projection is exact in one shot: with $\alpha_{hydro} = 1$ the particle's own constraint is satisfied precisely. With the exact determinant you would solve a quadratic.
  • The factor $1/d$ matters. Without it (the paper's line 5 with $\alpha_{hydro} = 1$), the trace changes by $d$ times too much. In 2D the volume error flips sign instead of vanishing: a particle at $J = 0.93$ with $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D} = 0.015$ would end the step 5.6 % too small without correction and 5.6 % too large with the literal formula. Read the paper's $\alpha_{hydro}$ as including the $1/d$, or use the code's form.

The viscosity term, three versions

Here the text, the algorithm and the code disagree, and it is worth seeing why. The text says viscosity drives the off-diagonal elements of $\mathbf{D}$ towards zero. Algorithm 2 writes $-(\mathbf{D} - \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{D})\mathbf{I})$, which looks like a deviatoric part with the $1/d$ missing again. The code subtracts the symmetric part, $\tfrac12(\mathbf{D} + \mathbf{D}^T)$.

Split $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} = h\nabla\mathbf{v}$ into its pieces: the trace (volume change), the symmetric traceless part (shearing strain rate), and the antisymmetric part (spin). Viscosity should damp the shear strain rate and nothing else: damping the spin would brake a rotating blob of liquid, which no fluid does. The code's version removes trace and shear, keeps spin, and then the hydrostatic step, which runs after it in the code, resets the trace to the incompressible value. Net effect: shear damped by $\alpha_{visc}$, spin kept, volume constrained. That is the version to implement. "Off-diagonal elements" alone would not be frame independent (rotate the axes and diagonal shear appears), and the algorithm's literal form also damps spin.

The text calls the two liquid constraints "orthogonal". In the code's form they are: pressure lives in the trace, shear in the symmetric traceless part, and those two subspaces of matrices are orthogonal under $\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{B}$. Correcting one does not undo the other, so they can be applied one after the other without iterating between them.

Section 2.1: the rest of the liquid story

Read the paragraph on volume loss carefully. If the solver does not fully converge, the integrated $J$ drifts, and the liquid slowly loses volume while believing itself incompressible. The fix is an "objective" volume measure from the grid. In the code (useGridVolumeForLiquid): P2G also splats each particle's volume, G2P gathers the resulting volume fraction around each particle, and if it says the particle is compressed, $J$ is pulled 10 % of the way toward the grid's value each iteration. So crowded particles feel crowded even if their integrated $J$ claims otherwise, and the liquid recovers. The paragraph also admits that relaxation factors are a crude control (their effect changes with time step and iteration count) and suggests an XPBD variant for proper material control.

Check your understanding

1. A particle has $J = 1.05$ and $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} = 0$. What correction $a$ does the code apply (2D, relaxation 1), and what is $J$ after integration?

$a = \tfrac12(1/1.05 - 1) = -0.0238$, so the trace becomes $-0.0476$ and $J^{n+1} = (1 - 0.0476)\cdot1.05 = 1.000$. The particle is told to shrink by the amount it is over-expanded. (If the grid then averages its $\mathbf{D}$ with neighbours that disagree, it will not quite get there, which is what iterations are for.)

2. Why does the liquid not need $\mathbf{F}$?

An ideal liquid has no memory of shape: its only elastic response is to volume. All the information it needs is the scalar $J$. The code stores a full $\mathbf{F}$ field for every particle, but for liquids it is never used.

Algorithm 2: the constraint solve, elastic solids

As printed:

else if p.isElastic:
  F*      = F (I + D)
  A_shape = PolarDecomposition(F*)
  A_vol   = F* / det(F*)
  D       = F^-1 (beta A_vol + (1 - beta) A_shape) - I

And as the code does it:

$$\mathbf{F}^* = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}})\mathbf{F}, \qquad \mathbf{R} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^T, \qquad \mathbf{Q} = \frac{\mathbf{F}^*}{\sqrt{\det\mathbf{F}^*}}\ \ (\text{2D; } |\det| \text{ clamped to } [0.1, 1000]),$$ $$\mathbf{A} = \beta\mathbf{R} + (1 - \beta)\mathbf{Q}, \qquad \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} \leftarrow \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} + \omega\big((\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}^{-1} - \mathbf{I}) - \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}\big).$$

Line by line.

Line 9, the candidate. $\mathbf{F}^*$ is the deformation gradient the particle will have at the end of the step if its current $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}$ stands. The paper writes $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D})$, multiplying on the right; the code writes $(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D})\mathbf{F}$, on the left. The code is right: $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} = h\nabla\mathbf{v}$ is a world-space velocity gradient and must act on the left, as in $\dot{\mathbf{F}} = (\nabla\mathbf{v})\mathbf{F}$ (chapter 29, section 5.4). The integrator uses the same left product, and the solve must match the integrator, or it is constraining a different matrix from the one that will be stored. The rearrangement changes accordingly.

Line 12, the rearrangement. To make the end-of-step deformation equal some target $\mathbf{A}$, solve $(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}})\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{A}$ for $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}$:

$$\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}^{-1} - \mathbf{I}.$$

(The paper's $\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{I}$ goes with its right-multiplied line 9.) Like the liquid case, the condition is linear in $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}$, so the projection is one line. Note that $\mathbf{F}$ here is the start-of-step value, fixed during the iterations; only $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}$ changes. The code adds an under-relaxation $\omega$ (elasticRelaxation); $\omega = 1$ is the paper's assignment.

Line 10, the shape target is the polar rotation. Why a rotation? Because an elastic solid at rest is deformed only by a rotation: any $\mathbf{F}$ that is a rotation has zero elastic energy, and the nearest such state is the nearest rotation. That is exactly the polar factor $\mathbf{R}$ of $\mathbf{F}^* = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}$: among all rotations, $\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^T$ minimises $\|\mathbf{F}^* - \mathbf{R}\|_F$. That distance squared is the $\mu$ term of the fixed corotated energy, $\mu\sum(\sigma_i - 1)^2 = \mu\|\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{R}\|_F^2$. So projecting to $\mathbf{R}$ is the PBD version of fixed corotated elasticity: instead of a force proportional to $\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{R}$, move $\mathbf{F}$ to $\mathbf{R}$. If you know Müller et al.'s shape matching (2005), this is the same idea per particle: find the best rigid fit, pull towards it. (In 2D the code's SVD is unnecessary for $\mathbf{R}$; the angle $\operatorname{atan2}(c - b, a + d)$ gives it, chapter 29.)

Line 11, the volume target is a uniform rescaling. The volume constraint is $\det\mathbf{F} = 1$. Among the many matrices with unit determinant, the one that changes nothing but the volume is a uniform scaling of $\mathbf{F}^*$: same rotation, same ratio of stretches, area put back to 1. Since $\det(c\mathbf{F}) = c^d\det\mathbf{F}$, the scale is $c = (\det\mathbf{F}^*)^{-1/d}$:

$$\mathbf{A}_{vol} = \frac{\mathbf{F}^*}{(\det\mathbf{F}^*)^{1/d}} = \frac{\mathbf{F}^*}{\sqrt{\det\mathbf{F}^*}}\ \ \text{in 2D}, \qquad \frac{\mathbf{F}^*}{\sqrt[3]{\det\mathbf{F}^*}}\ \ \text{in 3D}.$$

The paper's $\mathbf{F}^*/\det(\mathbf{F}^*)$ has determinant $1/\det\mathbf{F}^*$ in 2D, so it would invert the volume error rather than remove it; treat it as a typo. The code uses the square root, with the sign of the determinant kept and its magnitude clamped to $[0.1, 1000]$ so a nearly collapsed particle cannot produce a huge target. In singular values: $(\sigma_1, \sigma_2) \mapsto (\sigma_1, \sigma_2)/\sqrt{\sigma_1\sigma_2}$, sliding along the ray through the origin to the curve $\sigma_1\sigma_2 = 1$.

The blend. $\mathbf{R}$ already has unit determinant, so the shape target preserves volume too; but it also removes all shear. $\mathbf{Q}$ preserves volume and leaves shape alone. The paper notes the two constraints are not orthogonal (unlike the liquid's trace and shear): both are full matrices, and projecting onto one then the other would fight. So it projects once, onto a blend $\mathbf{A} = \beta\mathbf{R} + (1 - \beta)\mathbf{Q}$. With $\beta = 1$ the particle is pulled fully back to its rest shape (a stiff jelly); with $\beta = 0$ only its area is held, so it flows like an incompressible liquid that happens to track $\mathbf{F}$; in between it is a softer solid. Which term carries $\beta$: the text says $\beta\mathbf{A}_{shape}$, Algorithm 2 says $\beta\mathbf{A}_{vol}$, and the code's elasticityRatio multiplies the rotation. Follow the code.

Section 2.2: badly conditioned F

The last paragraph of section 2.2 is a practical warning: with large time steps, the tracked $\mathbf{F}$ can become nearly singular, and the paper deletes elastic particles whose condition number exceeds $10^6$. The released code takes a gentler route: after every integration it clamps the singular values of $\mathbf{F}$ to $[0.2, 10^4]$. Liquids are exempt in both, because their volume comes partly from the grid. The code also contains two materials the talk does not mention: Drucker-Prager sand (a return mapping in log-strain space, after Klár et al. 2016; see chapter 25) and a simple volume-preserving viscoplastic material. They reuse the elastic projection with plasticity applied at integration.

Check your understanding

1. Show that with $\beta = 1$, $\omega = 1$ and a single particle on its own, one projection puts $\mathbf{F}^{n+1}$ exactly on a rotation.

The projection sets $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}^{-1} - \mathbf{I}$. If the grid hands it back unchanged (no neighbours to average with, and an affine field survives APIC transfers exactly), integration gives $(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}})\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}$.

2. In 2D, $\mathbf{F}^* = \operatorname{diag}(1.2, 1.2)$. What are $\mathbf{A}_{shape}$, the code's $\mathbf{A}_{vol}$, and the paper's literal $\mathbf{F}^*/\det\mathbf{F}^*$?

$\mathbf{R} = \mathbf{I}$. $\det\mathbf{F}^* = 1.44$, so $\mathbf{A}_{vol} = \operatorname{diag}(1.2, 1.2)/1.2 = \mathbf{I}$, the right answer for a pure expansion. The literal form gives $\operatorname{diag}(1.2, 1.2)/1.44 = \operatorname{diag}(0.833, 0.833)$, with determinant 0.694: it overshoots into compression by about as much as the particle was expanded.

3. Why is $\mathbf{F}$ (start of step) held fixed during the iterations, while $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}$ changes?

Because the unknown of the step is the motion, and $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}$ (with $\mathbf{d}$) is the motion. $\mathbf{F}^n$ is history. The constraint is evaluated on the end-of-step value $(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}})\mathbf{F}^n$, and $\mathbf{F}$ is only overwritten once, by the integrator, after the iterations.

Section 3: Discussion

Two paragraphs, both important. Read them twice.

Why the solver is Jacobi-like, and what that means

The talk says the method "amounts to a Jacobi-style solver because all the constraints are updated in parallel" without information passing between them during the constraint step. Concretely: in step (a) every particle projects its own $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p$, all reading the same input, as in Jacobi iteration (chapter 9). Steps (c) to (e) then reconcile the corrections: P2G takes a mass-weighted average of neighbouring particles' affine displacement fields at each node, and G2P fits a new affine field to the averaged node values. So each particle leaves the iteration with a compromise between its own target and its neighbours'. This is Jacobi iteration with averaging, the same structure as PBD's Jacobi mode (each constraint computes a correction, overlapping corrections are averaged).

The consequence is slow convergence for large-scale errors. A quadratic B-spline couples a particle to nodes within 1.5 cells, so one iteration moves information about one or two cells. An error that spans the whole body, such as a liquid column compressed by its own weight, needs the correction to travel the whole height. A model problem shows the scale: Jacobi iteration on a 1D chain of $N$ coupled unknowns (a discrete Laplacian) reduces its slowest error mode by a factor $\cos(\pi/(N+1))$ per iteration. For $N = 64$, that is 0.9988 per iteration, and reducing that mode tenfold takes about 2,000 iterations. PB-MPM runs one to a few. The unconverged part does not explode; it shows up as missing stiffness. The talk's word is "artificial softness": columns that sag, jelly that is softer than its $\beta$ suggests, and both depending on time step and iteration count.

Substeps beat iterations, again. The argument of chapter 8 carries over. Each substep starts from a warm initial guess (the previous displacement plus gravity) and only has to correct the error created in that substep, which shrinks with $h^2$. So for the same total work, more substeps with one iteration each usually beat fewer substeps with many iterations. The talk's own example uses one iteration.

The 30 Hz versus 240 Hz claim

The second paragraph makes the only quantitative claim. One iteration costs about one explicit MPM step. In a liquid dam break, PB-MPM was stable at 30 Hz with one iteration; an explicit MPM liquid with a stiffness chosen to hold the same volume at rest needed 240 Hz to survive the splash. That is eight times the work for the explicit version. (The talk says "we are able to take much smaller time steps"; from context it means larger, or fewer.)

Why does the explicit liquid need so many steps? Its pressure comes from an equation of state, and to keep the resting compression small the bulk stiffness $K$ must be large: holding a column of height $H$ to a compression of $\varepsilon$ needs $K/\rho \approx gH/\varepsilon$. The sound speed $c = \sqrt{K/\rho}$ then sets the time step through $h \lesssim \Delta x/c$. As a back-of-envelope example (not the talk's setup): $H = 1$ m and $\varepsilon = 1\,\%$ give $c = 31$ m/s, and with 64 cells per metre $h \lesssim 0.5$ ms, about 2,000 steps per second. PB-MPM's projection has no stiffness in it, so no such limit; its accuracy degrades instead. The paragraph closes with the fair point that you can stabilise explicit MPM by limiting its forces, but that also makes it artificially soft, whereas PBD is "as stiff as possible" without going unstable.

Why it does not explode

The talk asserts unconditional stability; it does not prove it. The intuition, which you should be able to explain: in explicit MPM the change of velocity is force times time step, and the force is stiffness times deformation, so a stiff material over a long step overshoots its rest state, further each time (the $h\omega > 2$ instability of chapter 3). In PB-MPM the projection moves each particle's end-of-step deformation to a target (a rotation, a unit-volume matrix), never past it, whatever the step or stiffness; and the grid transfers are weighted averages, which cannot amplify. The safety clamps (density at least 0.1, singular values in $[0.2, 10^4]$, determinant clamp in the volume target) cover the degenerate cases where the argument breaks down.

Check your understanding

1. Doubling the iteration count roughly doubles the cost. Does it double the stiffness?

No. For a Jacobi-type solver, each iteration removes only a fraction of the slowest errors, so the effective stiffness grows slowly and nonlinearly with iterations. Halving the substep is often the better use of the same work.

2. What is the explicit time-step limit, by the estimate above, for a 25 cm deep pool held to 1 % compression on a grid of 128 cells per metre?

$c = \sqrt{9.81\times0.25/0.01} = 15.7$ m/s, $\Delta x = 7.8$ mm, so $h \lesssim 0.5$ ms again: shallower water lowers the sound speed, the finer grid lowers $\Delta x$, and here the two cancel.

Section 4: Conclusion

One paragraph: a simple modification of MPM that is stable at large steps, useful wherever PBD-style solvers are already common, with destruction and new material types as possible applications. Nothing new to extract.

What the paper does not tell you

  • The loop around Algorithm 1: substeps, grid clearing, gravity added to the displacement after integration, and the warm start. All reconstructed above from sim.js and particleIntegrate.wgsl.
  • That the grid holds displacements, not velocities, and that collisions are therefore resolved on displaced node positions $\mathbf{x}_i + \mathbf{d}_i$.
  • Momentum. The transfers conserve linear momentum (they are APIC transfers). The talk does not discuss whether the constraint projections conserve linear and angular momentum; the liquid projection adds a multiple of the identity, which carries no spin, but the elastic projection changes the antisymmetric part of $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}$ in general. Measuring this is an exercise below.
  • Compliance. There is none; stiffness is set by relaxation factors and convergence. The talk suggests an XPBD variant would give physical control; it is not in the release.
  • 3D. The code is 2D. In 3D the volume target needs the cube root and the trace correction a factor $\tfrac13$.
  • The several small disagreements between text, algorithms and code, collected here so you can annotate your copy: right versus left multiplication in line 9; $\det$ versus $\sqrt{\det}$ in line 11; which term $\beta$ multiplies; the missing $1/d$ in lines 5 and 6; "smaller" for "larger" time steps; and the condition-number deletion versus the clamp.

Exercises

reproduce the 30 Hz versus 240 Hz claim, qualitatively

Use the dam break of the chapter 27 demo, or your own solution to its exercises. Run PB-MPM at 30 substeps per second with one iteration and confirm it survives the splash. Then take your explicit liquid from chapter 24 with an equation of state, and choose its stiffness so the resting column height matches PB-MPM's within a few percent. Lower its step rate until it fails. Predict the ratio first, using the sound-speed estimate above. Is the measured ratio close to 8? What does it depend on?

Hint

Match the steady state, not the parameters: PB-MPM's resting compression depends on iterations and substeps, so measure it first, then tune the explicit stiffness to match. The explicit limit is set by the sound speed during the splash, so watch for the first frame where particles fly apart.

the factor $1/d$

In your chapter-27 code, replace the liquid correction by the paper's literal line 5 ($a = 1/J - 1 - \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D}$, no $1/d$) with relaxation 1. Plot the mean $J$ over time. What happens, and why? Then find the relaxation factor that makes the literal form equivalent to the code's.

Answer

Each projection overshoots by a factor of $d = 2$, so a particle's own volume error flips sign instead of vanishing; averaging through the grid damps some of it, but the liquid jitters. $\alpha_{hydro} = \tfrac12$ makes the two identical.

which viscosity?

Set up a disk of liquid spinning rigidly in zero gravity. Implement three viscosity corrections: the code's (remove the symmetric part), the algorithm's ($-(\mathbf{D} - \operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{D})\mathbf{I})$) and the text's (zero the off-diagonal entries). Plot the disk's angular velocity over time for each with $\alpha_{visc} = 0.5$. Predict before you run which ones brake the spin.

Answer

A rigid spin has a purely antisymmetric $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}$. The code's version leaves it alone. The other two both modify the off-diagonal entries, which hold the spin, so both slow the disk down like a stirred fluid coming to rest against nothing.

softness, substeps and iterations

Clamp one end of an elastic beam ($\beta = 1$) and let it sag under gravity. Measure the tip deflection for (substeps, iterations) = (1, 8), (2, 4), (4, 2), (8, 1), the same total work. Which is stiffest? Relate your answer to the Jacobi argument of section 3 and to chapter 8.

break it on purpose: the order of multiplication

Change $\mathbf{F}^* = (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D})\mathbf{F}$ to the paper's $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D})$ in the solve (with the matching rearrangement $\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{I}$), but keep the integrator as it is. First, on paper: show that if the grid passes the corrected $\mathbf{D}$ through unchanged, the integrated deformation gradient is $\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}$ instead of the target $\mathbf{A}$. For which $\mathbf{F}$ are the two equal? Then test your prediction on two jelly blocks: one only rotated by 45 degrees, and one given an initial $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}(45^\circ)\operatorname{diag}(1.3, 0.77)$, that is, stretched and rotated.

Answer

$(\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{A} - \mathbf{I})\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}$. If $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}_0$ is a pure rotation, both targets commute with rotations on either side, $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{R}_0\mathbf{M}) = \mathbf{R}_0\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{M})$ and $\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{M}\mathbf{R}_0) = \mathbf{A}(\mathbf{M})\mathbf{R}_0$, so the wrong version produces exactly the same result as the right one, so a rotated but undeformed block hides the bug. Near the identity the two agree to first order too. With both stretch and rotation, $\mathbf{F}^{-1}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}$ is in general not a rotation even when $\mathbf{A}$ is, so the block is pulled towards the wrong shape and does not settle back to its rest shape.

an open question: momentum

Measure total linear and angular momentum (including the APIC spin term, chapter 28) before and after the constraint solve, for a spinning elastic disk and a spinning liquid disk. Which projections conserve which quantity? This is not answered in the talk; write down what you find.

Summary

  • PB-MPM is explicit MPM's loop with the stress computation replaced by a per-particle constraint projection, iterated through the grid: solve constraints, P2G, grid update, G2P, repeated, then integrate.
  • Particles carry a displacement $\mathbf{d}_p = h\mathbf{v}_p$ and a deformation displacement $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p = h\mathbf{C}_p$; the transfers are APIC/MLS-MPM transfers of these, and $\mathbf{F} \leftarrow (\mathbf{I} + \mathbf{D}^{\text{def}})\mathbf{F}$.
  • Liquids: a trace correction $a = \tfrac1d(1/J - 1 - \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D})$ makes the linearised volume update $(1 + \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{D})J$ land on 1; viscosity removes the symmetric part and keeps spin.
  • Elastics: project the end-of-step $\mathbf{F}^*$ to a blend of its polar rotation (shape) and $\mathbf{F}^*/\sqrt{\det\mathbf{F}^*}$ (volume, in 2D), via $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}} = \mathbf{A}\mathbf{F}^{-1} - \mathbf{I}$.
  • The solve is Jacobi with averaging: unconditionally stable in practice, but slow to converge for large-scale errors, which shows up as softness. Substeps help more than iterations.
  • Several details differ between the talk's text, its algorithms and its code; the code is the reference.
