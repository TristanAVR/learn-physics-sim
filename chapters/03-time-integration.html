Time Integration

Why simulations explode, and the three lines of code that decide whether yours will.

In the last chapter you moved a particle by adding its velocity to its position every frame. It looked fine. In this chapter we hang that particle on a spring, and with exactly the same code it will fly off the screen within a few seconds. Nothing is wrong with the spring. The problem is the way we stepped through time, and fixing it is the first real decision every simulation makes.

By the end of this chapter you will know three integrators, be able to predict which one survives a given spring and time step, and understand the sentence that opens almost every modern simulation paper: "explicit integration has well known stability issues."

Physics as a recipe for the next moment

Newton's second law tells us how velocity changes, and the definition of velocity tells us how position changes:

$$\frac{dx}{dt} = v, \qquad \frac{dv}{dt} = a = \frac{F(x, v)}{m}.$$

Together, position and velocity are the state of the particle. If we know the state now, these two equations tell us which way it is heading. They do not tell us where the particle will be one frame from now: that would need us to add up (integrate) all the tiny changes that happen during the frame, while the force itself keeps changing. Solving that exactly is possible only for the simplest systems. A simulation instead takes a finite time step $h$ (usually 1/60 s or smaller) and approximates.

The simplest approximation pretends that the rates of change stay fixed for the whole step, at the values they had at the start:

$$x_{n+1} = x_n + h\,v_n, \qquad v_{n+1} = v_n + h\,a_n.$$

This is explicit Euler (or forward Euler). The subscript $n$ counts steps: $x_n$ is the position after $n$ steps, at time $t = nh$. "Explicit" means that everything on the right-hand side is already known, so each new value is one line of code:

function explicitEuler(b, h) {
  const a = springForce(b.x) / mass;   // acceleration from the CURRENT state
  b.x = b.x + h * b.v;                 // move with the CURRENT velocity
  b.v = b.v + h * a;
}

For a ball thrown in gravity this is nearly perfect, because the acceleration really is constant. Let's try a spring instead.

The test problem: a mass on a spring

A spring of stiffness $k$ pulls a mass $m$ back toward its rest position with a force proportional to the stretch $x$ (Hooke's law):

$$F = -k\,x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad a = -\frac{k}{m}\,x = -\omega^2 x, \qquad \omega = \sqrt{k/m}.$$

We know the exact answer: the mass oscillates forever as $x(t) = x_0 \cos(\omega t)$, with angular frequency $\omega$ (in radians per second) and constant total energy

$$E = \tfrac12 m v^2 + \tfrac12 k x^2.$$

This tiny system is the most useful test problem in all of simulation. Almost everything you will simulate in this site, from a rope to a block of jelly, behaves locally like a collection of springs. If an integrator mistreats one spring, it will mistreat all of them.

Run the demo below. It holds three identical springs, each advanced by a different integrator, and plots each one's energy relative to the start. For now, look only at the red one: explicit Euler.

The red block swings wider and wider, and its energy curve climbs without limit. Lower the time step and it climbs more slowly, but it still climbs. Explicit Euler creates energy out of nothing.

Why explicit Euler explodes

Here is the beautiful part: for the spring we can work out exactly what one step does. Write the state as a vector and the step as a matrix. With $a_n = -\omega^2 x_n$, explicit Euler is

$$\begin{pmatrix} x_{n+1} \\ v_{n+1} \end{pmatrix} = \underbrace{\begin{pmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h\omega^2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}}_{A} \begin{pmatrix} x_n \\ v_n \end{pmatrix}.$$

Every step multiplies the state by the same matrix $A$. After $n$ steps the state is $A^n$ times the start, so what matters is whether repeated multiplication by $A$ makes vectors grow or shrink. That is decided by the eigenvalues of $A$: if any has magnitude above 1, some part of the state grows geometrically. Here the eigenvalues are $\lambda = 1 \pm i\,h\omega$, with

$$|\lambda| = \sqrt{1 + h^2\omega^2} \;>\; 1 \quad\text{for every } h > 0.$$

So explicit Euler is unstable for an undamped spring at any time step. You can see the same thing through energy: substitute the update into $E$ and you get, exactly,

$$E_{n+1} = (1 + h^2\omega^2)\,E_n.$$

With the demo's defaults, $\omega = 10$ rad/s and $h = 16$ ms, so $h\omega = 0.16$ and the energy grows by 2.6 % per step. At 60 steps a second that compounds to roughly a factor of 4.6 every second. Halving the time step only slows the growth.

The quantity to remember is $h\omega$: the time step measured in units of the fastest oscillation. A stiff spring (large $k$) or a light mass (small $m$) means a large $\omega$, which needs a small $h$. When someone says a system is stiff, in the numerical sense, they mean exactly this: it contains motions much faster than the time step you would like to take.

Symplectic Euler: swap two lines

Now change one thing. Update the velocity first, and then move the particle with the new velocity:

$$v_{n+1} = v_n + h\,a_n, \qquad x_{n+1} = x_n + h\,v_{n+1}.$$

This is symplectic Euler (also semi-implicit Euler). It costs nothing extra; it is the same two lines in the opposite order. Its step matrix is

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - h^2\omega^2 & h \\ -h\omega^2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \det A = 1.$$

The determinant is exactly 1, which means the step preserves area in the $(x, v)$ plane. That is the property called symplectic, and it has a remarkable consequence: the method does not drift in energy. The energy wobbles a little around the true value but never runs away, which is why games and most real-time simulators use this integrator.

It is not unconditionally stable, though. The eigenvalues stay on the unit circle only while $|\operatorname{tr} A| \le 2$, that is

$$|2 - h^2\omega^2| \le 2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h\,\omega \le 2.$$

Past that limit one eigenvalue leaves the circle and the simulation explodes just as violently as explicit Euler did. So symplectic Euler gives you a rule you can actually use: keep $h\omega$ below 2, and in practice well below, because accuracy suffers long before the limit.

Implicit Euler: ask the end of the step

The third option evaluates the acceleration at the end of the step instead of the start:

$$v_{n+1} = v_n + h\,a_{n+1}, \qquad x_{n+1} = x_n + h\,v_{n+1}.$$

This is implicit Euler (backward Euler). The catch is visible in the formula: $a_{n+1}$ depends on $x_{n+1}$, which we do not know yet. Each step is now an equation to solve, not an assignment. For one linear spring we can solve it by hand, and that is your first exercise. For a thousand coupled springs it becomes a large linear system, and for nonlinear forces a nonlinear one: this cost is the price of implicit methods, and a good part of this site is about paying it cleverly.

What do we get for the price? The step matrix has eigenvalues of magnitude

$$|\lambda| = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + h^2\omega^2}} \;<\; 1 \quad\text{for every } h > 0,$$

so implicit Euler is unconditionally stable: no time step, however large, makes it explode. In the demo the blue spring never misbehaves. But look at its energy curve: it decays, by the factor $1/(1 + h^2\omega^2)$ every step. Implicit Euler removes energy, exactly as much as explicit Euler adds. The motion looks damped even though our spring has no damper. This numerical damping is often welcome (it calms jitter) and sometimes not (a pendulum that should swing for minutes stops in seconds). Good simulators know how much of it their integrator adds, and account for it.

Hint: solving the implicit step

You should arrive at $v_{n+1}\,(1 + h^2\omega^2) = v_n - h\,\omega^2 x_n$. Everything on the right is known.

Solution

Compare with solution.js. The two functions are:

function symplecticEuler(b, h) {
  const a = springForce(b.x) / mass.value;
  b.v = b.v + h * a;
  b.x = b.x + h * b.v;
}

function implicitEuler(b, h) {
  const w2 = stiffness.value / mass.value;
  b.v = (b.v - h * w2 * b.x) / (1 + h * h * w2);
  b.x = b.x + h * b.v;
}

Choosing a time step in a real program

Two practical points before moving on.

Use a fixed time step. Frame rates vary, but your integrator's stability depends on $h$. If you pass the measured frame time straight into the step, a single slow frame can push $h\omega$ past the limit and blow the simulation up. The standard fix is an accumulator: add the real elapsed time to a bank, and run fixed steps of size $h$ while the bank holds at least $h$. The sandbox does the simplest version of this, one or more fixed steps per animation frame.

Know your fastest mode. The stability limit is set by the largest $\omega$ anywhere in the system: the stiffest spring attached to the lightest particle. A rope of a hundred soft springs with one tiny bead on a stiff link has the time step limit of that one link. When a simulation explodes, the first question is always: what is the fastest thing in it, and how does it compare with my time step?

where this shows up in the papers

The PB-MPM paper's abstract starts from exactly this chapter: explicit MPM is easy to implement but has stability problems that force small time steps, and implicit MPM (solving a system each step with Newton's method) allows large steps but is complex. Its contribution is a third route, which you will build in the Position-Based MPM chapter. The MPM course notes cover the same split in their sections on Explicit Time Integration and Implicit Time Integration; their "symplectic Euler MPM" is the green integrator from this chapter, applied to millions of particles through a grid.

Summary

  • An integrator turns "how the state is changing now" into "the state one step later". The choice matters more than almost anything else in a simulator.
  • Explicit Euler evaluates everything at the start of the step. For oscillating systems it gains energy at any time step.
  • Symplectic Euler updates velocity first. Same cost, no energy drift, stable while $h\omega \le 2$.
  • Implicit Euler evaluates forces at the end of the step. Stable for any step, but it must solve an equation each step and it adds numerical damping.
  • $h\omega$, the time step measured against the fastest motion, predicts stability. Stiff systems need small steps or implicit methods.
