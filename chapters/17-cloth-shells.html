Cloth and Thin Shells

A sheet of particles that barely stretches, bends at a touch, and tears when pulled too hard.

Pick up a handkerchief by two corners. It sags, folds, swings, and the folds settle in places that look effortless and are hard to predict. Now pick up a sheet of paper the same way: it bends into a smooth curve but will not fold the way the handkerchief does. Both are thin shells, surfaces whose thickness is tiny compared with their size, and both obey the same rule: they resist stretching enormously and resist bending hardly at all. How much "hardly" is, is what separates a silk scarf from a sheet of paper from a tin roof.

You already have every tool needed to simulate them. A rope (PBD, XPBD) is a line of particles held by distance constraints; cloth is a grid of them. This chapter adds the two ingredients that a line does not need: constraints against shear, and a way to resist bending of a surface. It then works out why thin sheets behave the way they do, from the two stiffnesses of a shell, so you can set the constraint compliances from a real thickness and stiffness instead of by guesswork.

By the end you will have a cloth that swings, drapes over a ball, flaps in the wind and tears, and you will be able to say how stiff its bending should be relative to its stretching for a sheet of a given thickness.

A grid of particles

Lay out an $N \times N$ grid of particles with spacing $\ell$. The demo uses $N = 25$ and $\ell = 4$ cm, a square of 0.96 m. Give the cloth an areal density $\rho_A$, mass per area, and share it among the particles: for a medium cotton, $\rho_A = 0.2$ kg/m2, so the whole square weighs 0.184 kg and each of its 625 particles about 0.29 g. Integrate them as usual, gravity included. Then connect them with three kinds of constraint.

  • Structural (stretch): each particle to its right and lower neighbour. These hold the length of the threads, the main job of cloth.
  • Shear: both diagonals of each square. Without them, a square of four stretch constraints can collapse into a thin rhombus without changing any length. This is a mechanism, a motion that costs nothing, and a grid without shear constraints drapes like a fishing net.
  • Bending: something that resists folding the surface along a line. This one needs thought, and has its own section below.

Each type gets its own XPBD compliance $\alpha$ (the inverse of a stiffness), so you can make the cloth nearly inextensible along its threads, softer in shear and very soft in bending, as real fabric is. The distance constraint is the one from the XPBD chapter, written in 3D:

function solveDistance(c, alphaTilde) {           // alphaTilde = alpha / h^2
  const a = c.a, b = c.b, wsum = w[a] + w[b];
  if (wsum === 0) return;
  const dx = x[a] - x[b], dy = y[a] - y[b], dz = z[a] - z[b];
  const len = Math.hypot(dx, dy, dz);
  const C = len - c.rest;
  const dlambda = (-C - alphaTilde * c.lambda) / (wsum + alphaTilde);
  c.lambda += dlambda;
  const s = dlambda / len;                          // gradient wrt a is (xa - xb) / len
  x[a] += w[a] * s * dx;  y[a] += w[a] * s * dy;  z[a] += w[a] * s * dz;
  x[b] -= w[b] * s * dx;  y[b] -= w[b] * s * dy;  z[b] -= w[b] * s * dz;
}

The demo runs 15 substeps per frame with one pass over every constraint per substep, the scheme from the substepping chapter. Pinning a particle is setting its inverse mass $w$ to zero, which every constraint already respects.

The cloth starts horizontal, pinned at two corners, and swings down. Drag it with the mouse, turn on the wind, try the drape over the sphere, and switch the bending model. The view sliders rotate the camera. The picture is a small orthographic projection written in the demo file itself (rotate about the vertical axis by the yaw, then about the horizontal axis by the pitch, drop the depth) with the triangles drawn back to front.

How inextensible is inextensible?

Look at the readout "largest stretch". With the default stretch compliance of $10^{-7}$ m/N, an edge stiffness of 10 million N/m, a link of a hanging 0.18 kg cloth should stretch by far less than a micrometre. Yet the readout says almost 6 %, at the two pinned corners. Set the compliance to $10^{-8}$ or $10^{-5}$: the number does not change.

This is the solver, not the material. One Gauss-Seidel pass per substep moves information one constraint at a time, and the whole weight of the cloth funnels into the few edges at each pin. The solvers chapter explains why: an iterative solver at a fixed iteration count has a maximum effective stiffness, and any compliance below it behaves the same. Only above about $10^{-3}$ m/N does the compliance itself become visible. Averaged over the whole cloth the stretch is only 0.11 %, which is why it still looks like cloth. If you need less, the options are more substeps, more iterations, or long range attachments (Kim, Chentanez and Müller, 2012), extra one-sided constraints from each particle to the pins that cap the total stretch.

Bending

The cheap way: second neighbours

The oldest trick adds a distance constraint between particles two apart along each row and column, $(i, j)$ to $(i + 2, j)$. If the cloth folds at the middle particle by an angle $\theta$, the two links of length $\ell$ form a V, and the distance across it shrinks from $2\ell$ to $2\ell\cos(\theta/2)$. So the constraint is

$$C = 2\ell\cos(\theta/2) - 2\ell \approx -\frac{\ell\,\theta^2}{4}.$$

It is easy to build, uses the distance code you already have, and works. But look at the shape of $C$: it is quadratic in the fold angle, so its energy $\tfrac12 k C^2$ is quartic. At small angles the constraint has no stiffness at all (its gradient with respect to $\theta$ is zero at $\theta = 0$), and at large angles it is very stiff. Small wrinkles cost nothing, big folds cost a lot. It also resists in-plane compression, which is not bending at all. Cloth looks fine with it. A sheet of metal does not.

The proper way: the dihedral angle

Triangulate the grid (two triangles per square). Every interior edge is shared by two triangles, a hinge. The angle $\theta$ between the two triangles' normals, zero when they are coplanar, is the dihedral angle, and it measures the local fold directly. The bending constraint is

$$C = \theta - \theta_0,$$

with $\theta_0$ the rest angle (zero for a flat cloth, nonzero for a pre-curved shell such as a hat brim or a leaf). Its energy is quadratic in the angle, like a linear spring, which is what the elastic theory of plates says it should be.

For a hinge with shared edge $\mathbf{x}_3\mathbf{x}_4$ and wing vertices $\mathbf{x}_1$ and $\mathbf{x}_2$, let $\mathbf{E} = \mathbf{x}_4 - \mathbf{x}_3$ and take the (unnormalised) triangle normals $\mathbf{N}_1 = (\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_3)\times(\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_4)$ and $\mathbf{N}_2 = (\mathbf{x}_2 - \mathbf{x}_4)\times(\mathbf{x}_2 - \mathbf{x}_3)$. With unit normals $\hat{\mathbf{n}}_1$, $\hat{\mathbf{n}}_2$ and unit edge $\hat{\mathbf{e}}$, a signed angle that is smooth through zero is

$$\theta = \operatorname{atan2}\big((\hat{\mathbf{n}}_1\times\hat{\mathbf{n}}_2)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{e}},\; \hat{\mathbf{n}}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_2\big).$$

Using $\arccos(\hat{\mathbf{n}}_1\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}_2)$ instead loses the sign and has an infinite derivative at exactly the flat rest state, which is why the formula in Müller et al.'s original Position Based Dynamics paper (2007, whose appendix derives an arccos-based bending constraint) needs special care there.

The gradients have a beautiful closed form, given by Bridson, Marino and Fedkiw in Simulation of Clothing with Folds and Wrinkles (2003), who call them the bending modes $\mathbf{u}_1 \dots \mathbf{u}_4$:

$$\mathbf{u}_1 = |\mathbf{E}|\frac{\mathbf{N}_1}{|\mathbf{N}_1|^2}, \qquad \mathbf{u}_2 = |\mathbf{E}|\frac{\mathbf{N}_2}{|\mathbf{N}_2|^2},$$ $$\mathbf{u}_3 = \frac{(\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_4)\cdot\mathbf{E}}{|\mathbf{E}|}\frac{\mathbf{N}_1}{|\mathbf{N}_1|^2} + \frac{(\mathbf{x}_2 - \mathbf{x}_4)\cdot\mathbf{E}}{|\mathbf{E}|}\frac{\mathbf{N}_2}{|\mathbf{N}_2|^2}, \qquad \mathbf{u}_4 = -\frac{(\mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_3)\cdot\mathbf{E}}{|\mathbf{E}|}\frac{\mathbf{N}_1}{|\mathbf{N}_1|^2} - \frac{(\mathbf{x}_2 - \mathbf{x}_3)\cdot\mathbf{E}}{|\mathbf{E}|}\frac{\mathbf{N}_2}{|\mathbf{N}_2|^2}.$$

The wing gradients are perpendicular to their own triangle, inversely proportional to its height above the edge; the edge vertices take whatever balances them, so that the four sum to zero (moving the whole hinge does not change its angle). With the sign convention of the atan2 above, $\nabla_i\theta = -\mathbf{u}_i$. Signs are the classic trap here, so the demo was checked against finite differences of $\theta$; they agree to eight digits. With $C$ and its four gradients, the XPBD update is the usual one:

$$\Delta\lambda = \frac{-C - \tilde\alpha\lambda}{\sum_i w_i|\nabla_i C|^2 + \tilde\alpha}, \qquad \Delta\mathbf{x}_i = w_i\,\nabla_i C\,\Delta\lambda.$$

Grinspun, Hirani, Desbrun and Schröder's Discrete Shells (2003) built the corresponding energy, the squared change of dihedral angle weighted by the edge length over the triangles' heights, derived from the bending energy of a continuous shell. That weighting is what connects a hinge's stiffness to a material's, below.

Try both in the demo on the sphere with the bending compliance at 1. The second-neighbour cloth drapes in soft folds, because small bends are free. The dihedral cloth stays a smooth, stiff sheet bent in one direction only, like a sheet of plastic laid over a ball. The next section explains why that one direction is no accident.

Thin shells: stretching versus bending

Why does every thin sheet bend so much more easily than it stretches? Take a plate of thickness $t$, Young's modulus $E$ and Poisson's ratio $\nu$ (the beams chapter introduced both).

Stretching it involves the whole thickness. The force per unit width needed for a strain $\varepsilon$ is $E t\,\varepsilon$, so the membrane stiffness is

$$k_{\text{membrane}} \sim E\,t \quad \text{(N/m)}.$$

Bending it stretches one face and compresses the other, and the material in the middle does nothing. Exactly as for a beam's $EI$, where the second moment of area of a rectangle of width $b$ and height $t$ is $bt^3/12$, the bending stiffness per unit width of a plate is

$$D = \frac{E\,t^3}{12\,(1 - \nu^2)} \quad \text{(N m)}.$$

The factor $1/(1 - \nu^2)$ appears because a plate, unlike a narrow beam, cannot contract sideways freely when it bends. The two stiffnesses have different units, so compare them through their ratio, which has units of one over area:

$$\frac{E\,t}{D} = \frac{12\,(1 - \nu^2)}{t^2}.$$

The material drops out entirely. Only the thickness matters, and it matters squared. For a 1 mm sheet with $\nu = 0.3$ the ratio is $1.09\times10^{7}$ m-2; for 2 mm it is a quarter of that, $2.73\times10^{6}$ m-2. Halve the thickness and bending becomes four times easier relative to stretching.

Put it into a picture you can feel. A strip 10 cm long, clamped at one end: pulling its tip along its length needs a stiffness $E t b/L$; pushing it sideways, as a cantilever, needs $3 D b/L^3$. Their ratio is $4(1 - \nu^2) (L/t)^2$, which for a 1 mm sheet is 36,400. Pushing the tip sideways is more than thirty thousand times easier than pulling it. Or compare energies: bending a 1 mm sheet into a curve of radius 10 cm stores as much energy as stretching it by 0.3 %.

Why tablecloths fold

That huge ratio has a geometric consequence that you can see in the demo. A flat sheet can be bent into a cylinder or a cone without stretching anywhere: those surfaces are developable. It cannot be shaped over a sphere without stretching, because a sphere is curved in two directions at once (Gauss's Theorema Egregium: you cannot flatten an orange peel without tearing it). A stiff-in-stretch, soft-in-bend sheet laid over a ball therefore does the only cheap thing available: it bends in one direction, like the dihedral cloth at compliance 1, or, if bending is cheap enough, it gathers into folds, each of which is locally a cylinder. A tablecloth over a round table, a skirt, a flag: all their folds are thin shells avoiding stretch.

From material to compliance

To set the compliances from a real sheet, match the discrete stiffnesses to the continuous ones.

  • On a square grid stretched along its rows, one link carries the load of a strip of width $\ell$ and has length $\ell$, so the link stiffness equals the membrane stiffness per width: $k_{\text{link}} = E t$, and $\alpha_{\text{stretch}} = 1/(E t)$.
  • Bend the grid into a cylinder of radius $R$ about one of its axes. On this triangulation, the hinges across one set of grid edges each turn by $\ell/R$ and the others not at all, one hinge per square of area $\ell^2$. The hinge energy per area is then $\tfrac12 k_\theta(\ell/R)^2/\ell^2$, and setting it equal to the plate's $\tfrac12 D/R^2$ gives $k_\theta = D$, so $\alpha_{\text{bend}} = 1/D$. On other meshes the factor differs (this is what the edge length and height weights in Discrete Shells take care of), but it stays of order one.

The demo inverts this and reports, from your two compliances, the thickness of the plate they represent: $t = \sqrt{12(1-\nu^2)\,\alpha_{\text{stretch}}/\alpha_{\text{bend}}}$. Real fabrics come out thinner than they are, because their threads slide and twist past each other and bend far more easily than a solid plate of the same thickness. A cotton with $E t \approx 1000$ N/m and $D \approx 10^{-5}$ N m behaves like a solid plate a third of a millimetre thick.

Why cloth simulation is hard in one sentence. The ratio $E t/D$ is in the millions per square metre, so a cloth is a system whose stretching modes are thousands of times stiffer than its bending modes, and the fast modes set the time step (the integration chapter's $h\omega$). Position-based methods made cloth practical in real time precisely because they treat the stiff stretch as a near-hard constraint instead of a fast spring.

Anisotropy, rest shapes, wind and pins

Anisotropy. Woven fabric has two thread directions, the warp and the weft, and is stiff along both. Pulled at 45°, along the bias, it stretches easily, because the weave can shear. In our model that is simply a softer shear compliance than stretch compliance (the default is $10^{-3}$ m/N against $10^{-7}$). Give the horizontal and vertical links different compliances and you have a fabric with different warp and weft. Knits are stretchy along both threads and want soft stretch too.

Rest shapes. A nonzero rest angle $\theta_0$ per hinge, measured from a curved initial mesh, gives a shell with a natural shape: a leaf, a cap, the curl of a ribbon. The demo measures every hinge's rest angle when it builds the cloth.

Wind and drag. Air pushes on a surface mostly along its normal. For each triangle with unit normal $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$, area $A$ and average velocity $\mathbf{v}$, a simple linear model applies a force $\mathbf{F} = -c\,A\,\big((\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_{\text{wind}})\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\big)\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}$, a third to each corner. A sail moving with the wind feels nothing; a sail edge-on to it feels nothing either. The demo uses $c = 1$ kg/(m2s) and caps each corner's velocity change so the drag can stop motion but never reverse it, which keeps it stable even on badly stretched triangles. Real air drag is closer to quadratic in speed, and real flags flutter because of vortices this model ignores, but it gives convincing billowing.

Collisions. The sphere and the floor use the particle-against-surface push-out and positional friction from the previous chapter, with a contact thickness of 8 mm so the cloth does not show through. The cloth does not collide with itself; adding that means the spatial hash of the collision chapter plus a minimum distance between particles, and, for reliable results, care about tunnelling, since cloth is the thinnest object you will ever simulate.

Tearing

Tearing is a rule about constraints: if a structural or shear constraint is stretched beyond a threshold, say 1.3 times its rest length, delete it. Everything that depended on it has to go too: the triangles that used the edge (so they are no longer drawn and no longer catch wind), the hinges between those triangles, and the second-neighbour constraints that spanned the edge.

for (const c of stretchAndShear)
  if (c.alive && dist(c.a, c.b) > tearAt * c.rest) c.alive = false;
// then: a triangle is alive only if its three edges are; a hinge only if both its triangles are

Turn on tearing and pull on the cloth. Grab a single particle and pull, and the particle tears out on its own: all the force goes through its handful of links, a stress concentration. Real tears also start where stress concentrates, at a notch or a hole: once a hole exists, the links around its rim carry more than their share of the load, which is why tears grow from holes.

Deleting constraints leaves the particles shared between the two sides of a tear, so a torn edge is ragged and the separated flaps still share corner particles. Production cloth tears by splitting instead: duplicate the particles along the crack and give each side its own copy, so the mesh separates cleanly.

Solution

Compare with solution.js. The constraint is the code in "A grid of particles" above; the shear constraints are:

for (let j = 0; j < N - 1; j++) for (let i = 0; i < N - 1; i++) {
  addDistance(shear, id(i, j), id(i + 1, j + 1));
  addDistance(shear, id(i + 1, j), id(i, j + 1));
}

With very soft shear the cloth hugs the ball much more closely. A grid whose squares can turn into rhombi can cover a doubly curved surface without stretching any thread (a Chebyshev net; it is how a woven fabric is fitted over a curved shape), so the shear mechanism lets it avoid the folds that a shear-stiff sheet needs. Solution 
for (const list of [stretch, shear]) {
  for (const c of list) {
    if (c.alive && dist(c.a, c.b) > tearAt.value * c.rest) { c.alive = false; tornCount++; }

}

At a low threshold the cloth tears at the pinned corners first: the whole weight of the cloth, and the jerk of the swing, passes through the few links around each pin, which is also where the "largest stretch" readout peaks.

from edges to the deformation gradient

None of the three papers simulates cloth, but this chapter's central move, giving each kind of deformation its own stiffness taken from the material, is the one the MPM chapters make in full. A distance constraint measures the stretch of one edge. The MPM course notes measure all stretching and shearing at once with the deformation gradient $\mathbf{F}$, and their hyperelastic energy densities $\Psi(\mathbf{F})$ play the role of our separate compliances: the Lamé parameter $\mu$ prices shear and the Lamé $\lambda$ prices volume change, both computed from $E$ and $\nu$ exactly as we computed $Et$ and $D$. When you reach The Deformation Gradient, think of $\mathbf{F}$ as every edge of this chapter's grid measured in one matrix.

Summary

  • Cloth is a grid of particles with stretch, shear and bending constraints, each with its own XPBD compliance. Shear constraints remove the free rhombus mechanism of a square grid.
  • Second-neighbour bending is cheap but its energy grows as $\theta^4$: small folds are free and it also resists compression. Dihedral-angle bending, $C = \theta - \theta_0$ with Bridson's gradients, is quadratic in the angle like a real plate.
  • A thin shell has membrane stiffness $\sim Et$ and bending stiffness $D = Et^3/(12(1-\nu^2))$. Their ratio, $12(1-\nu^2)/t^2$, depends only on thickness: thin sheets bend easily and barely stretch.
  • Sheets avoid stretch by bending into developable shapes or folds; that is why cloth over a ball folds.
  • On a square grid, $\alpha_{\text{stretch}} \approx 1/(Et)$ and $\alpha_{\text{bend}} \approx 1/D$.
  • Below a certain compliance the stretch you see is set by solver convergence, not by the material.
  • Tearing deletes over-stretched constraints and everything that depended on them; splitting particles gives cleaner tears.
