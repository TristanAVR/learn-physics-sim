Position-Based MPM

MPM rewritten as a position-based constraint solver: stable at any time step, at the price of softness when it has not converged.

You now know two ways to step MPM, and neither suits a game. Explicit MPM (chapter 24) is simple, but its time step is tied to the speed of sound in the material, and when it is exceeded the simulation explodes. Implicit MPM (chapter 26) takes any step, but it needs second derivatives of every material, a linear solver, a line search, and care with indefinite Hessians, and it is not cheap. A game has a harder requirement than either: whatever the player does, even crushing water between two moving walls, the simulation must not blow up, and it must cost a fixed, small amount per frame.

Position-based dynamics (chapters 06 to 08) made exactly that trade for cloth and ropes: give up some accuracy, in the form of softness when the solver has not converged, in exchange for stability at any step. Chris Lewin's Position Based Material Point Method (SIGGRAPH 2024) makes the same trade for MPM, with a surprisingly small change to the explicit loop. This chapter builds it: liquid, elastic and sand constraints, the iteration loop, and the integration step, following the open-source reference code closely.

By the end you will be able to implement PB-MPM, explain why it is stable at any time step and why it is soft when unconverged, predict how the softness changes with the step and the iteration count, compare its relaxation factors with XPBD's compliance, and map every line of the paper's two algorithms to code.

From velocities to displacements

PB-MPM works with displacements instead of velocities, the way PBD works with positions. Each particle carries

  • a displacement $d_p$: how far it will move during this step, in metres; for a particle moving with velocity $v_p$, $d_p = h\,v_p$;
  • a deformation displacement $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p$: the gradient of the displacement field around it, a dimensionless 2 by 2 matrix. In APIC terms it is $h\,\mathbf C_p$. The step will change the particle's deformation gradient to $(\mathbf I + \mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p)\,\mathbf F_p$, exactly as $(\mathbf I + h\nabla v_p)\mathbf F_p$ in chapter 24.

Two different D's. The PB-MPM paper and code call the deformation displacement $\mathbf D$. The APIC paper and the MPM course use $\mathbf D_p$ for something unrelated: the inertia-like matrix $\sum_i w_{ip}(x_i - x_p)(x_i - x_p)^T$, which is $\frac14\Delta x^2\mathbf I$ for quadratic B-splines. On this site we write $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p$ for PB-MPM's quantity. When you read the paper, its $\mathbf D_i$ (subscript $i$ for a particle, not a grid node) is our $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p$.

With $h$ folded into the particle state, the MLS transfers of chapter 24 carry over unchanged, multiplied by $h$:

$$m_i = \sum_p w_{ip}\,m_p, \qquad (m d)_i = \sum_p w_{ip}\,m_p\Big(d_p + \mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p(x_i - x_p)\Big),$$ $$d_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\,d_i, \qquad \mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p = \frac{4}{\Delta x^2}\sum_i w_{ip}\,d_i\,(x_i - x_p)^T.$$

Gravity enters the way it does in PBD's prediction step: at the end of each step every particle's displacement for the next step gets $g\,h^2$ added, so $d_p = h v_p + h^2 g$ is the predicted motion if nothing pushed back. Notice what is missing: there is no stress, no force, and no $h f/m$ anywhere. That is the whole idea.

The loop

Explicit MPM does one P2G, one grid update and one G2P per step, with the stress as a force. PB-MPM does the same transfers several times per step, $K$ iterations of them, and before each P2G it lets every particle edit its own $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p$ toward a shape it would like to have. Only after the iterations does it update $\mathbf F$ and move the particles:

function stepPBMPM(h) {
  for (let it = 0; it < iterations.value; it++) {
    solveConstraints();   // each particle edits its own D_def toward a target (Algorithm 2)
    particleToGrid();     // mass and displacement momentum, with the affine term D_def (x_i - x_p)
    gridUpdate();         // d_i = (m d)_i / m_i, then walls
    gridToParticle();     // new d_p and D_def from the grid
  }
  integrate(h);           // F, plasticity, x += d, d += g h^2
}

The grid's job has changed. In explicit MPM it integrates forces. Here it reconciles: each particle has said, through its $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p$, how it would like the material around it to move, and neighbouring particles disagree. P2G averages their wishes into one displacement per node; G2P hands every particle back a displacement field that its neighbours can agree with. Repeat, and the wishes converge toward a motion that satisfies everyone as well as possible. This is a Jacobi constraint solver, with the grid as the averaging step.

Run the demo at its defaults: 240 steps per second, 4 iterations, water, a jelly block and a column of sand. Then drag the time step to 33 ms (30 Hz) and the iterations to 1. Nothing explodes, ever; but the jelly flattens and the water loses most of its depth. Switch the solver to explicit MPM, with a stiffness chosen to hold the water at a similar volume, and it blows up at the default step: it needs steps below 1.1 ms.

Constraints edit the deformation displacement

Here is the key observation from the paper. A particle's deformation gradient at the end of the step will be

$$\mathbf F^* = (\mathbf I + \mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p)\,\mathbf F_p.$$

So if we want the particle to end the step with some target deformation $\mathbf A$, we know exactly which deformation displacement would produce it: solve $(\mathbf I + \mathbf D^{\text{def}})\mathbf F_p = \mathbf A$ to get $\mathbf D^{\text{def}} = \mathbf A\mathbf F_p^{-1} - \mathbf I$. A constraint, in PB-MPM, is a rule for choosing $\mathbf A$ and a relaxation factor $\omega$ for how far to move toward it:

$$\mathbf D^{\text{def}} \leftarrow \mathbf D^{\text{def}} + \omega\,\big(\mathbf A\,\mathbf F_p^{-1} - \mathbf I - \mathbf D^{\text{def}}\big).$$

With $\omega = 1$ the particle asks for its target outright; with $\omega < 1$ it asks for part of it; with $1 < \omega < 2$ it over-relaxes, which speeds up a Jacobi solve the way successive over-relaxation does. Either way, the edit is local (one particle, no neighbours), cheap, and bounded: it moves toward a perfectly sensible deformation.

Liquids

A liquid has no rest shape, only a rest volume. Each liquid particle tracks its volume ratio $J$ (it never needs $\mathbf F$, as in chapter 24's water). For small $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}$, $\det(\mathbf I + \mathbf D^{\text{def}}) \approx 1 + \operatorname{tr}\mathbf D^{\text{def}}$, so after the step the volume ratio will be about $(1 + \operatorname{tr}\mathbf D^{\text{def}})\,J$. We want that to be 1. Adding a uniform expansion $\alpha\mathbf I$ to $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}$ raises the trace by $2\alpha$ in 2D, so

$$\big(1 + \operatorname{tr}\mathbf D^{\text{def}} + 2\alpha\big)\,J = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \alpha = \tfrac12\Big(\frac1J - 1 - \operatorname{tr}\mathbf D^{\text{def}}\Big).$$

The edit is $\mathbf D^{\text{def}} \leftarrow \mathbf D^{\text{def}} + \omega_{\text{liquid}}\,\alpha\,\mathbf I$: a purely hydrostatic correction, a pressure in all but name. Viscosity is just as simple: a viscous liquid resists deforming, so remove a fraction $\nu_{\text{visc}}$ of the symmetric (deforming) part of $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}$ and keep the antisymmetric (rotating) part. The reference code applies the viscosity first and then the volume term:

const sOff = 0.5 * (D01 + D10);                     // viscosity: D -= visc * sym(D)
D00 -= visc * D00; D11 -= visc * D11; D01 -= visc * sOff; D10 -= visc * sOff;
const alpha = 0.5 * (1 / pJ[p] - 1 - (D00 + D11));  // volume: drive (1 + tr D) J toward 1
D00 += relL * alpha; D11 += relL * alpha;

This is incompressibility stated directly, with no equation of state and no bulk modulus, which is why the paper says the liquid case works well. It has one weakness. $J$ is integrated from $\operatorname{tr}\mathbf D^{\text{def}}$ every step, and when the solver has not converged, the integrated $J$ drifts away from how tightly the particles are really packed. We measured it with the correction below switched off. At 240 Hz with 4 iterations, the particles' mean $J$ claimed a 5 % volume loss while the settled pool had actually lost 39 % of its depth; at 60 Hz, the mean $J$ climbed to 1.86 (the particles believed they were stretched) while the pool held a third of its original area.

The cure is an objective volume measure from the grid. In P2G, scatter each particle's volume $V_p^0$ with its weights; in G2P, gather it back: $\phi_p = \sum_i w_{ip} \sum_q w_{iq} V_q^0 / \Delta x^2$. At rest density, with four particles per cell, $\phi_p = 1$. If $\phi_p > 1$, the particles really are packed too tightly, and $J$ is pulled 10 % of the way toward the grid's answer $1/\phi_p$ every iteration. The correction is applied only under compression, because at a free surface $\phi_p$ drops below 1 for reasons that have nothing to do with volume (there are simply no particles above). The demo's checkbox turns it off so you can see the drift.

Elastic solids

An elastic solid wants two things: to keep its shape and to keep its volume. Both are easy targets. Take the candidate $\mathbf F^* = (\mathbf I + \mathbf D^{\text{def}})\mathbf F_p$ and

  • for shape, use its closest rotation, $\mathbf A_{\text{shape}} = \mathbf R$ from the polar decomposition: "be undeformed, but feel free to rotate";
  • for volume, use $\mathbf F^*$ scaled to unit determinant, $\mathbf A_{\text{vol}} = \mathbf F^* / \sqrt{\det\mathbf F^*}$ in 2D: "keep whatever shape you have, but at the right area".

Unlike the liquid's two terms, these are not independent (both change the volume and the shape), so they are blended with an elasticity ratio $\beta$: $\mathbf A = \beta\mathbf A_{\text{shape}} + (1-\beta)\mathbf A_{\text{vol}}$, and $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}$ is moved toward $\mathbf A\mathbf F_p^{-1} - \mathbf I$. With $\beta = 1$ the target is a rotation, which preserves shape and area; with $\beta = 0$ the material keeps its area but no shape at all, like a liquid that remembers nothing. The code clamps $|\det\mathbf F^*|$ to $[0.1, 1000]$ before the square root, so a crushed particle cannot produce a huge target.

compose(1, 1, T);                                   // shape target: R = U V^T
const det = Fs[0] * Fs[3] - Fs[1] * Fs[2];          // Fs = F* = (I + D) F
const q = 1 / ((det < 0 ? -1 : 1) * Math.sqrt(Math.min(Math.max(Math.abs(det), 0.1), 1000)));
for (let i = 0; i < 4; i++) T[i] = beta * T[i] + (1 - beta) * q * Fs[i];   // A
// D += relE * (A F^-1 - I - D)

There is no Young's modulus. A PB-MPM jelly is as stiff as the solver can make it: with enough iterations it behaves like a nearly rigid body that can still bend under large loads, because the iterations never fully converge. Its apparent softness is set by the time step and the iteration count, which is the subject of a later section.

Sand

The reference code treats sand as an elastic solid with two twists. Its shape target keeps the singular values of $\mathbf F^*$ but clamps them to at least 1 (and the volume target only scales down, never up): sand resists being compressed, not being stretched. If the particle still has stored dilation from earlier (chapter 25's volume correction), the clamp is skipped and the particle does not resist at all. Then, in the integration step, the full Drucker-Prager return mapping of chapter 25 decides how much of the deformation is forgotten.

Integrating the step

After the $K$ iterations, every particle has a displacement $d_p$ and a deformation displacement $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p$ that the grid has reconciled. The integration step applies them, once:

  1. Liquid: $J \leftarrow J\,(1 + \operatorname{tr}\mathbf D^{\text{def}})$, clamped to at least 0.1.
  2. Solids: $\mathbf F \leftarrow (\mathbf I + \mathbf D^{\text{def}})\,\mathbf F$. Then the safety net: the paper deletes particles whose $\mathbf F$ has a condition number above $10^6$ (large steps can produce wildly distorted particles), and the code clamps the singular values to $[0.2, 10^4]$. Then plasticity: Drucker-Prager for sand.
  3. Move: $x_p \leftarrow x_p + d_p$.
  4. Gravity for the next step: $d_p \leftarrow d_p + g\,h^2$.

One practical detail: $d_p$ and $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p$ are displacements over one step of length $h$. If the step changes, scale both by $h_{\text{new}}/h_{\text{old}}$, or the material will suddenly speed up or slow down. The demo does this when you move the slider.

The grid update needs one more ingredient, the walls. PB-MPM collides the displaced grid positions: node $i$ would move to $x_i + d_i$, and if that point would be outside the box, the node's displacement is set to zero. Since displacements are bounded by construction, so is this check.

Why it is stable at any step

Follow what can grow. In explicit MPM, the update $v_i \mathrel{+}= h f_i / m_i$ multiplies a force by $h$; a stiff material and a large $h$ give an update that overshoots, and the overshoot feeds the next force: that is the instability of chapter 03, in thousands of springs. PB-MPM has no such term. Each iteration does three things to the displacements, none of which can amplify them:

  • The constraint edit moves $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p$ part of the way toward a target that is a valid, bounded deformation (a rotation, or a matrix of determinant 1 with a clamped scale). It is a blend, not an extrapolation, as long as the relaxation stays below 2.
  • P2G followed by G2P is an averaging: B-spline weights are positive and sum to one, so the gathered $d_p$ is a weighted average of node displacements, each of which is a mass-weighted average of particle displacements. A filter cannot create large values from small ones; it is the same property that makes PIC so stable and so dissipative.
  • The walls only ever remove displacement.

The only thing that adds energy is gravity, $g h^2$ per step, and it is bounded too. So whatever the step, the iteration produces a motion that is a blend of reasonable wishes. It may be a wrong motion if the iterations have not converged, but it is never a wild one. In PBD terms, it is a sequence of projections, not of forces. In the demo, PB-MPM stayed stable at every step we tried, up to 34 ms with a single iteration; the explicit solver, with a water bulk modulus of 100 kPa ($c$ = 10 m/s), blows up above 1.13 ms.

Why it is soft when unconverged

The price is softness. In a Jacobi solve, information travels about one grid cell per iteration: a particle at the top of a column only learns that the floor is there after enough iterations for the floor's correction to reach it. Our water column is 35 cells tall. Meanwhile gravity adds $g h^2$ of downward displacement every step, 2.7 mm at 60 Hz but only 0.17 mm at 240 Hz. If the iterations cannot propagate the floor's support through the column within a step, the column compresses until the volume errors it builds up are large enough to push back. The result looks like a material that is softer than it should be.

We measured it, with the grid volume correction on, as the mean $J$ of a settled pool:

Read along the diagonals. Halving the time step gives exactly the same volume as four times the iterations: 1/120 s with $K$ = 1 matches 1/60 s with $K$ = 4, and so on down the table. The volume error depends on $h^2/K$, and fits roughly $1 - J \propto h/\sqrt K$. The elastic block agrees: resting under its own weight, its height (rest 0.156 m) is 0.037, 0.094, 0.132 and 0.140 m at 1/60 s with $K$ = 1, 4, 16 and 32, and 0.134 m at 1/240 s with $K$ = 1, the same as 1/60 s with $K$ = 16.

Small steps beat many iterations. Halving $h$ doubles the cost per simulated second; quadrupling $K$ quadruples it; they buy the same accuracy. So for PB-MPM, as for XPBD in the substepping chapter, the efficient way to stiffen a material is more steps with fewer iterations each. The demo's default, 240 Hz with 4 iterations, follows that advice.

The scale matters too. What sets the softness is how far gravity moves the material per step, measured in cells: $g h^2 / \Delta x$ is 0.17 for our 1 m tank at 60 Hz. A 10 m dam on a grid of the same resolution has a ten times larger $\Delta x$, a ten times smaller ratio, and looks much stiffer at the same step and iteration count. A position-based method is at its best when the scene is large compared with $g h^2$.

Relaxation factors and compliance

PB-MPM's relaxation factors play the role of PBD's stiffness parameter $k \in [0, 1]$ from chapter 06, and they have the same flaw: what they mean depends on the time step and the iteration count. The paper says so plainly, calls them a crude control, and notes that an XPBD variant of the method is very likely possible.

Recall what XPBD did (chapter 07). It gave each constraint a compliance $\alpha$, the inverse of a stiffness, and a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ accumulated over the iterations; the update solved for $\Delta\lambda = -(C + \tilde\alpha\lambda)/(\nabla C^T \mathbf M^{-1}\nabla C + \tilde\alpha)$ with $\tilde\alpha = \alpha/h^2$. In the converged limit the material then behaves with the physical stiffness $1/\alpha$, independent of $h$ and $K$, and the unconverged softness only appears on top of it. For the liquid, that would mean a constraint $C = (1 + \operatorname{tr}\mathbf D^{\text{def}})J - 1$ with a compliance equal to the inverse bulk modulus: the water would compress by $\rho g d / K$ at depth $d$, as chapter 24's equation of state does, but without any time step limit. The hard part, and the reason it is research rather than a homework exercise, is that the "effective mass" seen by one particle's constraint, the analogue of $\nabla C^T\mathbf M^{-1}\nabla C$, depends on its neighbours through the grid.

What it costs

One PB-MPM iteration is a constraint pass, P2G, a grid update and G2P: almost exactly the work of one explicit step. In our code an iteration takes 0.38 ms and an explicit step 0.43 ms, matching the paper's estimate.

The paper's headline comparison: in a dam break, PB-MPM was stable at 30 Hz with one iteration, while an explicit solver with a stiffness chosen to hold the same steady-state volume needed 240 Hz to survive the splash. On our scene the explicit solver needs about 890 Hz. PB-MPM at 30 Hz with one iteration is therefore 25 times cheaper per simulated second, and perfectly stable, but our small tank of water is then squashed to a puddle with a mean $J$ of about 0.26. At 240 Hz with 4 iterations its mean $J$ is 0.92, and it costs about the same as the explicit solver. That is the honest trade: PB-MPM does not make an accurate answer cheaper; it makes a stable answer available at any budget, and lets you buy accuracy with steps and iterations.

Algorithm 1, line by line

The paper's Algorithm 1, Position Based MPM, marks the lines that differ from explicit MPM. In solution.js:

  • Line 1, UpdatePBMPM(P), one step for the particle set $P$: stepPBMPM(h).
  • Line 2, the iteration loop (new compared with explicit MPM): for (let it = 0; it < iterations.value; it++).
  • Line 3, SolveConstraints(P) (new): solveConstraints(), which is Algorithm 2.
  • Line 4, ParticleToGrid(P): particleToGrid(), mass and $m\,w\,(d + \mathbf D^{\text{def}}(x_i - x_p))$.
  • Line 5, GridUpdate(G): gridUpdate(), normalisation and the displaced-position wall test.
  • Line 6, GridToParticle(P, G): gridToParticle(), $d_p$, $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p = \frac{4}{\Delta x^2}\sum_i w\,d_i(x_i - x_p)^T$ and the grid volume measure.
  • Line 8, IntegrateParticles(P), after the loop: integrate(h).

The paper writes the algorithm without a time step or substeps. In the reference code (sim.js), each frame runs some number of substeps, and each substep runs the iteration loop of particle update, grid clear, P2G, grid update and G2P, then the integration shader, exactly our structure with one step per substep.

Algorithm 2 (constraint solve), line by line

Algorithm 2 handles two materials. Its symbols, translated: $p_i$ is particle $p$; $\mathbf D_i$ is $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p$; $L_i$ is the tracked liquid density ratio, which is $1/J$ in our code (the code stores the volume ratio and calls it liquidDensity); $\alpha_{\text{hydro}}$ and $\alpha_{\text{visc}}$ are our liquidRelax and viscosity; $\beta$ is the elasticity ratio. In solveConstraints():

  • Lines 2 and 3, the loop over particles and the liquid test: for (let p ...) and if (pmat[p] === LIQUID).
  • Line 4, $c \leftarrow \operatorname{tr}\mathbf D_i$, and line 5, $\mathbf D_{\text{hydro}} = \mathbf I(L_i - 1 - c)$: our alpha = 0.5 * (1 / pJ[p] - 1 - (D00 + D11)). The code has the factor $\frac12$ that 2D needs (the trace of $\alpha\mathbf I$ is $2\alpha$); the paper's line leaves it to the relaxation factor.
  • Line 6, $\mathbf D_{\text{visc}}$: the paper's text says viscosity drives the off-diagonal elements toward zero. The code subtracts a fraction of the whole symmetric part, $\frac12(\mathbf D + \mathbf D^T)$, and we follow the code: the sOff lines.
  • Line 7, adding both with their relaxation factors: the code applies viscosity first, then computes the hydrostatic term from the damped matrix.
  • Line 8, the elastic test: the else branch.
  • Line 9, $\mathbf F^* \leftarrow \mathbf F_i(\mathbf I + \mathbf D_i)$: we use $(\mathbf I + \mathbf D^{\text{def}})\mathbf F$, as the code does and as the MPM update $\mathbf F \leftarrow (\mathbf I + h\nabla v)\mathbf F$ requires; the order in the paper's formula is a notational slip with no effect on the idea.
  • Line 10, $\mathbf A_{\text{shape}}$ from the polar decomposition: svd2 and compose(1, 1, T).
  • Line 11, $\mathbf A_{\text{vol}} = \mathbf F^*/\det(\mathbf F^*)$: in 2D the unit-determinant version is $\mathbf F^*/\sqrt{\det\mathbf F^*}$ (in 3D, the cube root), which is what the code computes, with the clamp.
  • Line 12, the new $\mathbf D_i$ from the blended target: the paper writes $\mathbf F_i^{-1}\mathbf A - \mathbf I$ to match its line 9 ordering; with our ordering it is $\mathbf A\mathbf F^{-1} - \mathbf I$. The paper sets $\mathbf D_i$ to it directly; the code moves part way with elasticRelaxation. The paper's text and line 12 also assign $\beta$ to different targets; in the code the elasticity ratio weights the shape target, as in our T[i] = beta * T[i] + (1 - beta) * q * Fs[i].

The code adds a sand branch that the paper does not describe (the clamped shape target above) and a visco-plastic material that differs from elastic only in its integration step (chapter 25). The integration step itself is described in prose in the paper (section 2.2 for the deletion rule, section 2.1 for the liquid's grid volume) and in full in particleIntegrate.wgsl.

At $\beta = 0$ the target is "your current shape, at unit area": nothing pulls the material back to its rest shape, so it flows, but it keeps its volume.

Summary

  • PB-MPM particles carry a displacement $d_p = h v_p$ and a deformation displacement $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p = h\mathbf C_p$ (the paper's $\mathbf D$, not APIC's $\mathbf D_p$). Gravity enters as $d \mathrel{+}= g h^2$.
  • Each step runs $K$ iterations of: constraints edit $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}_p$ per particle, P2G of displacement momentum, grid update with displaced-position walls, G2P. Then it integrates $\mathbf F$ (or $J$), plasticity and positions once.
  • A constraint picks a target deformation $\mathbf A$ and moves $\mathbf D^{\text{def}}$ toward $\mathbf A\mathbf F^{-1} - \mathbf I$: a hydrostatic $\alpha\mathbf I$ for liquids, a blend of rotation and unit-determinant targets for solids. Liquids need an objective volume measure from the grid.
  • Stable at any step, because every operation is a bounded blend or an average; soft when unconverged, because the Jacobi solve propagates about one cell per iteration. The error depends on $h^2/K$, so small steps are cheaper than many iterations.
  • Relaxation factors behave like PBD's stiffness parameter; an XPBD-style compliance would make stiffness independent of $h$ and $K$. One iteration costs about one explicit step.
