Reading: APIC (2015)

A guided tour of the paper that made particle-grid transfers stop losing spin.

You have built PIC, FLIP and APIC yourself in chapters 22 and 23. Now it is time to read the paper where APIC first appeared. It is ten pages long, it is well written, and most of it will feel familiar. But research papers are compressed: a result that took the authors weeks is stated in one sentence, the proofs live in a supplementary document, and the notation is theirs, not ours.

This chapter is a reading companion. Keep the paper open beside it and go section by section. For each section you will find what it says, what to read slowly and what you can skim, the key equations restated in this site's notation, and full derivations of the results the paper only states. Short "check your understanding" questions follow each major section, with answers you can unfold.

By the end you should be able to read the paper unaided, explain why $\mathbf{D}_p = \tfrac14\Delta x^2\mathbf{I}$ for quadratic B-splines without looking it up, and prove to a sceptical colleague that APIC conserves angular momentum.

the paper

Chenfanfu Jiang, Craig Schroeder, Andrew Selle, Joseph Teran, Alexey Stomakhin. The Affine Particle-In-Cell Method. ACM Transactions on Graphics 34(4), Article 51, 2015 (SIGGRAPH 2015). DOI 10.1145/2766996. The authors were at the University of California, Los Angeles and Walt Disney Animation Studios. The paper refers to a supplementary technical document for its proofs; a later journal paper by Jiang, Schroeder and Teran (2017, listed under further reading) contains the full analysis.

What the paper is, and where it sits

Every hybrid particle-grid method faces the same awkward moment twice per step. Particles carry the material around; the grid is where forces, pressure and collisions are computed. So each step you copy velocity from particles to grid (P2G), do the physics on the grid, and copy it back (G2P). Information is lost in those copies, and how much is lost decides how the simulation looks.

The history in one table:

YearMethodWhat it changed
1964PIC (Harlow)Particles carry mass and position; velocity is interpolated back from the grid each step. Stable, but heavily dissipative: everything looks like honey.
1986FLIP (Brackbill and Ruppel)Particles keep their own velocity and receive only the grid's change in velocity. Lively, but noisy and sometimes unstable.
1994-95MPM (Sulsky, Chen and Schreyer; Sulsky, Zhou and Schreyer)PIC extended to solids: particles also carry deformation and stress history.
2005Zhu and BridsonFLIP brought to graphics liquids and sand, with a PIC/FLIP blend to tame the noise.
2013Stomakhin et al.MPM for snow, developed for feature-film production; MPM becomes a graphics staple.
2015APIC (this paper)Particles carry a small matrix describing the local velocity field. Stable like PIC, nearly as lively as FLIP, and angular momentum is conserved exactly.
2016MPM course notesThe standard tutorial, recommending APIC transfers (chapter 29).
2018MLS-MPM (Hu et al.)Reuses the APIC matrix as the velocity gradient, fusing the force computation into P2G.
2024PB-MPM (Lewin)A position-based solver on top of APIC-style transfers (chapter 30).

The problem the paper solves, in plain words: before 2015, every simulation forced a choice between PIC (stable but syrupy, and spinning objects slow down as if stirred in treacle) and FLIP (energetic but noisy, with particles that jitter and clump). Practitioners blended the two and tuned the blend per shot. APIC removes the choice. Its idea is almost embarrassingly small: a particle sees several grid nodes with different velocities, and PIC throws all but their average away. Keep the slope too.

First, the paper's $\mathbf{D}_p$ is not the deformation displacement of PB-MPM, which that paper also writes $\mathbf{D}$; on this site we call the PB-MPM matrix $\mathbf{D}^{\text{def}}_p$ (see chapter 30). If your code differentiates with respect to $\mathbf{x}_i$ instead, every gradient flips sign.

Abstract and section 1: Introduction

Read the introduction carefully; it is the clearest short account of the PIC/FLIP dilemma in the literature. It makes three points.

  1. PIC dissipates because each grid-to-particle copy averages away detail. The most visible symptom is lost angular momentum: a spinning object slows down as if in a viscous fluid.
  2. FLIP is noisy because of the ringing instability. There are usually more particles than grid nodes, so some patterns of particle velocity are invisible to the grid: they transfer to zero grid velocity and receive no response. PIC wipes such patterns out each step (it is a true filter); FLIP keeps them on the particles, where they persist and grow. You saw these "null modes" in chapter 22.
  3. The blend of the two (Zhu and Bridson 2005) reintroduces dissipation and needs per-scene tuning.

The last paragraph of the introduction is the thesis: keep PIC's pure filtering, but stop throwing information away by giving each particle a locally affine description of the velocity, a $3\times3$ matrix in 3D. Note the claim precisely: the paper does not say APIC cures the ringing instability; it says APIC avoids the instability that FLIP adds on top of it. Section 9 comes back to this.

The paragraph on angular momentum mentions that FLIP would conserve angular momentum with a "consistent" (full, non-diagonal) mass matrix, but that matrix can be singular, so in practice everyone uses the lumped (diagonal) one. You can skim this; it matters again in the MPM course, section 9.2 (chapter 29).

Check your understanding

1. Why is PIC called a "filter" and FLIP not?

In PIC, the new particle velocity is computed only from grid values, and the grid values are computed only from particle values. Everything passes through the grid, which has fewer degrees of freedom than the particles, so any particle pattern the grid cannot represent is removed. In FLIP the new particle velocity is the old particle velocity plus a grid increment, so the old particle state has a direct path to the new one that bypasses the grid. Patterns the grid cannot see survive along that path.

2. If PIC filters, why does the paper say the ringing instability exists "to a lesser extent" in PIC too?

Because the particles move after each step. Even if the transfer removed every invisible pattern, the new particle positions define a new set of weights, and the same velocities can contain new invisible patterns relative to the new weights. The filter is applied once per step; motion recreates what it removed.

Section 2: Previous work

Skim on a first pass. Two items are worth a note in the margin: Edwards and Bridson's discontinuous-Galerkin work, which the authors call similar in spirit because it also stores more data per cell, and an SPH method whose particles carry angular momentum samples, close in spirit to RPIC in section 5.2.

Section 3: Method outline

This section is short and important. Its point is that PIC, FLIP and APIC share one time-step loop: kinematics on particles, dynamics on the grid, and only the two transfers differ. Figure 7 shows the data flow of the three methods. In words: in PIC and APIC the arrows go particle to grid to particle, a closed loop through the grid; in FLIP there is an extra arrow from the old particle state straight to the new particle state, which the authors label the "unsafe path".

The figure also contains a viewpoint you should adopt: a transfer can be read as the grid averaging particles, or, equivalently, as each particle collecting a weighted set of grid values. In the second view a particle is a fuzzy blob that overlaps several nodes, and a fuzzy blob can sensibly own a spin or a shear. That viewpoint is what makes RPIC and APIC feel natural.

Read the paragraph that begins with the key observation: a particle receives data from several nodes but keeps only one constant value. That sentence is the whole paper.

Section 5.1: PIC

The standard transfers, equations (1) and (2):

$$m_i^n = \sum_p w_{ip}^n m_p, \qquad m_i^n\mathbf{v}_i^n = \sum_p w_{ip}^n m_p \mathbf{v}_p^n, \tag{1}$$ $$\mathbf{v}_p^{n+1} = \sum_i w_{ip}^n\,\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i^{n+1}. \tag{2}$$

Term by term: node $i$ collects a weighted share $w_{ip}m_p$ of every nearby particle's mass, and the same share of its momentum; dividing gives the grid velocity, a mass-weighted average. After the grid update, each particle's new velocity is the weighted average of its nodes' velocities. Nothing else is kept.

Then equation (3) defines total angular momentum on particles and on the grid:

$$\mathbf{L}^{P}_{\text{tot}} = \sum_p \mathbf{x}_p^n \times m_p\mathbf{v}_p^n, \qquad \mathbf{L}^{G}_{\text{tot}} = \sum_i \mathbf{x}_i \times m_i^n\mathbf{v}_i^n. \tag{3}$$

The paper states that P2G conserves angular momentum and G2P does not. Here is why, in three lines. We need two properties of B-spline weights, which you met in chapter 22: partition of unity, $\sum_i w_{ip} = 1$, and linear reproduction, $\sum_i w_{ip}\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{x}_p$.

P2G. Substitute (1) into $\mathbf{L}^G$ and swap the sums:

$$\sum_i \mathbf{x}_i \times \sum_p w_{ip} m_p\mathbf{v}_p = \sum_p \Big(\sum_i w_{ip}\mathbf{x}_i\Big)\times m_p\mathbf{v}_p = \sum_p \mathbf{x}_p\times m_p\mathbf{v}_p.$$

G2P. Substitute (2) into $\mathbf{L}^P$:

$$\sum_p \mathbf{x}_p\times m_p\sum_i w_{ip}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i = \sum_i \Big(\sum_p w_{ip}m_p\mathbf{x}_p\Big)\times\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i.$$

For conservation we would need $\sum_p w_{ip}m_p\mathbf{x}_p = m_i\mathbf{x}_i$: the mass-weighted centre of the particles near node $i$ would have to sit exactly on the node. It does not, in general, so angular momentum leaks.

A one-particle example shows where it goes. Put a single particle of mass $m$ at $\mathbf{x}_p$ and suppose the grid update leaves its nodes spinning about it: $\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i = \mathbf{v}_p + \boldsymbol\omega\times(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)$. In 2D, with $\omega$ a scalar, the grid holds an extra angular momentum about the particle of

$$\sum_i w_{ip}\,m\,(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)\times\big(\omega\times(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)\big) = m\,\omega\sum_i w_{ip}\,|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p|^2.$$

PIC's G2P gives the particle $\sum_i w_{ip}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i = \mathbf{v}_p$, because the spin term averages to zero by linear reproduction. The spin is simply gone. For quadratic B-splines the sum $\sum_i w_{ip}|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p|^2$ is exactly $\Delta x^2/2$ in 2D (we prove this below), so the lost angular momentum is $m\omega\Delta x^2/2$: the angular momentum of a fuzzy blob about one grid cell across, which a point particle cannot hold.

Check your understanding

1. Does PIC conserve linear momentum in both transfers?

Yes. P2G: $\sum_i m_i\mathbf{v}_i = \sum_p m_p\mathbf{v}_p\sum_i w_{ip} = \sum_p m_p\mathbf{v}_p$ by partition of unity. G2P: $\sum_p m_p\mathbf{v}_p^{n+1} = \sum_i\big(\sum_p w_{ip}m_p\big)\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i = \sum_i m_i\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i$, using the definition of $m_i$. Only angular momentum is lost.

2. A disk of particles rotates rigidly. After many PIC steps without forces, what happens?

Each G2P removes the part of the local spin that the particle cannot represent, so the rotation decays exponentially, as if the disk were immersed in a viscous fluid. You can watch it happen in the check at the end of this chapter.

Section 5.2: Rigid Particle-In-Cell (RPIC)

RPIC is a stepping stone, not the destination: it fixes rotation but still damps shear. Read it for the idea, skim the details on a first pass. The idea: give each particle a sample of angular momentum $\mathbf{L}_p$, defined as exactly what PIC's G2P throws away, equation (4):

$$\mathbf{L}_p^{n+1} = \sum_i w_{ip}^n(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p^n)\times m_p\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i^{n+1}. \tag{4}$$

Total particle angular momentum becomes orbital plus spin, equation (5): $\mathbf{L}^P_{\text{tot}} = \sum_p(\mathbf{x}_p\times m_p\mathbf{v}_p + \mathbf{L}_p)$. The paper says G2P now conserves angular momentum "trivially". Verify it: with $\mathbf{v}_p^{n+1}$ from (2) and $\mathbf{L}_p^{n+1}$ from (4),

$$\sum_p\Big[\mathbf{x}_p\times m_p\sum_i w_{ip}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i + \sum_i w_{ip}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)\times m_p\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i\Big] = \sum_p\sum_i w_{ip}m_p\,\mathbf{x}_i\times\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i = \sum_i m_i\,\mathbf{x}_i\times\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i.$$

The $\mathbf{x}_p$ terms cancel, which is the whole trick. For the way back, treat each particle as a rigid body whose mass is spread over its nodes. Its angular velocity is $\boldsymbol\omega_p = \mathbf{K}_p^{-1}\mathbf{L}_p$, its velocity at node $i$ is $\mathbf{v}_p + \boldsymbol\omega_p\times(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)$, and the transfer is equation (6):

$$m_i\mathbf{v}_i = \sum_p w_{ip}m_p\big(\mathbf{v}_p + (\mathbf{K}_p^{-1}\mathbf{L}_p)\times(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)\big). \tag{6}$$

The inertia tensor of masses $w_{jp}m_p$ placed at the nodes is equation (7):

$$\mathbf{K}_p = \sum_j w_{jp}m_p\,(\mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_p)^*\,(\mathbf{x}_j - \mathbf{x}_p)^{*T}. \tag{7}$$

This is the textbook inertia tensor from chapter 10, because $[\mathbf{r}]_\times[\mathbf{r}]_\times^T = |\mathbf{r}|^2\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{r}\mathbf{r}^T$. Plug (6) into $\mathbf{L}^G$ and the spin part gives $\sum_i w_{ip}m_p\,\mathbf{r}_i\times(\boldsymbol\omega_p\times\mathbf{r}_i) = \mathbf{K}_p\boldsymbol\omega_p = \mathbf{L}_p$ with $\mathbf{r}_i = \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p$, so P2G conserves angular momentum as well.

Check your understanding

Why is RPIC not enough?

A rigid motion has six degrees of freedom in 3D (three translational, three rotational), but a general linear velocity field around a point has twelve: three for the velocity and nine for its gradient. The gradient's antisymmetric part is the rotation that RPIC keeps; its symmetric part (stretching and shearing) is still averaged away at every G2P. So RPIC preserves spinning but still damps, for example, a jelly cube's shear oscillation.

Section 5.3: Affine Particle-In-Cell (APIC)

This is the section to read slowly, twice. Each particle stores a matrix $\mathbf{C}_p$ and represents the local velocity field $\mathbf{v}_p + \mathbf{C}_p(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_p)$, an affine function. The paper explains why it cannot simply copy RPIC: nine numbers cannot be recovered from three. So it changes the goal. Instead of conserving angular momentum directly, it asks that affine velocity fields survive both transfers unchanged, and finds that angular momentum conservation then comes for free.

The transfers are equations (8) to (10):

$$m_i\mathbf{v}_i^n = \sum_p w_{ip}^n m_p\big(\mathbf{v}_p^n + \mathbf{B}_p^n(\mathbf{D}_p^n)^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p^n)\big), \tag{8}$$ $$\mathbf{D}_p^n = \sum_i w_{ip}^n(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p^n)(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p^n)^T, \tag{9}$$ $$\mathbf{B}_p^{n+1} = \sum_i w_{ip}^n\,\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i^{n+1}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p^n)^T, \tag{10}$$

together with PIC's $\mathbf{v}_p^{n+1} = \sum_i w_{ip}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i^{n+1}$, which the paper leaves implicit. Term by term:

  • In (8), each node receives the particle's affine velocity evaluated at the node, not the particle's own velocity. $\mathbf{B}_p\mathbf{D}_p^{-1} = \mathbf{C}_p$ is the velocity gradient the particle carries.
  • (9) is the second moment of the weights about the particle, a covariance matrix of the stencil. It plays the role of an inertia tensor (compare (7)), and it is the matrix that makes the transfers consistent.
  • (10) is a weighted outer product of the node velocities with the node offsets: a least-squares-like measure of how the velocity varies across the particle's stencil.

The paper says (9) is "derived by preserving affine motion" and leaves it there. Let us derive it.

Affine fields survive both transfers

Suppose the grid velocity is exactly affine: $\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i = \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}_i$ for some vector $\mathbf{a}$ and matrix $\mathbf{M}$. A good transfer should hand the particle exactly this field.

G2P. The particle velocity is

$$\mathbf{v}_p = \sum_i w_{ip}(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}_i) = \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}_p,$$

by partition of unity and linear reproduction. For $\mathbf{B}_p$, write $\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{x}_p + (\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)$:

$$\mathbf{B}_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\big(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)\big)(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)^T = (\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}_p)\underbrace{\sum_i w_{ip}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)^T}_{=\,\mathbf{0}} + \mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}_p.$$

So $\mathbf{B}_p = \mathbf{M}\mathbf{D}_p$, and the particle recovers the gradient exactly if it computes $\mathbf{C}_p = \mathbf{B}_p\mathbf{D}_p^{-1} = \mathbf{M}$. That is where (9) comes from: $\mathbf{D}_p$ is whatever matrix undoes the weighting in (10).

P2G. Now suppose every particle carries the same affine field, $\mathbf{v}_p = \mathbf{a} + \mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}_p$ and $\mathbf{C}_p = \mathbf{M}$. Then (8) gives

$$m_i\mathbf{v}_i = \sum_p w_{ip}m_p\big(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{M}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)\big) = (\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}_i)\sum_p w_{ip}m_p = m_i(\mathbf{a} + \mathbf{M}\mathbf{x}_i).$$

The grid gets the affine field exactly, at every node with mass, whatever the particle distribution. Compare PIC, where the same calculation gives $m_i\mathbf{v}_i = \mathbf{a}\,m_i + \mathbf{M}\sum_p w_{ip}m_p\mathbf{x}_p$, which is affine only if particles sit symmetrically around every node.

The point of the derivation: translations, rotations, uniform stretches and shears are all affine, so all of them now pass through a P2G-G2P round trip untouched. PIC preserves only translations. This is why APIC removes almost all of PIC's dissipation while keeping its filter.

Why $\mathbf{D}_p = \tfrac14\Delta x^2\mathbf{I}$ for quadratic B-splines

The paper says $\mathbf{D}_p$ takes a "surprisingly simple form" for quadratic and cubic B-splines. Here is the calculation the paper skips.

Step 1: it is diagonal. The weights are tensor products, $w_{ip} = N(\tfrac{x_p - x_i}{\Delta x})N(\tfrac{y_p - y_i}{\Delta x})$ in 2D. The off-diagonal entry of (9) factorises:

$$D_{xy} = \Big(\sum_{i_x} N_x\,(x_i - x_p)\Big)\Big(\sum_{i_y} N_y\,(y_i - y_p)\Big) = 0\cdot 0,$$

because each factor is a 1D first moment, which vanishes by linear reproduction. The diagonal entry is the 1D second moment times $\sum N_y = 1$, so everything reduces to one 1D sum.

Step 2: the 1D second moment. Measure positions in cells. The quadratic B-spline touches three nodes. Let $t\in[-\tfrac12,\tfrac12)$ be the particle's offset from the middle node. The three nodes are at signed distances $-1-t$, $-t$, $1-t$ from the particle, with weights

$$w_0 = \tfrac12(\tfrac12 - t)^2,\qquad w_1 = \tfrac34 - t^2,\qquad w_2 = \tfrac12(\tfrac12 + t)^2.$$

These are the weights in your chapter-22 code. The second moment is $w_0(1+t)^2 + w_1t^2 + w_2(1-t)^2$. Pair the outer terms: $(\tfrac12 - t)(1 + t) = \tfrac12 - \tfrac t2 - t^2$ and $(\tfrac12 + t)(1 - t) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac t2 - t^2$, so

$$\tfrac12\Big[(\tfrac12 - t^2 - \tfrac t2)^2 + (\tfrac12 - t^2 + \tfrac t2)^2\Big] = (\tfrac12 - t^2)^2 + \tfrac{t^2}{4} = \tfrac14 - \tfrac34t^2 + t^4.$$

Add the middle term $(\tfrac34 - t^2)t^2 = \tfrac34t^2 - t^4$ and everything that depends on $t$ cancels:

$$\sum_i w_{ip}\Big(\frac{x_i - x_p}{\Delta x}\Big)^2 = \frac14 \quad\text{for every } t.$$

Restoring units, $\mathbf{D}_p = \tfrac14\Delta x^2\mathbf{I}$, wherever the particle is. In code, $\mathbf{C}_p = \mathbf{B}_p\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}$ becomes C = B * (4 / (dx * dx)), and nobody ever forms or inverts $\mathbf{D}_p$. For $\Delta x = 0.01$ m, the factor is $4/10^{-4} = 40{,}000\ \text{m}^{-2}$.

Cubic. The cubic B-spline touches four nodes at distances $-1-s, -s, 1-s, 2-s$ with $s\in[0,1)$, and weights $\tfrac16(1-s)^3$, $\tfrac12s^3 - s^2 + \tfrac23$, $\tfrac12(1-s)^3 - (1-s)^2 + \tfrac23$, $\tfrac16s^3$. The same calculation (tedious by hand, two lines in a computer algebra system) gives a second moment of exactly $\tfrac13$, so $\mathbf{D}_p = \tfrac13\Delta x^2\mathbf{I}$.

Why these numbers. There is a pretty reason behind $\tfrac14$ and $\tfrac13$. A B-spline of degree $k$ is the unit box function convolved with itself $k$ times, that is, the probability density of a sum of $k+1$ independent uniform random numbers on $[-\tfrac12, \tfrac12]$. Each has variance $\tfrac1{12}$ and variances add, so the spline's continuous second moment is $\tfrac{k+1}{12}$: $\tfrac16$ for linear, $\tfrac14$ for quadratic, $\tfrac13$ for cubic. The discrete sum over nodes equals this continuous value exactly once the spline is smooth enough (degree 2 or more); that is a consequence of the Poisson summation formula, which you can take on trust.

Linear (trilinear) weights fail. With the hat function, a particle at fraction $s$ of a cell sees two nodes at distances $-s$ and $1-s$ with weights $1-s$ and $s$, giving a second moment of $s(1-s)$. It averages to $\tfrac16$, as the variance argument predicts, but it varies with position and is zero when the particle sits on a node ($s = 0$). Then $\mathbf{D}_p$ is singular, which is exactly the complication the paper mentions.

The trilinear identity

For linear weights the paper offers a way out: the identity $w_{ip}\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p) = \nabla w_{ip}$, which lets you evaluate (8) without forming $\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}$. Check it in 1D first. With $s = (x_p - x_0)/\Delta x$, $D_p = s(1-s)\Delta x^2$. For node 0, $w_0 = 1 - s$ and $x_0 - x_p = -s\Delta x$:

$$w_0\,D_p^{-1}(x_0 - x_p) = \frac{(1-s)(-s\Delta x)}{s(1-s)\Delta x^2} = -\frac{1}{\Delta x} = \frac{\partial w_0}{\partial x_p}.$$

For node 1, $w_1 = s$ and $x_1 - x_p = (1-s)\Delta x$, giving $+1/\Delta x$, again the derivative. The $s(1-s)$ cancels, which is why the identity survives even where $D_p$ itself vanishes. In 2D or 3D, $\mathbf{D}_p$ is diagonal (step 1 above still applies) and each component of $w_{ip}\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)$ is the 1D identity in that axis multiplied by the other axes' weights, which is exactly the corresponding component of the tensor-product gradient. Equation (8) then reads

$$m_i\mathbf{v}_i = \sum_p m_p\big(w_{ip}\mathbf{v}_p + \mathbf{B}_p\nabla w_{ip}\big)\qquad\text{(trilinear weights).}$$

Keep this identity in mind. Replacing $\nabla w_{ip}$ by $w_{ip}\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)$ for quadratic weights, where it is an approximation rather than an identity, is precisely the move that turns MPM into MLS-MPM (2018). Chapter 29 works it through.

Equation (11): what angular momentum means for an APIC particle

An APIC particle carries more than $\mathbf{x}_p\times m_p\mathbf{v}_p$: its affine field spins the nodes around it. The paper defines particle angular momentum as the grid angular momentum that the next P2G would produce, equation (11):

$$\mathbf{L}^P_{\text{tot}} = \sum_p m_p\big(\mathbf{x}_p\times\mathbf{v}_p + (\mathbf{B}_p)^T:\boldsymbol\epsilon\big). \tag{11}$$

Here $\boldsymbol\epsilon$ is the permutation tensor ($\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma} = +1$ for even permutations of $(1,2,3)$, $-1$ for odd, 0 otherwise), and $(\mathbf{A}:\boldsymbol\epsilon)_\alpha = \sum_{\beta\gamma}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}A_{\beta\gamma}$. That looks fearsome; in 2D it is one number. Let us derive (11) and see.

Insert (8) into $\mathbf{L}^G = \sum_i\mathbf{x}_i\times m_i\mathbf{v}_i$ and write $\mathbf{r}_i = \mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p$:

$$\mathbf{L}^G = \sum_p m_p\sum_i w_{ip}\,\mathbf{x}_i\times(\mathbf{v}_p + \mathbf{C}_p\mathbf{r}_i).$$

The $\mathbf{v}_p$ part gives $\mathbf{x}_p\times\mathbf{v}_p$ by linear reproduction. In the $\mathbf{C}_p$ part, write $\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{x}_p + \mathbf{r}_i$; the $\mathbf{x}_p$ piece vanishes because $\sum_i w_{ip}\mathbf{r}_i = \mathbf{0}$, leaving $\sum_i w_{ip}\,\mathbf{r}_i\times\mathbf{C}_p\mathbf{r}_i$. In components,

$$\Big(\sum_i w_{ip}\,\mathbf{r}_i\times\mathbf{C}_p\mathbf{r}_i\Big)_\alpha = \epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,C_{\gamma\delta}\sum_i w_{ip}r_\beta r_\delta = \epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,(\mathbf{C}_p\mathbf{D}_p)_{\gamma\beta} = \epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}\,(\mathbf{B}_p)_{\gamma\beta},$$

summing over repeated indices and using the symmetry of $\mathbf{D}_p$. Since $(\mathbf{B}_p)_{\gamma\beta} = (\mathbf{B}_p^T)_{\beta\gamma}$, this is $(\mathbf{B}_p^T:\boldsymbol\epsilon)_\alpha$, which is (11).

In 2D only the $z$ component exists, and with the scalar cross product $\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b} = a_xb_y - a_yb_x$:

$$L^P_{\text{tot}} = \sum_p m_p\big(\mathbf{x}_p\times\mathbf{v}_p + (B_p)_{yx} - (B_p)_{xy}\big).$$

Only the antisymmetric part of $\mathbf{B}_p$ carries angular momentum, which is the paper's remark that the "skew-symmetric component" holds all of it. For a rigid spin, $\mathbf{C}_p = \omega\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{B}_p = \mathbf{C}_p\cdot\tfrac14\Delta x^2\mathbf{I}$, so the spin term is $m_p\,\omega\,\Delta x^2/2$: exactly the angular momentum that PIC lost in the one-particle example above. APIC keeps it.

The proof the paper leaves to its supplement: G2P conserves angular momentum

Conservation in P2G holds by construction, because (11) was defined as the result of P2G. The real claim is that G2P loses nothing either. In 2D, with (2) and (10), the spin term of the new particle state is

$$(B_p^{n+1})_{yx} - (B_p^{n+1})_{xy} = \sum_i w_{ip}\big(\tilde v_{iy}r_{ix} - \tilde v_{ix}r_{iy}\big) = \sum_i w_{ip}\,\mathbf{r}_i\times\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i.$$

So the new total particle angular momentum is

$$L^P = \sum_p m_p\Big[\mathbf{x}_p\times\sum_i w_{ip}\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i + \sum_i w_{ip}(\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_p)\times\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i\Big] = \sum_p\sum_i w_{ip}m_p\,\mathbf{x}_i\times\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i = \sum_i m_i\,\mathbf{x}_i\times\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i,$$

the grid angular momentum after the update. It is the same cancellation as in RPIC. The 3D proof is identical with $\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}$ in place of the 2D cross product.

Two loose ends complete the argument.

  • Advection. Particles then move: $\mathbf{x}_p^{n+1} = \mathbf{x}_p^n + h\mathbf{v}_p^{n+1}$. This leaves $\mathbf{x}_p\times\mathbf{v}_p$ unchanged, because the added term is $h\,\mathbf{v}_p\times\mathbf{v}_p = \mathbf{0}$, and $\mathbf{B}_p$ is stored, not recomputed. (The grid can be treated the same way: moving the nodes with their own velocities does not change $\mathbf{L}^G$.)
  • The next P2G computes $\mathbf{D}_p$ at the new position. For quadratic and cubic weights it is the same constant matrix anyway; in general, conservation needs the $\mathbf{D}_p$ in $\mathbf{C}_p = \mathbf{B}_p\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}$ to be exactly the moment (9) at the position where P2G is done.

So the transfers and advection together conserve angular momentum exactly, to round-off. Whether the whole step conserves it depends on the grid update: MPM elastic forces built from a symmetric Cauchy stress do (the course proves the continuous version; see chapter 29).

The last paragraph of section 5.3 makes one more point worth underlining: because conservation is exact, rotation comes out right even with uneven particle sampling; Figure 9 shows a disk with four times as many particles in a thin band, rotating cleanly. Nothing relies on symmetric sampling or lucky cancellation.

Check your understanding

1. Which parts of the derivations used which property of the weights?

Partition of unity ($\sum_i w_{ip} = 1$) and linear reproduction ($\sum_i w_{ip}\mathbf{x}_i = \mathbf{x}_p$, equivalently $\sum_i w_{ip}\mathbf{r}_i = \mathbf{0}$) were used everywhere. Symmetry of $\mathbf{D}_p$ was used in equation (11). That $\mathbf{D}_p$ is a constant multiple of the identity was used nowhere in the proofs, only in making the code cheap.

2. A colleague stores $\mathbf{C}_p$ instead of $\mathbf{B}_p$. Is that the same method?

Yes, for quadratic or cubic weights: $\mathbf{C}_p = \mathbf{B}_p\cdot 4/\Delta x^2$ (or $3/\Delta x^2$), a constant scaling, so storing either is equivalent. Most codes, including the MPM course's successors and the PB-MPM code, store $\mathbf{C}_p$ (or its time-scaled version).

3. Compute the spin angular momentum of one APIC particle of mass 0.01 kg, spinning at 3 rad/s, on a grid with $\Delta x$ = 0.02 m, quadratic weights, 2D.

$m\,\omega\,\Delta x^2/2 = 0.01 \times 3 \times 0.0004/2 = 6\times10^{-6}$ kg m$^2$/s. Small for one particle, but summed over a disk of thousands of particles it is the fraction of angular momentum that PIC destroys every step.

4. Why must particles be advected with $\mathbf{x}_p + h\mathbf{v}_p$ rather than with interpolated moved grid positions?

For PIC and APIC the two are identical: $\sum_i w_{ip}(\mathbf{x}_i + h\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i) = \mathbf{x}_p + h\mathbf{v}_p^{n+1}$. The distinction only matters for FLIP, where the particle velocity is not the interpolated grid velocity. The MPM course makes the same remark in its step 8.

Section 6: Fluids

Read this section if you build incompressible liquid solvers on a staggered (MAC) grid; otherwise skim. On a MAC grid each velocity component lives on its own set of face centres, so each component $a$ is a separate scalar field with its own trilinear weights $w_{aip}$. Instead of a matrix, the particle stores one vector $\mathbf{c}_{pa}$ per axis, which is just row $a$ of $\mathbf{C}_p$ (the gradient of velocity component $a$). Equations (12) to (14):

$$m_{ai} = \sum_p m_p w_{aip}, \qquad m_{ai}v_{ai} = \sum_p m_p w_{aip}\big(\mathbf{e}_a^T\mathbf{v}_p + \mathbf{c}_{pa}^T(\mathbf{x}_{ai} - \mathbf{x}_p)\big), \tag{12, 13}$$ $$\mathbf{v}_p^{n+1} = \sum_{a,i} w_{aip}\,\tilde v_{ai}^{n+1}\mathbf{e}_a, \qquad \mathbf{c}_{pa}^{n+1} = \sum_i \nabla w_{aip}\,\tilde v_{ai}^{n+1}. \tag{14}$$

The extracted text of the paper loses the gradient symbol in (14); it must be there, because the trilinear identity turns $\mathbf{B}_p\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}$ into $\sum_i\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i\nabla w_{ip}^T$, whose row $a$ is the formula above. Storage is the same as the collocated case (three vectors or one matrix), and the pressure projection is untouched.

Check your understanding

Why can a MAC-grid APIC use trilinear weights when collocated APIC with trilinear weights has trouble?

Because the method stores $\mathbf{c}_{pa}$ (the $\mathbf{C}_p$ form) directly and computes it with weight gradients, it never needs $\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}$, so the singularity of $\mathbf{D}_p$ for particles on grid facets never arises.

Section 7: Lagrangian forces

This section is independent of the transfers, as the paper says. It shows how to apply forces from a mesh (springs, finite elements, anything with a potential energy $\Phi(\mathbf{x}_p)$) through the MPM grid, so that a meshed cloth can collide with MPM material for free. The MPM course repeats it almost word for word as its section 12.2. Skim on a first pass.

The derivation the paper compresses: imagine the grid nodes move during the step, $\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i = \mathbf{x}_i + h\mathbf{v}_i$, and the particles follow them by interpolation, $\mathbf{x}_p = \sum_i w_{ip}\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i$. Then the energy is a function of node positions through the particles, and the chain rule gives the force on a node,

$$\mathbf{f}_i = -\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i} = -\sum_p\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\mathbf{x}_p}\frac{\partial\mathbf{x}_p}{\partial\hat{\mathbf{x}}_i} = \sum_p w_{ip}\,\mathbf{f}_p,$$

and differentiating once more, the change in node force for a change $\mathbf{u}_j$ in node positions (needed by an implicit solver),

$$\delta\mathbf{f}_i = \sum_{p,q,j} w_{ip}\,\frac{\partial\mathbf{f}_p}{\partial\mathbf{x}_q}\,w_{jq}\,\mathbf{u}_j. \tag{15}$$

Read (15) right to left, which is also how to compute it: interpolate the node displacements to particles ($\sum_j w_{jq}\mathbf{u}_j$), multiply by the mesh's force derivative, and splat the result back to nodes. Three cheap passes instead of a triple sum.

Check your understanding

Why does the meshed particle's stored $\mathbf{F}_p$ go unused?

Because for a meshed object the deformation is measured by the mesh itself (from vertex positions), which is more accurate than an $\mathbf{F}_p$ evolved by the grid velocity gradient. The particle is still an MPM particle for the transfers, so it collides through the grid, but its forces come from the mesh.

Section 8: Results

Read the figure captions and the energy paragraph; the rest is a tour of examples (liquid pours, sand, lava, a coupled cloth example) comparing APIC with PIC, FLIP and blends. What to look for:

  • APIC versus blends. The paper reports that pure APIC beats APIC/PIC blends (a blend means scaling $\mathbf{B}_p$ by a factor below 1), unlike FLIP, where blends are needed. A useful knob to know exists.
  • Granular material. PIC makes sand look wet and clumpy, because its dissipation acts like cohesion; FLIP makes it noisy; APIC looks like dry powder. This is a qualitative claim about appearance, and the figures are the evidence.
  • The energy test. A circle of radius 0.3 at the centre of the unit square, on a $32\times32$ grid, four particles per cell, density 2, an initial horizontal velocity proportional to $x - 0.5$ (so the circle stretches and springs back), the snow paper's elastic model with $E = 0.5$ and $\nu = 0.4$. These numbers are enough to reproduce the test; it is an exercise below.
  • Figures 13 and 14. Figure 13 is honest: FLIP retains more total energy than APIC in several tests. The caption explains why, and the explanation is worth remembering: FLIP stores kinetic energy in the transfer's null modes, where nothing can dissipate it, so FLIP particles can keep moving relative to each other even when the bodies they belong to have stopped. Figure 14 splits the energy lost per frame into transfer losses and grid losses for PIC and APIC; the caption quotes total transfer losses of 82 % for APIC against 95 % for PIC. Work out for yourself from the plot what the percentages are percentages of; the robust conclusion is that APIC's transfers are far less dissipative than PIC's, and that no energy increase was ever observed across an APIC transfer round trip.
  • Table 2 and Figure 15. Run times are reported relative to PIC; extra cost per step is small, and total cost is dominated by how fast things move (the CFL condition), so energetic, unstable FLIP runs are the slowest.

Check your understanding

Why would an unstable method take longer to run, if each step costs the same?

Because the time step is limited by a CFL condition: particles should not move more than about a cell per step, so $h \le C\,\Delta x/v_{\max}$. Noise and instability create large spurious velocities, which force smaller steps.

Section 9: Discussion and limitations

Short; read it all. Three admissions:

  1. APIC neither improves nor worsens the ringing instability itself. It is stable because it filters like PIC, not because the null modes are gone.
  2. It filters information in G2P, so FLIP keeps more energy in some tests. If FLIP could be stabilised another way, that would be an advantage for FLIP.
  3. One extra matrix per particle and a few extra operations per transfer, negligible in practice.

The final paragraph argues that the liveliness artists like in PIC/FLIP blends is really instability, and that artists would rather add detail deliberately than inherit it uncontrollably from the method.

What the paper does not tell you

  • The full MPM loop with APIC. The paper shows only transfers. The MPM course (2016) gives the complete eight-step algorithm with APIC; see chapter 29.
  • The proofs. They are in the supplementary document and, more completely, in Jiang, Schroeder and Teran's 2017 journal paper. The derivations above are self-contained versions of the two central ones.
  • Using $\mathbf{C}_p$ for the deformation gradient. In MPM, the F update needs the velocity gradient at the particle, $\sum_i\tilde{\mathbf{v}}_i\nabla w_{ip}^T$. APIC already computes something very close, $\mathbf{C}_p$. MLS-MPM (2018) uses $\mathbf{C}_p$ for both, which saves work and makes the scheme consistent.
  • Higher-order versions. PolyPIC (2017) stores more polynomial modes per particle and recovers even more of the grid's information.

implement equations (8) to (10) and verify angular momentum

Take your chapter-23 code (or the check above). Set up the rotating-disk test of the paper's Figure 4: a disk with velocity $\mathbf{v} = \omega\times(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{c})$, no gravity, no forces. Print the relative change of $L^P$ from equation (11) per step. Predict it before you run: what should it be for APIC, and roughly what for PIC?

Hint

Measure angular momentum as $\sum_p m_p(\mathbf{x}_p\times\mathbf{v}_p + B_{yx} - B_{xy})$, or equivalently on the grid right after P2G. Forgetting the $\mathbf{B}_p$ term makes APIC look like it loses angular momentum; it has only moved from orbital to spin form.

Solution

APIC: relative change around $10^{-15}$ per step in double precision, round-off only. PIC: a steady decay. You can predict its rate. A rigidly spinning disk of mass $M$ and radius $R$ has $L = \tfrac12MR^2\omega$, and each PIC G2P throws away the spin part, about $\tfrac12M\Delta x^2\omega$ (the one-particle result summed over particles). The relative loss per transfer is therefore about $\Delta x^2/R^2$. In the check, $\Delta x = 1/24$ m and $R = 0.3$ m give 1.9 % per transfer; the measured value is 1.8 %, so with ten substeps per frame the PIC disk has lost almost all its spin within one second. The key lines are in checks.js: p2g and g2p.

The shear field $(\gamma y, 0)$ is affine but not rigid: half of it is rotation and half is pure strain. APIC keeps all of it; RPIC keeps the rotational half and damps the strain half; PIC damps both.

Summary

  • The paper's contribution is equations (8) to (10): particles carry an affine velocity $\mathbf{v}_p + \mathbf{C}_p(\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}_p)$, with $\mathbf{C}_p = \mathbf{B}_p\mathbf{D}_p^{-1}$.
  • $\mathbf{D}_p$ is the second-moment matrix of the weights; it is exactly the matrix that makes affine velocity fields survive both transfers. For quadratic B-splines it is $\tfrac14\Delta x^2\mathbf{I}$ everywhere, for cubic $\tfrac13\Delta x^2\mathbf{I}$; for linear weights it varies and can be singular, which the trilinear identity sidesteps.
  • Particle angular momentum includes a spin term from the antisymmetric part of $\mathbf{B}_p$ (equation (11)). With it, P2G, G2P and advection conserve angular momentum exactly.
  • APIC keeps PIC's filtering (so it is stable) while preserving all affine motion (so it is barely dissipative). It does not remove the ringing instability; it just does not amplify it.
  • Sections 6 and 7 are a MAC-grid variant and a mesh-coupling technique; section 8 is evidence; section 9 is an honest list of limits.
