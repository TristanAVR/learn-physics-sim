Small Steps

Ten small steps beat one big step with ten iterations, for the same price. Here is why.

At the end of the last chapter you had a cord that needed 110 iterations per step to reach its true stretch, and a steel cable that no sensible number of iterations would ever reach. Every constraint solver eventually runs into this wall. The obvious response is to spend more effort per step: more iterations.

In 2019 Miles Macklin and colleagues published a short paper, Small Steps in Physics Simulation, that recommends spending the same effort differently. Instead of one step of $h$ with $n$ iterations, take $n$ substeps of $h/n$ with a single iteration each. The cost is about the same. The result, for stiff systems and especially for heavy objects hanging on light ones, is dramatically better. It has since become the default way to run XPBD.

In this chapter you will see the difference side by side, derive how the error of each option depends on $n$ (one goes like $1/n$, the other like $1/n^2$), understand why a heavy weight on a light chain is the classic failure of iterative solvers, and learn which parts of a simulator belong inside the substep loop and which do not.

One budget, two ways to spend it

Suppose you can afford $n$ Gauss-Seidel sweeps over your constraints per frame. Here are the two loops. The first is the one from chapters 6 and 7:

// One step of h, n iterations.
function stepIterations(h, n) {
  predict(h);                                // x~ = x + h v + h^2 w f_ext
  lambda.fill(0);
  for (let it = 0; it < n; it++) solveSweep(alpha / (h * h));
  updateVelocities(h);                       // v = (x - x_old) / h
}

The second moves the loop outwards, around the whole step, and shrinks the step to match:

// n substeps of h_s = h / n, one iteration each.
function stepSubsteps(h, n) {
  const hs = h / n;
  for (let s = 0; s < n; s++) {
    predict(hs);
    lambda.fill(0);
    solveSweep(alpha / (hs * hs));           // the substep's own time-scaled compliance
    updateVelocities(hs);
  }
}

Both do $n$ sweeps over the constraints per frame. The substepped version also repeats the cheap per-particle work, the prediction and the velocity update, $n$ times; we will measure what that costs later. We write the substep as $h_s = h/n$, so with $h$ = 1/60 s and $n = 10$, each substep is 1.67 ms.

The demo runs both on identical chains: 20 links, 1.5 m long, 0.1 kg beads, nearly rigid ($\alpha = 10^{-6}$ m/N), and a weight at the bottom whose mass is set by the mass ratio slider, $M/m$. It starts at a ratio of 100, so the weight is 10 kg. The dashed green line is the exact static stretch from chapter 7, about 2 mm here. The plot shows how far each chain is from it, as a percentage of the chain length.

The left chain sags like a bungee cord: with the defaults it ends up 37 % longer than it should be. The right chain, doing exactly the same number of constraint projections, is 3.8 % too long. Raise $n$ and both improve, but not at the same rate. Lower the mass ratio and both improve, and at a ratio of 1 the left chain looks almost acceptable.

Error in, error out

Think about the chain at rest. Every step, the prediction moves each free particle down by $h^2 g$, gravity's contribution to $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$. Because the whole chain drops by the same amount, the only constraint that notices is the top link, attached to the fixed support: it stretches by $h^2 g$. That is error in. The solver then has to lift the entire chain, weight included, back up. That is error out.

Gauss-Seidel does not remove an error in one sweep. A linear iteration like this one shrinks each pattern (each eigenvector) of the error by a fixed factor per sweep, and what matters in the long run is the slowest pattern, which shrinks by a factor $\mu$ slightly below 1. Here it is the pattern in which the whole chain moves together with the weight. Write $1 - \mu$ for the fraction of that pattern removed by one sweep. For our 20-link chain it is about 0.006 with equal masses and 0.0005 at a mass ratio of 100 (chapter 9 shows how to compute it).

At steady state, error in equals error out. Call $E$ the standing stretch error of the chain.

Iterations. Per frame, $h^2 g$ comes in, and $n$ sweeps remove a fraction $1 - \mu^n$ of what is standing. While $n(1-\mu) \ll 1$, that fraction is close to $n(1 - \mu)$, so

$$E_{\text{iter}}\,n(1 - \mu) \approx h^2 g \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_{\text{iter}} \approx \frac{h^2 g}{n\,(1-\mu)} .$$

Substeps. Per substep, only $h_s^2 g = h^2 g/n^2$ comes in, and one sweep removes a fraction $1-\mu$:

$$E_{\text{sub}}\,(1 - \mu) \approx \frac{h^2 g}{n^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad E_{\text{sub}} \approx \frac{h^2 g}{n^2\,(1-\mu)} .$$

Per projection, both remove error at the same rate. The difference is how much error comes in. Over a frame, the substeps let in $n$ helpings of $h^2g/n^2$, which is $h^2 g/n$ in total: $n$ times less than one big step. The error going in is the gravity the prediction step adds without any constraint pushing back, and it is quadratic in the step size. Splitting the step splits that quadratic.

Check the formulas against the table. With $h$ = 1/60 s, $h^2 g = 2.7$ mm. At a mass ratio of 100, $1 - \mu = 4.94\times10^{-4}$, so with $n$ = 10:

$$E_{\text{iter}} \approx \frac{0.002725}{10 \times 0.000494} = 0.55\ \text{m} = 36.7\,\%,\qquad E_{\text{sub}} \approx \frac{0.002725}{100 \times 0.000494} = 0.055\ \text{m} = 3.67\,\%,$$

against 37.4 % and 3.76 % measured. At a ratio of 1 the same formulas give 2.95 % and 0.30 %, measured 3.36 % and 0.35 %: still the right size, a little low because the slowest pattern is less dominant there. Not bad for a back-of-the-envelope argument.

The rule of thumb: with $n$ iterations, the leftover constraint error falls like $1/n$; with $n$ substeps, like $1/n^2$. Iterations fight the error after it has been made. Substeps make less of it.

The argument also says when iterations can catch up: when $n(1-\mu)$ is not small, so that $\mu^n$ is close to zero and a single step's sweeps really do converge. Then the iterations version has essentially no error left, while the substepped one still carries its $1/n^2$. That happens when the constraints are soft compared with the time step, the $S \ll 1$ regime of chapter 7, or when the system is small. For the stiff chains, cables and stacks that cause trouble in practice, you are nowhere near it: a rigid 20-link chain with equal masses has $1/(1-\mu) \approx 160$ sweeps, and at a mass ratio of 1000 it is about 19,000.

Heavy weights on light chains

Why does the mass ratio make $1 - \mu$ so small? Watch what one sweep does to the bottom link of a stretched chain. The correction is shared in proportion to inverse mass, so the 0.1 kg bead moves and the 10 kg weight hardly moves at all. But moving the bead stretches the link above it, whose correction moves the next bead, and so on up to the support. A sweep shuffles error along the chain among the light beads. Only the tiny share $w_M/(w_m + w_M)$ of each bottom-link correction actually lifts the weight, and lifting the weight is the whole point.

In matrix terms, the system matrix $\nabla\mathbf{C}\,\mathbf{M}^{-1}\nabla\mathbf{C}^T$ from chapter 7 has entries of size $w_m = 10$ kg-1 for the bead links and one direction, the whole chain moving with the weight, along which it is nearly flat because $w_M = 0.1$ kg-1. A nearly flat direction is a nearly singular matrix, and iterative solvers crawl along nearly flat directions. Here is the measured slowest factor for our 20-link chain:

At large ratios $1-\mu$ falls almost in proportion to $1/(M/m)$ (a factor of 9.5 from 100 to 1000), and so the error of both methods grows almost in proportion to the mass ratio, as the table of errors showed. Substepping does not change $\mu$; it does not make the solver converge any faster. It reduces how much error each step creates, and that is enough to push the failure point out by a factor of $n$.

Mass ratios are everywhere: a heavy anchor on a light rope, a wrecking ball on a chain, a character holding a crate, a tablecloth weighted by plates. Anywhere a light thing holds up a heavy thing, iterations alone will make the light thing stretch.

Substeps and compliance

For XPBD constraints with compliance, substepping has a second effect. Each substep uses its own time-scaled compliance

$$\tilde\alpha_s = \frac{\alpha}{h_s^2} = n^2\,\frac{\alpha}{h^2}, \qquad\text{so}\qquad S_s = \frac{h_s^2\sum w|\nabla C|^2}{\alpha} = \frac{S}{n^2}.$$

The stiffness ratio of chapter 7 shrinks with the square of the number of substeps. A middle link of the demo chain, two 0.1 kg beads and $\alpha = 10^{-6}$ m/N, has $S = 20 \times 2.78\times10^{-4}/10^{-6} \approx 5{,}600$ at $h$ = 1/60 s. With 10 substeps it is 56, and with 40 substeps 3.5: soft enough, by chapter 7's measure, that a single iteration per substep gets close. The cord from chapter 7 with $\alpha = 10^{-4}$ m/N, which needed 110 iterations, has $S$ = 0.56 with 10 substeps, well inside the fast-converging regime.

Remember also what XPBD promised: at convergence, the static result does not depend on the time step. So substepping does not change the answer you are converging to. It only gets you there.

What goes inside the substep loop

In the demo, everything is inside the loop because there is nothing else. A real simulator has expensive work that does not need to run every substep. The rule is: things that depend on positions changing a lot can run once per frame; things that enforce constraints run every substep.

function frame(h, n) {
  // Once per frame: find everything that MIGHT touch during this frame.
  // Expand each object's bounding box by how far it can move, |v| h, plus a margin.
  const candidates = broadPhase(h);
  const hs = h / n;
  for (let s = 0; s < n; s++) {
    predict(hs);
    lambda.fill(0);
    solveConstraints(hs);                    // joints, links, bending, ...
    solveContacts(candidates, hs);           // re-evaluate each candidate's distance NOW
    updateVelocities(hs);
    solveVelocities(hs);                     // friction and restitution, if you have them
  }
}

Collision detection, the search for which pairs might touch, is the expensive part, and objects move very little within one frame, so it runs once with a safety margin. Collision response runs per substep: each candidate pair becomes a contact constraint whose current penetration is recomputed from the current positions, so a pair that was apart at the start of the frame can still make contact halfway through. Contacts are covered in chapter 15. Rendering, user input and anything driven by the game logic also stay per frame.

Energy and damping

Substepping has a side effect you will notice as soon as something swings. In chapter 3, implicit Euler took away energy from an oscillation of frequency $\omega$ by a factor $1/(1 + h^2\omega^2)$ per step. XPBD inherits that numerical damping. With $n$ substeps the factor per frame becomes

$$\left(\frac{1}{1 + h_s^2\omega^2}\right)^{n} \approx 1 - \frac{h^2\omega^2}{n} \qquad\text{instead of}\qquad \frac{1}{1 + h^2\omega^2}.$$

For a mode with $h\omega$ = 0.5, one step keeps 80 % of the energy per frame; ten substeps keep 97.5 %. Press "swing both weights" in the demo (at a mass ratio of 10): after ten seconds the substepped chain still has about 65 % of its swing energy and the iterated one about 37 %. Part of the iterated chain's loss is its unconverged constraints, which act like extra dissipation; part is the big step.

Usually this is welcome: things look livelier and less like they are moving through syrup. But a large step was quietly damping your jitter, and substepping removes that. Simulations that looked calm with one step can buzz with many. The fix is to add physical damping where you want it, such as the XPBD damping term from chapter 7, rather than relying on the integrator's.

Very small steps and single precision. With $h_s$ = 0.1 ms, the gravity displacement per substep is $h_s^2 g \approx 10^{-7}$ m. A single-precision float storing a position near 1 m has a resolution of about $10^{-7}$ m, so the update is barely representable. On a GPU in single precision, hundreds of substeps can start to lose motion to rounding. Storing positions relative to a nearby origin, or accumulating displacements rather than absolute positions, helps. JavaScript numbers are doubles, so you will not see this here.

check that the cost is the same

Press "time 1000 frames of each". It runs each method on a fresh chain for 1000 frames and reports the time per frame. Try $n$ = 10 and $n$ = 40. Are the costs equal? Which parts of the substep loop run $n$ times that the iteration loop runs once, and how much work are they compared with a sweep? What would change for cloth, where every particle has three to six constraints?

Solution

On my laptop the two take about 5.2 and 6.2 µs per frame at $n$ = 10, and 19 and 24 µs at $n$ = 40: the substeps cost about 20-25 % more. The extra is the prediction, the velocity update and the multiplier reset, which the substep loop runs $n$ times. On this chain there is one constraint per particle, so that per-particle work is a noticeable fraction of a sweep. In cloth or a soft body with several constraints per particle, and with constraints that cost more than a distance (bending, volume, contacts), the sweeps dominate and the two costs become nearly equal. Either way, the substepped chain is ten times more accurate for 20 % more time; to get the same accuracy from iterations you would need ten times as many.

Small Steps in Physics Simulation

Macklin, Storey, Lu, Terdiman, Chentanez, Jeschke and Müller (Symposium on Computer Animation, 2019) make the argument of this chapter with XPBD: for a fixed budget, many substeps with one iteration each beat few steps with many iterations, most clearly on examples with large mass ratios and stiff constraints. They also discuss the reduced numerical damping that comes with it, and run collision detection once per frame with the substeps inside. Their substep is our $h_s = h/n$. The follow-up paper on rigid bodies, which you will meet in the next chapter but one, is built on the same loop. Expect to meet substepping again whenever a position-based method needs stiffness without iterations, including in the Position-Based MPM chapter.

Summary

  • For the same number of constraint projections, $n$ substeps of $h/n$ with one iteration each are far more accurate than one step with $n$ iterations, for stiff systems.
  • The reason is error in versus error out. Both remove error at the same rate per sweep, but the error created per frame by the prediction step is $h^2 g$ for one step and $h^2g/n$ for $n$ substeps. The standing error is $\approx h^2g/(n(1-\mu))$ for iterations and $\approx h^2g/(n^2(1-\mu))$ for substeps.
  • Heavy objects on light constraints make the solver's slowest mode nearly flat: $1-\mu$ shrinks like $1/(M/m)$, and the error grows with the mass ratio. Substepping does not fix the convergence, it pushes the failure point out by a factor of $n$.
  • With compliance, each substep has $\tilde\alpha_s = n^2\tilde\alpha$ and $S_s = S/n^2$, so stiff constraints start to look soft to the solver.
  • Collision detection runs once per frame with a margin; contact constraints are re-evaluated every substep.
  • Substeps reduce numerical damping by about a factor of $n$. Add physical damping explicitly where you want it.
