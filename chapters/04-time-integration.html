Time Integration

Why simulations explode, and the three lines of code that decide whether yours will.

In Particles and Newton you moved a particle by adding its velocity to its position every frame, and in Springs and Hooke's Law you met the spring and its exact bounce. In this chapter we put the two together: we hang that particle on a spring and step it with the same simple code, and it flies off the screen within a few seconds. Nothing is wrong with the spring. The problem is the way we stepped through time, and fixing it is the first real decision every simulation makes.

By the end of this chapter you will know three ways to take a step, called explicit, symplectic and implicit Euler. You will be able to predict which one survives a given spring and time step, and you will understand the sentence that opens almost every modern simulation paper: "explicit integration has well known stability issues."

Before you read on, make a guess. A ball on a spring swings back and forth. You simulate it with the simplest possible code: each step, add velocity times the step to the position, and acceleration times the step to the velocity. Over many steps, will the swing get bigger, get smaller, or stay the same? Write your answer down; we will come back to it.

The chapter is built in layers. First the notation, on its own, with a picture. Then the three methods side by side, worked with real numbers you can check on a calculator. Then the picture that explains everything, and only then the algebra that proves it. A final, optional layer gives the matrix view used in papers.

The notation, before any formulas

A simulation chops time into equal slices of length $h$, the time step (in seconds; 1/60 s is common). We count the slices with an integer $n$: step 0, step 1, step 2, and so on. So "after $n$ steps" means "at time $t_n = n\,h$".

The state of our particle is two numbers: its position $x$ and its velocity $v$. We write a subscript to say when: $x_n$ is the position at time $t_n$, and $x_{n+1}$ is the position one step later. The particle's acceleration $a$ depends on where it is, so it gets a subscript too: $a_n$ is the acceleration the particle has at time $t_n$.

Throughout the chapter the colours mean the same thing: blue is known at the start of the step, and orange is new, computed by the step. In formulas that looks like $\now{x_n}$ and $\nxt{x_{n+1}}$.

One more piece of physics and we are ready. Velocity is how fast position changes, and acceleration is how fast velocity changes. So over a short step, roughly:

$$\begin{aligned} \text{new position} &\approx \text{old position} + h \times \text{some velocity}, \\ \text{new velocity} &\approx \text{old velocity} + h \times \text{some acceleration}. \end{aligned}$$

The whole chapter is about one word in that sentence: some. The velocity and acceleration change during the step, so which value do we multiply by $h$: the one at the start, or the one at the end? Each choice is a different integrator.

the notation

The time step is $h = 0.5$ s. What time is $t_3$? Is $\nxt{v_{4}}$ known before or after we compute step 3 → 4?

Answer

$t_3 = 3h = 1.5$ s. $v_4$ is what the step from $t_3$ to $t_4$ computes, so it is known only after that step.

The test problem: a mass on a spring

This is the spring from the previous chapter; here is everything we need from it. A spring of stiffness $k$ (N/m) pulls a mass $m$ (kg) back toward its rest position with Hooke's law, $F = -k\,x$. Newton's second law turns that into an acceleration:

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = -\frac{k}{m}\,x = -\omega^2 x, \qquad \text{where } \omega = \sqrt{k/m}.$$

$\omega$ (omega) is the spring's angular frequency in radians per second: a stiff spring or a light mass means a large $\omega$, a fast bounce. The exact answer is the cosine of the previous chapter, $x(t) = x_0\cos(\omega t)$, and the total energy never changes:

$$E = \underbrace{\tfrac12 m v^2}_{\text{kinetic}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12 k x^2}_{\text{stored in the spring}}.$$

That is what makes the spring such a good test: we can measure exactly how wrong each integrator is. And almost everything you will simulate on this site, from a rope to a block of jelly, behaves locally like a collection of springs. If an integrator mistreats one spring, it will mistreat all of them.

One step, three ways

Here are the three integrators side by side. Read them across, not down: they differ only in which velocity and which acceleration they use.

Look at the colours on the right-hand sides. Explicit Euler uses only blue: everything it needs is already known, so each line is a plain assignment. Symplectic Euler uses one orange value, but it is the velocity it computed on the line before, so it is still just two assignments in a particular order. Implicit Euler uses the orange acceleration $\nxt{a_{n+1}}$, which depends on the orange position, which depends on the orange velocity we are trying to compute. That circle is why implicit Euler needs an equation to be solved, as you will see in a moment.

In code, explicit and symplectic Euler are the same two lines in a different order:

// explicit Euler: both lines use the OLD values
const a = -omega2 * x;
x = x + h * v;     // old v
v = v + h * a;

// symplectic Euler: velocity first, then position with the NEW velocity
const a = -omega2 * x;
v = v + h * a;
x = x + h * v;     // new v

Working one step by hand

Formulas make much more sense with numbers in them. Take the easiest spring there is: $k = 1$ N/m and $m = 1$ kg, so $\omega^2 = 1$ and the acceleration is simply $a = -x$. Start it stretched by $x_0 = 1$ m, at rest ($v_0 = 0$), and take one big step, $h = 0.5$ s. At the start, $\now{a_0} = -\now{x_0} = -1$ m/s². The starting energy is $E_0 = \tfrac12(0)^2 + \tfrac12(1)^2 = 0.5$ J.

Notice why explicit Euler went wrong: it moved the particle with the starting velocity, which was zero, so the particle did not move at all during the step, while its velocity grew anyway.

Solving the implicit step, line by line

Implicit Euler's velocity line contains $\nxt{a_{n+1}}$, which we do not know. But for a spring we know what it is in terms of the new position, and the new position in terms of the new velocity. Substitute and solve; every line below does one thing:

$$\begin{aligned} \nxt{v_{n+1}} &= \now{v_n} + h\,\nxt{a_{n+1}} && \text{implicit Euler's velocity rule} \\ &= \now{v_n} - h\,\omega^2\,\nxt{x_{n+1}} && \text{the spring: } a = -\omega^2 x \\ &= \now{v_n} - h\,\omega^2\,(\now{x_n} + h\,\nxt{v_{n+1}}) && \text{implicit Euler's position rule} \\ &= \now{v_n} - h\,\omega^2\,\now{x_n} - h^2\omega^2\,\nxt{v_{n+1}} && \text{multiply out the bracket} \\ \nxt{v_{n+1}}\,(1 + h^2\omega^2) &= \now{v_n} - h\,\omega^2\,\now{x_n} && \text{gather the orange terms on the left} \\ \nxt{v_{n+1}} &= \frac{\now{v_n} - h\,\omega^2\,\now{x_n}}{1 + h^2\omega^2} && \text{divide} \end{aligned}$$

Now everything on the right is blue, so it is computable. With our numbers: $v_1 = (0 - 0.5 \times 1 \times 1) / (1 + 0.25) = -0.5 / 1.25 = -0.4$ m/s, as in the table. Then the position rule gives $x_1 = 1 + 0.5 \times (-0.4) = 0.8$ m.

For one spring this took six lines of algebra. For a thousand springs connected to each other, the same idea gives a thousand equations that must be solved together, a large linear system. That cost is the price of implicit methods, and a good part of this site (the solvers chapter, and implicit MPM) is about paying it cleverly.

The picture that explains everything

Run the demo below. It holds three identical springs, each advanced by a different integrator, and plots each one's energy relative to the start. The red block (explicit Euler) swings wider and wider; lower the time step and it climbs more slowly, but it still climbs. The blue block (implicit Euler) slowly stops. The green one (symplectic Euler) keeps swinging.

A plot over time shows that they differ. A different picture shows why. Instead of plotting position against time, plot the state itself: position along the horizontal axis and velocity (divided by $\omega$, so the units match) along the vertical. This is called phase space. The exact motion of a spring is a circle in phase space: when the mass is furthest out it is momentarily still (right of the circle), and when it passes through the middle it is fastest (top or bottom). The radius of the circle measures the energy.

Now every integrator becomes a shape you can see:

  • Explicit Euler spirals outward. Each step moves the state along the direction it was heading at the start of the step: along the tangent to the circle. A tangent always leads outward from a circle, so every step lands a little further out: more energy.
  • Implicit Euler spirals inward. It uses the direction at the end of the step, which leans inward. Every step lands a little further in: less energy.
  • Symplectic Euler stays on a closed loop, a slightly tilted ellipse (drawn faintly). It neither gains nor loses energy over time; it just wobbles around the true circle. Push the time step up and the ellipse gets more tilted and stretched, until at $h\omega = 2$ it stops being a closed loop at all, and the simulation explodes.

the picture

In phase space, a damper (friction) would make the exact motion spiral inward too. So if you see a simulated spring slowly stop, how could you tell whether it is real damping or implicit Euler?

Answer

Change the time step. Real damping does not depend on $h$; implicit Euler's numerical damping does (it removes a factor $1/(1 + h^2\omega^2)$ per step, so smaller steps remove less). If the spiral tightens when you increase $h$, the integrator is doing the damping.

Why: the energy, line by line

The pictures and the numbers agree. Now let us prove what they show, using nothing but expanding brackets. We want to compare the energy after a step, $E_{n+1} = \tfrac12 m \nxt{v_{n+1}}^2 + \tfrac12 k \nxt{x_{n+1}}^2$, with the energy before it. Recall $k = m\omega^2$; we will use that to swap between them.

Explicit Euler gains energy, by an exact factor

Explicit Euler's two rules for the spring are $\nxt{x_{n+1}} = \now{x_n} + h\,\now{v_n}$ and $\nxt{v_{n+1}} = \now{v_n} - h\,\omega^2\,\now{x_n}$. Square both, using $(a + b)^2 = a^2 + 2ab + b^2$:

$$\begin{aligned} \nxt{x_{n+1}}^2 &= \now{x_n}^2 + 2h\,\now{x_n}\now{v_n} + h^2\now{v_n}^2 \\ \nxt{v_{n+1}}^2 &= \now{v_n}^2 - 2h\omega^2\,\now{x_n}\now{v_n} + h^2\omega^4\,\now{x_n}^2 \end{aligned}$$

Put them into the energy, writing $k = m\omega^2$ so every term has an $m$:

$$\begin{aligned} E_{n+1} &= \tfrac12 m\,\nxt{v_{n+1}}^2 + \tfrac12 m\omega^2\,\nxt{x_{n+1}}^2 \\ &= \tfrac12 m\left(\now{v_n}^2 - \underline{2h\omega^2\,\now{x_n}\now{v_n}} + h^2\omega^4\,\now{x_n}^2\right) \\ &\quad + \tfrac12 m\omega^2\left(\now{x_n}^2 + \underline{2h\,\now{x_n}\now{v_n}} + h^2\now{v_n}^2\right). \end{aligned}$$

The two underlined terms are equal and opposite ($-m h\omega^2 xv$ and $+m h\omega^2 xv$), so they cancel. Group what is left into the parts that look like the old kinetic and spring energies:

$$E_{n+1} = \tfrac12 m\,\now{v_n}^2\,(1 + h^2\omega^2) + \tfrac12 m\omega^2\,\now{x_n}^2\,(1 + h^2\omega^2) = \boxed{(1 + h^2\omega^2)\,E_n}.$$

That is the 1.25 from the table: $h = 0.5$, $\omega = 1$, so $1 + h^2\omega^2 = 1.25$. The factor is larger than 1 for every time step, so for an undamped spring explicit Euler is unstable at any $h$: smaller steps only slow the growth. With the first demo's defaults ($\omega = 10$ rad/s, $h = 16$ ms, so $h\omega = 0.16$) the energy grows 2.6 % per step, which compounds to about a factor of 4.6 every second at 60 steps per second.

Implicit Euler is explicit Euler run backwards

No new algebra is needed. Implicit Euler's rules are $\nxt{x_{n+1}} = \now{x_n} + h\,\nxt{v_{n+1}}$ and $\nxt{v_{n+1}} = \now{v_n} - h\omega^2\,\nxt{x_{n+1}}$. Rearrange each to give the old values from the new ones:

$$\now{x_n} = \nxt{x_{n+1}} - h\,\nxt{v_{n+1}}, \qquad \now{v_n} = \nxt{v_{n+1}} + h\,\omega^2\,\nxt{x_{n+1}}.$$

That is exactly explicit Euler, taking a step of $-h$ (backwards in time) from the new state. So the result we just proved applies with the roles swapped: $E_n = (1 + h^2\omega^2)\,E_{n+1}$, that is

$$E_{n+1} = \frac{E_n}{1 + h^2\omega^2}.$$

The 0.80 in the table is $1/1.25$. Implicit Euler loses energy by exactly the factor explicit Euler gains it.

Symplectic Euler keeps a slightly tilted energy

Symplectic Euler's energy wobbled in the table, so $E$ itself is not conserved. But a nearby quantity is, exactly. Define the tilted energy

$$\tilde E = \tfrac12 m v^2 + \tfrac12 k x^2 \;-\; \tfrac12 h\,k\,x\,v .$$

It is the ordinary energy plus a small extra term that vanishes as $h \to 0$. If you substitute symplectic Euler's two rules and expand, as we did for explicit Euler, every term cancels and you get $\tilde E_{n+1} = \tilde E_n$: the step preserves it exactly. (Try it in the last exercise.) Check it with the table: at step 0, $\tilde E = 0.5$; at step 1, $\tfrac12(0.25) + \tfrac12(0.5625) - \tfrac12(0.5)(0.75)(-0.5) = 0.125 + 0.281 + 0.094 = 0.5$. Exactly the same.

Because $\tilde E$ never changes, the state is trapped on the curve $\tilde E = \text{constant}$: the tilted ellipse in the phase demo. That is why symplectic Euler can run for hours without drifting.

There is one catch, and it gives us the stability limit. The curve is a closed ellipse only while the tilted energy is positive for every state, and the extra term $-\tfrac12 h k x v$ can win if $h$ is large. Written with $k = m\omega^2$ and the common factor $\tfrac12 m$ dropped, $\tilde E$ is proportional to $\omega^2 x^2 - h\omega^2 x v + v^2$. A quadratic $A x^2 + B x v + C v^2$ is positive for every nonzero $(x, v)$ exactly when $B^2 < 4AC$ (the same discriminant test you know from the quadratic formula). Here that means

$$h^2\omega^4 < 4\omega^2 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad h\,\omega < 2.$$

So symplectic Euler is stable while $h\omega < 2$. Past that, the "ellipse" opens into a curve that runs off to infinity, and so does your simulation. Set the phase demo's time step above $2/\pi \approx 0.64$ s and watch.

The quantity to remember is $h\omega$: the time step measured in units of the fastest oscillation. A stiff spring (large $k$) or a light mass (small $m$) means a large $\omega$, which needs a small $h$. When someone says a system is stiff, in the numerical sense, they mean exactly this: it contains motions much faster than the time step you would like to take.

Going deeper: the matrix view used in papers

Papers usually prove these results differently, and you will meet this form again in the solvers chapter. Write the state as a vector. For the spring, every integrator here is a matrix that multiplies the state:

$$\begin{pmatrix} x_{n+1} \\ v_{n+1} \end{pmatrix} = A \begin{pmatrix} x_n \\ v_n \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A_{\text{explicit}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & h \\ -h\omega^2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad A_{\text{symplectic}} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 - h^2\omega^2 & h \\ -h\omega^2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.$$

After $n$ steps the state is $A^n$ times the start, so what matters is whether repeated multiplication by $A$ makes vectors grow. That is decided by the eigenvalues of $A$, the factors by which $A$ stretches its special directions: if any has magnitude above 1, the state grows geometrically. For explicit Euler they are $\lambda = 1 \pm i\,h\omega$, with $|\lambda| = \sqrt{1 + h^2\omega^2} > 1$: the square root of the energy factor we derived, because energy goes with the square of the amplitude. For implicit Euler, $|\lambda| = 1/\sqrt{1 + h^2\omega^2} < 1$.

For symplectic Euler, $\det A = 1$: the step preserves areas in phase space, which is what symplectic means, and why it has a conserved quantity at all. Its eigenvalues stay on the unit circle (magnitude exactly 1) while $|\operatorname{tr} A| \le 2$, that is $|2 - h^2\omega^2| \le 2$, which is again $h\omega \le 2$. Two different proofs, the same limit.

Back to the guess you made at the start. The "simplest possible code" was explicit Euler: the swing grows, every time, at any time step.

Solution

Compare with solution.js:

function symplecticEuler(b, h) {
  const a = springForce(b.x) / mass.value;
  b.v = b.v + h * a;
  b.x = b.x + h * b.v;
}

function implicitEuler(b, h) {
  const w2 = stiffness.value / mass.value;
  b.v = (b.v - h * w2 * b.x) / (1 + h * h * w2);
  b.x = b.x + h * b.v;
}

Choosing a time step in a real program

Use a fixed time step. Frame rates vary, but your integrator's stability depends on $h$. If you pass the measured frame time straight into the step, a single slow frame can push $h\omega$ past the limit and blow the simulation up. The standard fix is an accumulator: add the real elapsed time to a bank, and run fixed steps of size $h$ while the bank holds at least $h$. The sandbox does the simplest version of this, one or more fixed steps per animation frame.

Know your fastest mode. The stability limit is set by the largest $\omega$ anywhere in the system: the stiffest spring attached to the lightest particle. A rope of a hundred soft springs with one tiny bead on a stiff link has the time step limit of that one link. When a simulation explodes, the first question is always: what is the fastest thing in it, and how does it compare with my time step?

where this shows up in the papers

The PB-MPM paper's abstract starts from exactly this chapter: explicit MPM is easy to implement but has stability problems that force small time steps, and implicit MPM (solving a system each step with Newton's method) allows large steps but is complex. Summary

  • An integrator answers one question: from what is known at the start of a step, what are the position and velocity at the end? The three Euler methods differ only in which velocity and which acceleration they use.
  • Explicit Euler uses start-of-step values. For a spring it multiplies the energy by $1 + h^2\omega^2$ every step: unstable at any time step.
  • Symplectic Euler updates velocity first and moves with the new velocity. Same cost, no drift: it conserves a tilted energy exactly, and is stable while $h\omega < 2$.
  • Implicit Euler uses end-of-step values, which turns each step into an equation to solve. It is explicit Euler run backwards: stable for any step, but it divides the energy by $1 + h^2\omega^2$ (numerical damping).
  • In phase space: explicit spirals out, implicit spirals in, symplectic stays on a closed loop.
  • $h\omega$, the time step measured against the fastest motion, predicts stability.
