Stress and Hyperelasticity

From "how deformed is it?" to "how hard does it push back?", with one energy function per material.

The last chapter gave every point of a material its deformation gradient $\mathbf{F}$. That tells us the shape of the material, but not what it does about it. A rubber block that has been squashed wants to spring back; a block of dough mostly does not. The rule that turns deformation into force is called the constitutive model, and it is where a simulator decides what material it is simulating.

For elastic materials there is a clean way to write that rule. Choose a function that says how much energy is stored per unit of rest volume for a given $\mathbf{F}$. Its derivative is the stress, and the derivative of the total energy with respect to the node positions is the force. Everything else follows. This chapter builds four such functions, shows you where each one breaks, and then uses them in a small finite element jelly block. The force formula you derive for a triangle has exactly the same shape as the one MPM uses for a particle, so by the end you will already understand half of an MPM step.

Energy first

A spring stores energy $\tfrac12 k (\ell - \ell_0)^2$ and its force is minus the derivative of that energy. A continuum does the same thing point by point. The strain energy density $\Psi(\mathbf{F})$ is the elastic energy stored per unit rest volume (J/m³, which is the same unit as pascals) when the neighbourhood has deformation gradient $\mathbf{F}$. Materials whose elastic behaviour comes entirely from such a function are called hyperelastic. The total elastic energy of a body is the integral over its rest shape,

$$\mathcal{E} = \int_{\Omega^0} \Psi(\mathbf{F}(X))\, dX \;\approx\; \sum_e V^0_e\, \Psi(\mathbf{F}_e),$$

where the sum runs over elements (triangles here, particles in MPM) with rest volumes $V^0_e$. In 2D we simulate a slab one metre thick, so a rest area in m² is also a volume in m³ and all the usual units carry over.

What makes a good $\Psi$? It should be zero at rest, $\Psi(\mathbf{I}) = 0$. It must not change when the deformed material is rotated afterwards, $\Psi(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}) = \Psi(\mathbf{F})$ for every rotation $\mathbf{R}$: turning a squashed block around does not squash it more. This is called frame indifference or objectivity, and the previous chapter's warning about small strain was really about it. Most of the materials we want are also isotropic, responding the same way whichever direction you pull, which means $\Psi(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{Q}) = \Psi(\mathbf{F})$ for rotations $\mathbf{Q}$ of the rest shape. Finally, a robust model should resist being crushed to zero area, and ideally cope when a simulation inverts an element anyway.

Stress is the derivative of energy

The first Piola-Kirchhoff stress is the derivative of the energy density with respect to $\mathbf{F}$:

$$\mathbf{P} = \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial \mathbf{F}}, \qquad P_{ij} = \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial F_{ij}}.$$

This is a 2×2 matrix of partial derivatives: entry $(i, j)$ says how fast the energy grows when you nudge $F_{ij}$. For a feel of what it means, take a bar in 1D with $\Psi = \tfrac12 k (F - 1)^2$; then $P = k(F - 1)$, which is Hooke's law with $F - 1$ the strain. In general $\mathbf{P}$ is a force per unit rest area: a small surface in the rest material with unit normal $N$ and area $dS$ feels the force $\mathbf{P} N\, dS$ from the material on its other side.

Engineers more often quote the Cauchy stress $\boldsymbol\sigma$, the true force per unit current area. The two are related by Nanson's formula from the last chapter, $n\,ds = J\mathbf{F}^{-\mathsf{T}}N\,dS$, which gives

$$\boldsymbol\sigma = \frac{1}{J}\,\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{F}^\mathsf{T}.$$

Both are in pascals. $\boldsymbol\sigma$ is always symmetric, $\mathbf{P}$ usually is not. Simulators that think in the rest shape (finite elements, and MPM's energy-based forces) use $\mathbf{P}$; pressure in a fluid is $\boldsymbol\sigma = -p\,\mathbf{I}$.

Forces are the negative gradient of energy

Given positions for all the nodes, we can compute every $\mathbf{F}_e$ and so the total energy $\mathcal{E}(x)$. The elastic force on node $a$ is

$$f_a = -\frac{\partial \mathcal{E}}{\partial x_a},$$

exactly as for springs. Let us work it out for one linear triangle, because it is short and because MPM does the same calculation. We need one piece of notation: the double contraction of two matrices, $\mathbf{A} : \mathbf{B} = \sum_{ij} A_{ij} B_{ij}$, the sum of the products of matching entries. With it, the chain rule for a function of a matrix reads $d\Psi = \mathbf{P} : d\mathbf{F}$, which is just "sum over all entries of (derivative times change)".

For a triangle, $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{D}_s\mathbf{D}_m^{-1}$, and only $\mathbf{D}_s$ (the current edge vectors) depends on the node positions. So

$$d\mathcal{E}_e = V^0\, \mathbf{P} : d\mathbf{F} = V^0\, \mathbf{P} : \left(d\mathbf{D}_s\, \mathbf{D}_m^{-1}\right) = V^0 \left(\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{D}_m^{-\mathsf{T}}\right) : d\mathbf{D}_s.$$

The last step uses the identity $\mathbf{A} : (\mathbf{B}\mathbf{C}) = (\mathbf{A}\mathbf{C}^\mathsf{T}) : \mathbf{B}$, which you can check by writing out the sums. Now $d\mathbf{D}_s$ has columns $dx_1 - dx_0$ and $dx_2 - dx_0$. Call $\mathbf{H} = -V^0\,\mathbf{P}\,\mathbf{D}_m^{-\mathsf{T}}$; then $d\mathcal{E}_e = -\mathbf{H} : d\mathbf{D}_s$, and reading off the coefficients of $dx_1$, $dx_2$ and $dx_0$:

$$f_1 = \mathbf{H}_{:,1}, \qquad f_2 = \mathbf{H}_{:,2}, \qquad f_0 = -f_1 - f_2.$$

The forces on vertices 1 and 2 are the two columns of $\mathbf{H}$, and vertex 0 balances them, so a triangle never pushes itself along. In code, with $\mathbf{D}_m^{-1}$ precomputed per triangle:

// H = -V0 P Dm^-T;  (P Dm^-T)_ij = sum_k P_ik (Dm^-1)_jk
const H00 = -V0 * (P[0] * m00 + P[1] * m01), H01 = -V0 * (P[0] * m10 + P[1] * m11);
const H10 = -V0 * (P[2] * m00 + P[3] * m01), H11 = -V0 * (P[2] * m10 + P[3] * m11);
fx[i1] += H00; fy[i1] += H10;           // first column  -> vertex 1
fx[i2] += H01; fy[i2] += H11;           // second column -> vertex 2
fx[i0] -= H00 + H01; fy[i0] -= H10 + H11;

A derivation like this is easy to get subtly wrong, so the demo's forces were checked against a finite-difference gradient of the total energy: for all four models below they agree to about one part in a billion.

This is MPM's force formula. The columns of $\mathbf{D}_m^{-\mathsf{T}}$ are the rest-space gradients $\nabla_X N_1$, $\nabla_X N_2$ of the triangle's linear shape functions, so each vertex force is $f_a = -V^0\,\mathbf{P}\,\nabla_X N_a$. MPM replaces the triangle's shape functions with the grid's interpolation weights $w_{ip}$ and the triangle with a particle. Its weights are written in world space, and the chain rule converts: $\nabla_X w = \mathbf{F}^\mathsf{T}\nabla_x w$. The result is the force on grid node $i$,

$$f_i = -\sum_p V^0_p\, \mathbf{P}_p\, \mathbf{F}_p^\mathsf{T}\, \nabla w_{ip},$$

which is eq. (189) of the MPM course notes. You will implement it in the Material Point Method chapter; it is the same three lines with a different sum.

The block is 0.8 m by 0.4 m, cut into 256 triangles, with density 1000 kg/m³. It is integrated with symplectic Euler and as many substeps per frame as explicit stability requires: the fastest elastic wave, with speed $c = \sqrt{(\lambda + 2\mu)/\rho}$, must not cross a triangle within one substep. The readout shows the count; at the default settings $c$ = 16.4 m/s and the frame needs 28 substeps. Now the models.

Lamé parameters

Every model below has two stiffness parameters, the Lamé parameters $\mu$ and $\lambda$. Both are in pascals. $\mu$ is the shear modulus: resistance to changing shape. $\lambda$ adds resistance to changing volume. Materials are usually described instead by Young's modulus $E$ (how stiff a bar is when pulled) and Poisson's ratio $\nu$ (how much it thins sideways when stretched), and the conversion is

$$\mu = \frac{E}{2(1 + \nu)}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{E\,\nu}{(1 + \nu)(1 - 2\nu)}.$$

At the demo's defaults, $E$ = 200 kPa and $\nu$ = 0.3, this gives $\mu$ = 76.9 kPa and $\lambda$ = 115 kPa. For scale: jelly is around 1 to 10 kPa, soft silicone rubber around 100 kPa to 1 MPa, tyre rubber several MPa. As $\nu \to 0.5$ the denominator $1 - 2\nu$ vanishes and $\lambda \to \infty$: the material becomes incompressible. Rubber has $\nu \approx 0.49$, which gives $\lambda \approx 50\mu$, and the explicit time step shrinks with $1/\sqrt{\lambda}$; this is one reason nearly incompressible materials are hard to simulate explicitly. (We use the 3D formulas. A 2D slab that cannot thin through its thickness is in plane strain, for which they are exactly right.)

Linear elasticity, and why it fails

The textbook model uses the small strain $\boldsymbol\varepsilon = \tfrac12(\mathbf{F} + \mathbf{F}^\mathsf{T}) - \mathbf{I}$:

$$\Psi = \mu\, \boldsymbol\varepsilon : \boldsymbol\varepsilon + \frac{\lambda}{2} (\operatorname{tr}\boldsymbol\varepsilon)^2, \qquad \mathbf{P} = 2\mu\,\boldsymbol\varepsilon + \lambda \operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol\varepsilon)\,\mathbf{I}.$$

It is linear in $\mathbf{F}$, cheap, and accurate for tiny deformations, which is why engineering codes use it for bridges. It is not frame indifferent. For a rigid rotation by $\theta$, $\boldsymbol\varepsilon = (\cos\theta - 1)\mathbf{I}$ and

$$\mathbf{P} = 2(\mu + \lambda)(\cos\theta - 1)\,\mathbf{I},$$

a compressive stress in a body that is not compressed at all. At 90° with the default parameters that is $-385$ kPa: the model believes the block has been crushed to nothing and pushes outward with everything it has. These are often called ghost forces. Their total torque is not even zero, so linear elasticity does not conserve angular momentum. Select "linear", untick gravity and press "spin": the block swells to several times its area within half a second, while the other three models stay within 2 % of their rest area. Linear elasticity is only usable when rotations are small, or inside a corotated formulation that removes the rotation first, which is the next idea.

St Venant-Kirchhoff

Replace the small strain with the Green strain $\mathbf{E} = \tfrac12(\mathbf{F}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{I})$, which ignores rotation:

$$\Psi = \mu\,\mathbf{E} : \mathbf{E} + \frac{\lambda}{2}(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{E})^2, \qquad \mathbf{P} = \mathbf{F}\left(2\mu\,\mathbf{E} + \lambda\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{E})\,\mathbf{I}\right).$$

This is the St Venant-Kirchhoff (StVK) model. It is frame indifferent and reduces to linear elasticity for small strains, so it spins correctly. Its weakness is compression. Squash it along one axis by a stretch $s$, holding the other at 1; then $\mathbf{E} = \operatorname{diag}((s^2 - 1)/2, 0)$ and the resisting stress is

$$P_{11} = (2\mu + \lambda)\, \frac{s\,(s^2 - 1)}{2}.$$

As $s$ goes from 1 down to 0 the push-back grows, peaks at $s = 1/\sqrt3 \approx 0.577$, and then falls back to zero at $s = 0$. Past 58 % compression, StVK gets softer the harder you squash it. Worse, the energy is a function of $s^2$, so the inverted state $s = -1$ has zero energy: it is a second rest shape. Press "slam" with StVK selected and the block hits the floor at 6 m/s, folds through itself and stays inside out. The other models recover.

Corotated and fixed corotated

The polar decomposition $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}$ suggests a different repair: measure the deformation as the distance from $\mathbf{F}$ to the nearest rotation. In terms of the signed singular values $\sigma_i$ from the last chapter, $|\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{R}|^2 = \sum_i (\sigma_i - 1)^2$, so a pure rotation costs nothing and every stretch or squash costs energy. The fixed corotated model, from section 6.3 of the MPM course notes, is

$$\Psi = \mu \sum_i (\sigma_i - 1)^2 + \frac{\lambda}{2}(J - 1)^2,$$ $$\mathbf{P} = 2\mu\,(\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{R}) + \lambda\,(J - 1)\,J\mathbf{F}^{-\mathsf{T}}.$$

Two derivatives make up $\mathbf{P}$. The first term needs $\partial/\partial\mathbf{F}\sum_i(\sigma_i - 1)^2 = 2(\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{R})$, which we justify in the SVD section below. The second needs the derivative of the determinant, $\partial J/\partial \mathbf{F} = J\mathbf{F}^{-\mathsf{T}}$. In 2D that matrix is the cofactor matrix,

$$J\mathbf{F}^{-\mathsf{T}} = \begin{pmatrix} d & -c \\ -b & a \end{pmatrix} \quad\text{for}\quad \mathbf{F} = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix},$$

which contains no division and so stays finite when the element is flat or inverted. That is what makes the model robust: the volume term always pushes $J$ back towards 1, even from $J \lt 0$, and the rotation $\mathbf{R}$ comes from the polar decomposition you wrote in chapter 20. The word "fixed" refers to the original corotated model, whose volume term used $\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{S} - \mathbf{I})$ and handled inversion badly; Stomakhin et al. (2012) replaced it with $(J - 1)^2$. Fixed corotated is the most common default elastic material in graphics MPM codes, and the elastic constraint of the Position-Based MPM chapter is built on the same polar rotation $\mathbf{R}$.

Neo-Hookean

The most common model in mechanics texts is neo-Hookean (MPM course eqs. 46 and 48):

$$\Psi = \frac{\mu}{2}\left(\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{F}^\mathsf{T}\mathbf{F}) - d\right) - \mu\log J + \frac{\lambda}{2}(\log J)^2,$$ $$\mathbf{P} = \mu\,(\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{F}^{-\mathsf{T}}) + \lambda\,\log(J)\,\mathbf{F}^{-\mathsf{T}},$$

with $d = 2$ in 2D. The $-\mu\log J$ term cancels the first term's pull at rest, so $\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{I}) = 0$. The logarithms make the energy rise to infinity as $J \to 0$: neo-Hookean will not let you crush it flat, which is excellent physics. Numerically it has two costs. It is undefined for $J \le 0$, so any inversion caused by a large step or a violent collision is a problem; the demo continues the volume term as a straight line below $J = 0.1$, a common practical guard. And it stiffens enormously under compression, roughly like $1/J^2$, which shrinks the explicit time step; the demo watches the smallest $J$ and takes more substeps when needed (watch the substep count during a "slam").

Isotropy and the SVD

Every isotropic, frame indifferent energy can be written as a function of the singular values alone, $\Psi(\mathbf{F}) = \hat\Psi(\sigma_1, \sigma_2)$. This turns matrix calculus into ordinary calculus. The stress is

$$\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{U}\,\hat{\mathbf{P}}\,\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T}, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{P}} = \operatorname{diag}\!\left(\frac{\partial\hat\Psi}{\partial\sigma_1}, \frac{\partial\hat\Psi}{\partial\sigma_2}\right).$$

A proof sketch, following the course notes. Frame indifference, $\Psi(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}) = \Psi(\mathbf{F})$, can be differentiated: the change of energy $\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{F}) : d\mathbf{F}$ must equal $\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}) : \mathbf{R}\,d\mathbf{F}$ for every $d\mathbf{F}$, which forces $\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{F}) = \mathbf{R}\,\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{F})$. Isotropy gives $\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{Q}) = \mathbf{P}(\mathbf{F})\mathbf{Q}$ the same way. Apply both with $\mathbf{U}$ and $\mathbf{V}$, and $\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T}) = \mathbf{U}\,\mathbf{P}(\boldsymbol\Sigma)\,\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T}$; at a diagonal $\mathbf{F} = \boldsymbol\Sigma$ the stress is diagonal with entries $\partial\hat\Psi/\partial\sigma_i$.

Check it on fixed corotated. $\partial\hat\Psi/\partial\sigma_1 = 2\mu(\sigma_1 - 1) + \lambda(J - 1)\sigma_2$, since $J = \sigma_1\sigma_2$. So $\hat{\mathbf{P}} = 2\mu(\boldsymbol\Sigma - \mathbf{I}) + \lambda(J-1)J\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}$, and multiplying out, $\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T} = \mathbf{F}$, $\mathbf{U}\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T} = \mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{U}\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T} = \mathbf{F}^{-\mathsf{T}}$. That is eq. (52), and it also proves $\partial/\partial\mathbf{F}\sum(\sigma_i - 1)^2 = 2(\mathbf{F} - \mathbf{R})$.

The same trick handles the second derivative $\partial\mathbf{P}/\partial\mathbf{F}$, which implicit integrators need (chapter 26). In the rotated frame it splits into small blocks: one built from the second derivatives $\partial^2\hat\Psi/\partial\sigma_i\partial\sigma_j$, and, for each pair $i, j$, a 2×2 block containing $1/(\sigma_i^2 - \sigma_j^2)$. That fraction blows up when two singular values are equal (for example at rest), so the notes rewrite it in partial fractions, where one piece has a finite limit and the other is clamped. You do not need it yet; just remember that it lives in section 6.4.

Open the starter (run it, read it). The block falls and collapses into a line of dots, because no triangle produces a force yet. First fill in fixedCorotatedP, returning both $\mathbf{P}$ and $\Psi$. The helpers polarRotation and cofactor are provided. Before running anything, check by hand that your $\mathbf{P}$ is zero for $\mathbf{F} = \mathbf{I}$ and for a 90° rotation.

Hint

All the matrices are four numbers in row order, and both terms of $\mathbf{P}$ are entry-by-entry combinations: P[k] = 2 * mu * (F[k] - R[k]) + lam * (J - 1) * cof[k].

Solution 
function fixedCorotatedP(F, mu, lam) {
  const R = polarRotation(F), J = det(F), cof = cofactor(F);
  const D = [F[0] - R[0], F[1] - R[1], F[2] - R[2], F[3] - R[3]];   // F - R
  const P = [];
  for (let k = 0; k < 4; k++) P.push(2 * mu * D[k] + lam * (J - 1) * cof[k]);
  const psi = mu * (D[0] * D[0] + D[1] * D[1] + D[2] * D[2] + D[3] * D[3]) + 0.5 * lam * (J - 1) * (J - 1);
  return { P, psi };
}

Summary

  • A hyperelastic material is defined by its energy density $\Psi(\mathbf{F})$; total energy is $\sum V^0\Psi$.
  • First Piola-Kirchhoff stress $\mathbf{P} = \partial\Psi/\partial\mathbf{F}$ is force per rest area; Cauchy stress $\boldsymbol\sigma = \frac1J\mathbf{P}\mathbf{F}^\mathsf{T}$ is force per current area.
  • Forces are $-\partial\mathcal{E}/\partial x$. For a linear triangle, $\mathbf{H} = -V^0\mathbf{P}\mathbf{D}_m^{-\mathsf{T}}$ holds the forces on two vertices, and the third balances them. MPM's force has the same form.
  • $\mu = E/(2(1+\nu))$, $\lambda = E\nu/((1+\nu)(1-2\nu))$; $\lambda \to \infty$ as $\nu \to 0.5$.
  • Linear elasticity creates ghost forces under rotation; StVK collapses under strong compression and is stable when inverted; fixed corotated is robust, including under inversion; neo-Hookean resists compression infinitely but is undefined for $J \le 0$.
  • For isotropic models, $\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{U}\,\operatorname{diag}(\partial\hat\Psi/\partial\sigma_i)\,\mathbf{V}^\mathsf{T}$.
